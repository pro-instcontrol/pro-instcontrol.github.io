What the industry actually read

Every month Control Engineering publishes its ten most-read articles, ranked by pageviews. One list is a snapshot of a week's attention. Twelve of them, laid end to end, show which subjects keep coming back — and which only looked important the week they were published.

01Method

How this list is built

The source is the monthly hot topics post on controleng.com. Its ranks are Google Analytics pageviews for that month, so the list measures attention and not merit: a vendor announcement with a strong headline can outrank a careful tutorial, and a two-year-old article that search keeps serving can reappear beside news from last week.

The text was harvested from archived copies of those posts, because the live site refuses scripted requests. Titles and summaries are the publisher's, translated; the sentence under each month heading is ours, and says what that month looks like once you have seen the others.

The archive does not have twelve months

Control Engineering does not always publish a list, and the Wayback Machine does not always capture the ones it does. Five of the twelve months in this window have no archived post at all — March to July 2026 — and the August 2026 post is titled June 2026 because it covers June and July together. October 2025 survives with four of ten positions, November 2025 with eight. A year should hold a hundred and twenty ranked slots; sixty-two exist. Nothing below is estimated to fill a hole.

03Reading the year

What twelve months of most-read lists actually shows

Half the list is nine articles counted twice

Sixty-two ranked positions resolve to fifty-three distinct articles. Nine pieces were read enough in one month to appear again the next: the PID gap-control column, the simulation-myths piece, the industrial-AI survey, the C++ motion-control article, the control-software-engineering introduction, the zero-trust argument, the System Integrator of the Year announcement and its Thermo Systems profile, and the Arizona State manufacturing building. A most-read list is a rolling window, not a fresh sample.

A tutorial series holds a sixth of the year

Four of the sixty-two positions belong to one column: PID spotlight, at part 22 in January and parts 30 and 31 in August and September. Nine instalments across eight months, and the readership followed every one. Nothing else in the year — no product launch, no acquisition, no award — repeats like that. The lesson is uncomfortable for anyone who thinks trade publishing runs on news: the most reliable draw is a working engineer explaining one mechanism at a time.

Award copy is a sixth of the list

Ten of the sixty-two positions are award announcements or honoree profiles: System Integrator of the Year five times, Engineering Leaders Under 40 four times, the Integrator Giants once. In December the top three are the same award — the announcement, then two category profiles — so that month's list is really a top eight. This is not a complaint about the publisher; it is what a readership does. People read about their peers, and about their competitors' projects, before they read about a control strategy.

Security is the only subject that never leaves, and it has changed register

Five positions across four months: zero trust on the plant floor in December and again in January, a vendor OT suite in December, a podcast on cyberattacks that turn physical in February, and a framework for physical-AI safety in October. The argument has moved from IT frameworks do not fit OT to the consequence is now mechanical. That is the same shift the safety page here records, where NIS2 and the Cyber Resilience Act turned security from a project decision into a legal one.

Migration is where the money is

November alone carries DCS migration at three, brownfield integration risk at eight and controls-system upgrade at ten — three entries describing one decision from three angles. Nobody reads about a new loop; everybody reads about the upgrade they are about to be asked to justify.

What is not on the list

No functional-safety standard. No calibration. No SIL verification, no proof-test interval, no orifice-plate sizing. Outside the PID series there is almost no loop mathematics at all. A most-read list is not a curriculum, and the absence is the useful part: the subjects that decide whether a plant runs safely are read by a small, specific audience, in ones and twos, not in tens of thousands. That gap is why the technical pages here are longer than a trade article and shorter than a standard.

Read these lists as a market signal, not a syllabus. What the industry is curious about this year is architecture, security, careers and the cost of standing still. What it is not curious about, at least not in public, is the arithmetic inside the loop.

04Second publisher

The other magazine, and a different measurement

Control Engineering ranks by pageviews. Automation.com Monthly has no such table: an issue is the set of features an editor commissioned, and its order is the order of the magazine. Reprinting those titles as a second top ten would invent a ranking that does not exist, so they are listed unranked, issue by issue — and the difference is the point. One list measures what readers chose; the other measures what an editor thought they should read.

Eight issues are used, from June/July 2025 to October 2026. The source marks some cards Sponsored By a named company; those are advertising and are excluded, as are editor's messages and topical roundups. What is left is the editorial core of each issue, with the source's own summary under each title.

Why there is no InTech here

InTech was the ISA's magazine and it ended with the October 2024 issue — the last two years of it do not exist to collect. The 2025 issues on automation.com are largely ISA-sponsored InTech material, and those cards are excluded on the same rule as vendor placements. Nothing here comes from isa.org: the society's terms do not permit its material to be processed by a model.
