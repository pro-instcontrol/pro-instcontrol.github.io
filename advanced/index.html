Where the fundamentals stop being enough

Every other page here teaches one loop, one instrument, one drawing. This is the layer above: strategies that need more than one loop, safety cases that need numbers, the connectivity standards being rewritten while you read this — and the architecture that claims to carry one number from a diaphragm in the field to a margin in a boardroom.

02From the trade press

Ethernet-APL reliability: the architecture decides, not the cable

John Rezabek's On the Bus column in the October 2026 issue of Control is the most useful page published on Ethernet-APL this year, and it never discusses the 10BASE-T1L specification. It is about the Monday morning after a segment misbehaves, and about what an engineer has implicitly promised the control room before the first field switch leaves the shop.

The line that decides the project

“Operators have little patience for mystery failures that disrupt their shift, especially when they cause or contribute to a shutdown.”

The fieldbus story that sets up the question

An operator, Constantine, intercepts the engineer, Emile, before the morning ops meeting with a diagnosis already formed: “I think we must have a card failure.” Emile notices that the devices with spurious or constant connection problems all share one fieldbus segment — and that one of them is a control valve holding pressure on a distillation tower running near total vacuum, where losing the loop means losing the product specification. He also knows the card carries an H1 interface for a second segment of about ten devices, and that swapping it will drop those too, even if everything returns in the state it was in. Constantine, who fears the worst, insists other avenues be pursued. He is right: the culprit is a DP transmitter that had worked for 26 years on top of that same tower. Disconnected, communication came back; replaced, it stayed clean.

That is the real case for APL, and it has nothing to do with speed. A shared bus means one degraded instrument can take the segment with it. A point-to-point spur means it cannot.

What APL fixes, and what it only moves

Every APL device gets its own single-pair spur back to the field switch, so corrosion, moisture ingress or a dying transmitter stays local to the device it belongs to. What stays shared is the field switch, its power supply and its uplink — which means APL moves active electronics, firmware and (most likely) local power supplies out into the field, and every one of those is a new place for a random failure to happen. The arithmetic is uncomfortable: the more switches you deploy, the more chances the plant has to draw a bad one. And the loops whose health and diagnostic data you most wanted are precisely the critical ones — which is why some early fieldbus adopters kept their critical loops hardwired over 4–20 mA, and why the same temptation returns here.

The failure mode that isn't technical

The harder problem is what the crew concludes. If an APL field switch drops several instruments at once, it will not be filed as an unlucky random failure of a sound architecture. It will be remembered as the day the new networked scheme put the unit on its back — and the fix will look obvious to everyone in the room: hardwire those devices. Whether fairly or not, the engineer who recommended the architecture owns that story for a long time. Fieldbus was not always as robust as it is now; a short circuit could once fail an entire segment. APL will get the same fortification, but on the switch vendor's schedule, not yours.

What to settle in the design review, before the first switch ships

  • Loops per switch. Choose the blast radius deliberately and write the number down: a switch reset should cost one unit operation at most. If the answer is “however many spurs the switch has”, the architecture chose for you.
  • Power and uplink diversity. Dual power feeds, and a redundant uplink or ring wherever the switch serves safety-relevant or high-value loops. This is a scope line item, not a commissioning detail.
  • Fallback for the critical loops. Decide now whether a critical device can sit on APL at all, or whether it keeps a 4–20 mA path. Diagnostic data you cannot act on during a trip is not worth the exposure.
  • Reset and firmware behaviour. A switch reset must not disturb spur power on healthy devices. Test it on the bench, not on the unit at three in the morning.
  • Alarms for the new failure mode. One alarm — switch X offline, N devices affected — instead of N simultaneous device-failure alarms. Rationalise it before startup, not after the first event.
  • The one-sentence test. Can you tell the shift team, in one sentence, what happens when a switch dies and what they should do? If not, the design is not finished.

None of this argues against APL. It argues that APL is an architecture purchase disguised as a cabling decision: the wire is standardised, the fault isolation is not, and the difference between the two is a drawing the instrument engineer signs.

03From the trade press

Digital transformation's data problem: drowning in data, short of knowledge

Ian Verhappen's Without Wires column in the same October 2026 issue of Control names the failure that the total-plant section below describes from the other end. Digital transformation, as he defines it, is the integration of the Purdue Enterprise Reference Architecture from Level 1 to Level 4 — and it arguably began with the internet. Intelligent fieldbuses and wireless sensor networks then raised the information available from OT networks by orders of magnitude. What did not rise by orders of magnitude is the realised benefit, and the gap is not a connectivity problem.

His diagnosis in one line

“Perhaps the problem isn't the data, but the vast amount of it, and the ability to identify and find what's important about it—securely.”

The numbers behind the hoarding

Verhappen cites a graph showing enterprise data at 60% of all data, with volume growing at a compounded 27% a year. Organizations are therefore collecting vast amounts into warehouses, lakes and lakehouses without necessarily using any of it — because everyone else is collecting too, and everyone knows data is valuable. That is not a strategy; it is insurance against being the only one without a data lake. Capturing “everything” through the IIoT compounds the problem rather than solving it, and the datasets have grown past what any single physical repository can hold.

The response has been to virtualise instead of consolidate, and the two architectures worth knowing by name are:

  • Data fabric. Metadata, machine learning and APIs weave the existing disparate sources — lakes, warehouses, databases — into a single virtual access layer. You query the fabric; it fetches from wherever the data actually lives, behind the scenes. The data does not move; the query does.
  • Data mesh. An organisational answer rather than a technical one: instead of one central IT team owning a giant lake, each business domain — logistics, sales, a production unit — treats its data as a product, hosts and manages its own repository, and exposes standard APIs for everyone else to consume.

Why AI does not rescue this

Many people are looking again at artificial intelligence, including large language models, to save the day with a friendly interface that finds the jewels amid the dross. Verhappen's objection is the one that matters: an assistant can only answer the question you pose, and if you do not know what treasures are hidden in your data, posing the right question is itself the hard part. A model that queries a lake you never curated will return a confident answer drawn from whatever happened to be in it — which is exactly the failure mode the total-plant section below calls a reporting system rather than a control system.

The OT-specific fly in the ointment

Legacy OT stacks put a data historian in the control network and a long-term historian in the DMZ, so what gets captured is limited to time-stamped PV and status. Verhappen suspects systems with that minimal capability are now in the minority, since control systems have supported commercial off-the-shelf concepts and digital field communications for roughly 25 years. Yet, from his experience in IEC SC65E WG10, he estimates that at least 90% of systems still do not capture the data that is already available to them. The reason is not bandwidth. It is not knowing what to capture, and not knowing how it would be used to achieve safe, reliable operations that also return the investment.

So the conclusion is uncomfortable and correct: digital transformation needs data in context, and despite drowning in data we suffer a dearth of knowledge. RAMI has been with us since 2015 and the floundering continues, largely because — without knowing which data can become information, or how to integrate it — there are always more urgent fires competing for the same engineers. The fix is not a bigger lake. It is deciding, tag by tag, which measurements are allowed to matter, what status flag travels with them, and which decision they are supposed to improve.

What this asks of an instrument engineer

  • Start from the decision, not the tag. A historian full of everything is a cost centre. Twenty measurements that a unit's margin actually depends on, with quality flags intact, is an asset.
  • Capture what the device already knows. Diagnostics, valve signatures, sensor health: the 90% figure is not about missing instruments, it is about unconfigured ones.
  • Keep the context. Units, range, calibration date and the good / bad / uncertain flag have to survive the trip into the lake, or the lake is fiction at scale.
  • Own the fabric or the mesh. Whether the plant picks a virtual access layer or domain-owned data products, somebody in instrumentation has to be accountable for what the field contributes to it.

06Total plant

The whole plant, from the diaphragm to the boardroom

Every other section here stops at one loop. The total-plant idea is that a single data chain runs from a diaphragm in the field to a margin number in a boardroom, and that each layer in that chain is only as good as the layer below it. Emerson sells the shape as Plantweb; ABB Ability, Siemens Xcelerator, Honeywell Forge and Yokogawa IA2S sell the same layers with different logos. The lineage is worth knowing: Emerson introduced PlantWeb in 1997 as “the first field-based digital plant architecture” and relaunched it in 2016 as a digital ecosystem. The marketing changed twice; the layer model did not.

The layers are not a vendor invention. They are the only way the physics works out: a measurement is made at the diaphragm, converted to a signal, validated somewhere, aggregated into a unit, and only then turned into money. What varies between products is where each boundary sits, and what survives crossing it.

  1. LayerWhat lives thereWhat it hands upward
  2. L1Field devicesSmart transmitters, digital valve controllers, analysers, corrosion and acoustic sensors. HART, FOUNDATION Fieldbus, PROFIBUS, WirelessHART, Ethernet-APL.The value — plus the device's own opinion of itself: status, diagnostics, valve signature, sensor fouling.
  3. L2Control & safetyDeltaV, Ovation, safety PLCs. PID, model predictive control embedded in the controller, cause & effect.A validated signal (good / bad / uncertain), a manipulated output, and evidence that the loop can hold setpoint.
  4. L3OperationsOperator stations, engineering tools, historian, device management, alarm rationalisation.A human decision, an audit trail, and a work order.
  5. L4Site analyticsAsset-monitoring apps, performance software, a data lake on site or in the cloud — both AWS and Azure are documented.Health scores, remaining useful life, exceptions ranked by money at risk.
  6. L5BusinessProduction accounting, planning, maintenance budget, energy.Throughput, give-away, unplanned-downtime cost — the numbers a manager can act on.
The arithmetic the top layer inherits

Emerson's own 2003 white paper on this architecture is unusually specific about where the money is. It defines quality as (product produced − lost and rework) ÷ product produced, reports that its variability audits found 80% of control loops underperforming, and cites a study projecting more than 7% of cost of goods sold from lifting nine areas of control practice to best practice — of which 3.3% comes from the first three, all of them loop-level: final control element performance and tuning, unit operation control, and advanced regulatory control. The vendor wrote the paper, so read the percentages as an existence proof rather than a benchmark. The structure of the argument is what matters: the saving is created at the bottom and merely counted at the top.

What the vendor claims, and what it is worth

The marketing literature adds the numbers the white paper leaves out: up to 30% saving in installed cost, up to 66% improvement in maintenance productivity, at least 2% improvement in overall plant efficiency, 20–30% on specific processes. “Up to” and “at least” in the same paragraph is the tell — the ranges are unpriced, unnormalised, and drawn from the best installations. Two things in that literature are still worth taking seriously. The first is the functional decomposition, which is the same five layers under other names: leverage field intelligence, connect the plant, control the process, optimise the assets. The second is the concession that “incremental gains can be realised by implementing any part of this architecture” but that the greatest benefits need the complete solution — which is both the honest engineering statement and the commercial model.

Three things break between the layers

  • The quality flag. The same white paper observes that a DCS-centred architecture assumes “any analog signal between 4 and 20 mA is good”. If the field layer sends a value without its status, every layer above inherits an unquantified error. DeltaV and Ovation do consume good / bad / uncertain — but that flag has to survive all the way into the report, or the report is fiction.
  • The calibration state. A data lake cannot un-drift a transmitter. A remaining-useful-life estimate inherits whatever the sensor was doing when the model was trained, including the zero shift nobody has looked at since the last shutdown.
  • The boundary itself. Every layer boundary is where units, timestamps and quality get dropped. OPC UA and MQTT are the least-bad answer so far; a scheduled CSV export is where architectures go to die quietly.

So the test of a total-plant proposal is not how many layers it has, or how good the dashboard looks. It is whether a number on the top-level screen can be traced, in one step, back to a tag, a range, and the date that instrument was last calibrated. If it cannot, you have bought a reporting system, not a control system — and the first time it is wrong, nobody will be able to say where.

07Software-defined

Four benefits, one precondition: control stops living in its own hardware

Sean Saul, who runs Emerson's DeltaV platform, wrote the mechanism down two years before the enterprise version of the argument appeared. Software-defined control means the control workload becomes containerised and runs on general hardware — most likely hyperconverged infrastructure (HCI), with the control system operating as a redundant service rather than a set of purpose-built controllers in purpose-built cabinets. The claim is not that the loops change. It is that everything around them stops being a hardware decision.

He names four benefits, and it is worth being precise about which of them are engineering and which are procurement.

  1. The benefitWhat it actually buys
  2. 01ResiliencyFailover stops being 1:1. Multiple controllers run simultaneously on HCI, so automation workloads can be added without a shutdown: deploy the containerised control function, build the I/O subsystem in the expansion area, auto-sense the field devices. If one control function fails, HCI's rapid failover takes over — and the architecture allows fault tolerance beyond a primary/backup pair, including failing over to virtualised hosts in another physical location.
  3. 02ScalabilityCapacity stops being a cabinet. Adding compute is connecting another blade in a server cabinet. The value is not the blade; it is that the growth path no longer starts with a drawing, a cabinet purchase and a shutdown window.
  4. 03FlexibilityThe most concrete claim in the article. Today the project team must calculate every device signal tag (DST) up front, because the count determines the hardware. Under software-defined control the count becomes a configuration value: estimate, then scale up or down in software. Capacity planning stops being a decision made once, at the worst possible moment, by people with the least information.
  5. 04ExtensibilityUpdate one instance while its primary keeps running. That is the same decoupling the enterprise article later calls evergreen operation — here stated as a per-function property rather than a fleet strategy.
Why now, and what it rests on

The building blocks have existed for years; what changed is the field. Ethernet-APL is bringing video, acoustic monitoring and other high-bandwidth sources into the control system, and that volume will push purpose-built control hardware to or past its limits. So the two arguments are the same bet at different layers: APL makes the field discoverable and data-rich, and software-defined control assumes a field you can auto-sense. Neither works alone. Note also what the article concedes — today's most flexible controllers are still purpose-built hardware that merely borrows the ideas, which is the honest state of the art.

What to be sceptical of

Every one of the four benefits is written in the future tense: will offer, will be able to, will simplify. That is not evasion — the article says plainly where the technology is today — but it does mean the four benefits are a specification, not a result. Two are engineering claims that can be tested (does failover across sites hold the loop? does a DST change really require no hardware?) and two are procurement claims dressed as engineering (scalability and flexibility mostly mean you stop buying cabinets). The procurement ones are the easier sell and the harder to verify, because their cost lands years later, in the projects that did not have to be replanned.

The useful way to hold this is as a dependency chain rather than a product claim: containerised control needs a virtualised host, a virtualised host needs a field it can discover, and a discoverable field needs APL and a common data model. Section 02 covers the wire and this section covers the compute; the two that follow cover the data fabric that makes the result usable, and the enterprise claim built on top of both. Skip a link and the benefits do not appear — which is why the same four words have been on slides for a decade.

08Data fabric

Industry 4.0 as the springboard, and the silo it has not removed

Sean Saul's next piece is the honest framing of the same problem. Industry 4.0, he writes, has become hard to define: nobody is sure whether it is over or still arriving, and the technologies behind it have been generating data for years while “many operations do not look much different than they did a decade ago”. The reason is not that the technology failed. It is that project-based deployment produces silos — production, reliability, safety and sustainability each bought a plant-level solution with its own stack and its own data model, and getting that data out for cross-functional analysis is, in his phrase, a herculean task: manual, time-consuming and prone to error.

So the argument is a data argument, not a technology argument. Industry 4.0 is the springboard because it is where the data fabric can start; the failure mode it has to overcome is the one every plant already has.

  1. The pressureWhat it does to the architecture
  2. 01Optimisation has moved upMost high-performing organisations have already squeezed what closed-loop control can give them at unit and plant level. The remaining gain is across multiple plants or the whole enterprise — which is a data problem before it is a control problem.
  3. 02Rigid architecturesAutomation has relied for decades on rigidly defined architectures, highly specified installation requirements and hierarchical communication patterns. Those were reasonable when the boundary of the system was the fence line. They are the constraint now.
  4. 03Departmental stacksEach outcome — production, reliability, safety, sustainability — was solved at plant level with a stack and a data model designed for one department. Extracting, transforming and landing that data for shared use is where the effort goes, and it is not engineering effort.
  5. 04A software-defined stackHCI, edge and cloud supply the deployment model, with HCI collapsing complex networking into one scalable server cluster. The article is careful here: PID and other algorithms mostly run on fit-for-purpose controllers today, and only some workloads could move to HCI or cloud depending on performance requirements. That caution is the useful part.
The concession that dates the article

Two sentences carry the honest weight. The first: “some of the technologies behind the boundless automation vision are years away from broad adoption.” The second: the foundation “will be built upon the Industry 4.0 solutions available today.” Together they define the only defensible buying rule in the piece — choose systems built with integration by design, so that the path is an evolution rather than a full-scale modernisation. That is a real constraint on a procurement and not a slogan, because it rules out the cheaper point solution that solves this year's problem and creates next year's silo.

What is missing from the account

The article names the silo problem precisely and then treats the data fabric as the answer without pricing it. A fabric requires a common data model and common definitions — the very thing departmental stacks disagree about — and it requires someone to own the model as plants are added. The piece also inherits a comfortable assumption from its own vendor position: that the platform is the integration. In practice the hardest part is agreeing what a tag means across two sites that have both been running for twenty years, and no architecture diagram removes that meeting. The life-sciences example is telling — operations that are “born digital” find this easy, which is exactly why they are quoted and brownfield plants are not.

Read in sequence, the three articles are one argument at three depths. Software-defined control takes the control workload off purpose-built hardware. Industry 4.0 supplies the data fabric that makes the resulting data usable across sites — and concedes that the silos are organisational, not technical. The enterprise operations platform is what you claim to do once both are in place: govern standards, simulate before deploying, and patch without a shutdown. Each layer is a precondition for the next and the value appears only when all three are present — the conclusion of section 06, arrived at from the vendor side.

09Enterprise layer

The DCS is becoming an enterprise operations platform

Brian LaMothe, who runs applied research at Emerson's Process Systems and Solutions business, makes a claim in Control Engineering that is easy to read past: the unit of optimisation has moved. “Success is no longer defined by the performance of a flagship plant.” The next-generation DCS, in his framing, is an enterprise operations platform — software-defined control that spans sites, exposes one trusted real-time data set, and coordinates deterministic control with predictive and prescriptive analytics.

That is section 06 with a different owner. The layer stack in the total-plant argument ends at a boardroom screen; this article is about the software that has to sit underneath it for that screen to mean anything. Four moves carry the argument.

  1. The moveWhat actually changes
  2. 01Standards governanceFrom a document to a check. Corporate standards libraries fail not because they are wrong but because enforcement is manual — a paper or spreadsheet baseline, distributed, then compared by hand against live configuration at each site. Drift is not vandalism: it is local logic edits, staff turnover, seasonal temperature and humidity, and shifts in raw material quality. Data-driven governance means continuous visibility of deviations and a contextual comparison against the approved baseline, so “find the drift” becomes a targeted review instead of an audit.
  3. 02Enterprise engineering softwareConfiguration leaves the plant. Live and historical system configurations are pushed to a secure cloud, with role-based access scoped to each engineer's span of responsibility and exception-based views that show only where a site departs from corporate standard. Versioned history is the point: it lets a specific change be correlated with a shift in process performance, quality or reliability — the only way a standards library becomes evidence rather than policy.
  4. 03Simulation before deploymentProve, then promote. A digital twin seeded with the current configuration is the base model; candidate logic or ML-derived setpoint strategies are injected and the predicted KPI shifts compared — energy intensity, yield variance, constraint margin. What passes goes through a gated pipeline: shadow mode first, then a canary release, then fleet rollout. That is a software deployment pattern applied to control logic, and it is the most defensible idea in the article.
  5. 04Evergreen operationStop taking the unit down to patch. The control system has never been as patchable as IT because uptime is the product. The answer offered is decoupling: deterministic loops stay hardened, while adjacent services — analytics, reliability modules, visualisation adapters — run containerised and pull updates in a defined maintenance window without a shutdown. Orchestration then places each service by latency, data proximity and cost.
The one number, and what it is

The article's illustration: a coastal chemical plant faces a forecast 48-hour tropical storm. The platform ingests weather and logistics data, simulates a 12% loss of monthly ethylene output, and recommends that a sister inland plant raise feed rates 8% inside validated safety and energy envelopes, pre-position catalyst and adjust steam demand — checking flare limits, compressor loading and utility contracts against twin models, then publishing the approved changes as a versioned control update. Projected shortfall falls from 12% to 3%. Read that as arithmetic, not as measurement: the 12% and the 3% are modelled, and the nine-point recovery is the value of the coordination, not of any instrument. What the scenario does show honestly is where the difficulty sits — not in the analytics but in the constraint check and the versioned publish, which is the configuration-drift problem one layer up.

What the vendor thesis is worth

LaMothe is Emerson's vice president of applied research and emerging technologies, so this is a supplier describing the architecture its own products are being built against, and the fleet-wide language is aspirational — the article itself concedes that some of the enabling technology is “years away”. The parts worth taking seriously are the ones that do not depend on the vendor: that standards decay at a rate set by staff turnover and season rather than by policy; that a configuration change should be traceable to a KPI movement; and that a control system you cannot patch without a shutdown is a lifecycle cost that compounds. Those hold whether or not the platform is called an EOP.

The honest test of any enterprise platform proposal is the one from section 06 moved up a level: can a number on the corporate screen be traced back — in one step — to a tag, a range, the calibration date, and the version of control logic that produced it. Standards governance, engineering software, simulation and evergreen updates are four different ways of making that trace possible. A dashboard that spans twelve sites and cannot do it is a reporting system with a larger licence fee.

10Decision layer

The boardroom is also a control loop

R. Russell Rhinehart makes the argument in Control Global: management is continually taking control action, and managers rarely have a control background, so the same questions apply — is the measurement valid, what is the inventory, how long is the dead time. It is section 04 read from the top down. He also warns that the human response to unasked-for help is itself a process, which is why the case has to be made in the manager's language and not in block diagrams.

Control conceptThe managerial version
Cause and effectThere must be a mechanistic link between the action and the result. Choose the lever with the strongest effect, the lowest cost and the fewest side effects — and beware technical folklore that only looks like mechanism.
Measurement validityEasy to measure is not the same as true. Rhinehart's example: friendliness towards managers might be a measure of morale, or it might be self-serving obsequiousness.
Inventory, not the readingFeedback control adds or removes inventory; the reading is only the response. You do not add safety incidents — you top up the inventory of concern and knowledge, which leaks away as people leave and as productivity pressure rises.
Process dynamicsTraining decays like an open-loop stable process, a grudge accumulates like an integrating one, anger compounds like an unstable one. Each needs a different response, and only the first is safe to leave alone.
Dead timeA report approved at every level, a decree that must be disseminated before it takes effect. Push cautiously, then wait for the effect before adjusting again.
NoiseReacting to normal variation is tampering. Rhinehart's example: the new university president saw an enrolment dip, launched programmes to fix it, and left them running after enrolment recovered. The dip was annual variation.

The point for an instrument engineer is not that management is soft. It is that the top of the stack is the slowest loop in the plant: the dead time is measured in quarters, the disturbances are unmeasured, and there is no way to run a step test. That is exactly why the bottom of the stack has to be honest. A deadtime-dominant loop that overshoots pays for it twice — and the boardroom has the longest dead time of all.
