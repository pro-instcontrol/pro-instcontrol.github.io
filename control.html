The loop decides

A controller is a small piece of arithmetic with a large responsibility: it compares what is happening with what should be happening and moves something. Everything difficult about control comes from the gap between those two — and from how long the process takes to notice.

01Anatomy
Single-input single-output feedback loop. e = SP − PV; u drives the final element.

Error is the only input

The controller never sees the process — only the difference between the setpoint and the measurement. Every tuning problem is a problem of interpreting that difference.

The valve is part of the loop

A sticky valve, an oversized valve or a positioner with a 3 % dead band will destroy the performance of a perfectly tuned controller. Tune the valve first.

Feedback is always late

The correction arrives after the process has already moved. That delay, not the algorithm, sets the achievable performance — which is why the next two sections are about time.

02Three terms

Proportional, integral, derivative — and what each one costs

Ninety percent of industrial loops run PI. Derivative is useful on slow, smooth processes and actively harmful on noisy ones.

TermActionTurn it upToo much
P — proportional gainOutput proportional to the current error.Faster response, smaller offset, tighter control.Overshoot, oscillation, eventual instability.
I — integral timeAccumulates error over time until it disappears. Note: lower Ti means more integral action.Offset driven to zero at steady state.Windup during saturation, slow oscillation, hunting.
D — derivative timeReacts to the rate of change, anticipating where the error is going.Damping, less overshoot, faster settling.Amplifies sensor noise, wears the valve, hides in the scan time.
Integral windup

When the output saturates — valve fully open — the integral term keeps accumulating error it cannot act on. When the process finally responds, the controller is still carrying that history and overshoots badly. Use anti-windup: stop integrating, or back-calculate from the actual valve position.

04Dead time

The one number that limits everything

Dead time (transport delay) is the interval between moving the valve and seeing any change at all. It is not lag — lag is the process easing towards a new value; dead time is nothing happening. A controller cannot distinguish “no response yet” from “no response”, so it keeps pushing.

The controllability ratio L/T — dead time over the dominant time constant — predicts how well PID can work before you touch a single tuning parameter. Where does dead time come from? Transport in a pipe, analyser sample lines, a slow-responding sensor in a thermowell, a scan time that is too coarse, or damping applied to hide noise.

The engineering response is rarely “tune harder”. It is to remove the delay: move the analyser closer, use a faster sensor, cut the damping, shorten the sample line. Every minute spent on the physical delay is worth an hour of tuning.

Rule

If the dead time is longer than the time constant, PID is the wrong tool — use feedforward, dead-time compensation, or change the process.

L / T ratio

< 0.1

0.1 – 0.5

05Beyond the single loop

When one loop is not enough

Most real plants are coupled: the flow loop changes the pressure, which changes the temperature, which changes the composition. These structures exist to break that coupling deliberately.

Cascade

A slow outer loop (temperature) writes the setpoint of a fast inner loop (flow). The inner loop absorbs valve and pressure disturbances before the outer loop sees them.

Feedforward

Measure the disturbance and act before the error appears. Requires a measurable disturbance and a rough model — the classic example is boiler feedwater on steam flow.

Ratio

One stream follows another at a fixed ratio — reagent to feed, dilution water to product. Often the ratio itself is trimmed by a slow composition analyser.

Split range

One controller drives two valves in sequence: the small one for fine control, the large one for capacity. Common on pressure letdown and compressor anti-surge.

Override / selective

Several controllers share one final element; a selector passes the safest signal. Used for compressor surge control and fired-heater constraints.

MPC

A model predicts the future over a horizon and optimises the moves, respecting constraints. Worth it when there are many variables, long dead times and real economic value in pushing to a limit.

What to watch for

Set derivative to zero first — most loops do not need it. Then raise gain until the response is just short of overshooting, and add integral action only until the offset is gone. If the loop becomes unstable when you raise the dead time, no tuning will fix it: the process is telling you the delay must be removed.

07Platforms & safety

Four platforms, one boundary

The choice is about scale, redundancy and engineering workflow — not about which box is more modern. IEC 61131-3 defines the programming languages that all four implement.

PlatformScan / scaleStrengthTypical use
PLC1–50 ms; one machine or skidFast discrete logic, robust, cheap, stand-alone.Packaging lines, compressors, water skids.
DCS100–500 ms; plant-wideIntegrated engineering, history, alarms, redundancy by design.Refineries, petrochemical, large continuous plant.
SCADA + RTU0.1–1 s; hundreds of kmGeographic distribution, telemetry over WAN or radio.Water networks, pipelines, power distribution.
Edge / PAC1–10 ms + cloud analyticsLocal analytics, protocol translation, ML inference.Condition monitoring, advanced control pilots.
Keep the SIS separate

IEC 61511 requires the safety instrumented system to be independent of the basic process control system: separate logic solver, separate wiring, separate final elements where practicable. Sharing a valve between control and shutdown is one of the most common and most serious design errors — the controller can drive the valve to the unsafe position just before the trip needs it.
