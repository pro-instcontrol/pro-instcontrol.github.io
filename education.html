01Programs at a glance

Two routes into the same trade

BCIT trains instrumentation technicians to install, calibrate and maintain field instruments and PLCs. HUST's EE-E8 trains control and automation engineers across control theory, electronics, drives and intelligent systems. Both converge on the loops, signals and standards this guide covers.

BCIT — Industrial Instrumentation & Process Control Technician

Diploma · full-time · in person · School of Energy, Canada

Half hands-on; field instruments, PLCs, calibration and tuning.

HUST EE-E8 — Control Engineering & Automation

Advanced program · 4 years · 132 credits · ASIIN accredited, Vietnam

English-taught; control theory, electronics, drives, robotics, AI.

02BCIT — overview

A trade, taught hands-on

The program provides a foundation in the industrial instrumentation trade, with practical experience in applying, installing, repairing, calibrating and tuning measurement and control instruments. Delivery is in person, and half of the program is hands-on with industry-standard equipment.

Credential

Diploma, full-time

School

School of Energy, BCIT

Delivery

In person; half hands-on

Facilities & pathway

Practical work happens in the Spartan Controls Centre for Energy Education and Research — two common unit operations and a steam plant. Graduates work as instrumentation and control technicians and can enter an apprenticeship at an advanced level with an industry sponsor.

04HUST EE-E8 — overview

An advanced, English-taught program

EE-E8 is HUST's Advanced Program in Control Engineering and Automation, taught in English and accredited by ASIIN (international). It trains engineers to master modern control, robotics and automation technology, following international standards for a global career.

Program

EE-E8 · Advanced Program

Duration & credits

4 years · 132 credits

Accreditation

ASIIN (2023–2028)

11 programme learning outcomes (PLO)

The PLOs span mathematics and science; electrical, electronics, instrumentation and control foundations; design and optimisation of automatic systems; modelling and big-data solutions; modern software tools; data analysis and AI; English communication (TOEIC 600); critical thinking and lifelong learning; multidisciplinary teamwork; professional ethics and international standards; and research project management.

09From the trade press

Train for the plant you operate, not for the classroom

You would not board an aircraft whose pilot had never touched a simulator. Luke Bicklein and Chris Helbling of Experitec argue in Control's October 2026 Other Voices column that this is effectively the standard of practice across much of industrial manufacturing today — and that what separates aviation from the plant is not equipment, but how seriously the responsibility is codified into training.

The comparison

“You wouldn't board an airplane whose pilot never touched a flight simulator. Yet, that's effectively the standard of practice across much of the industrial manufacturing sector today.”

Two professions, two attitudes to proof

Put an airline cockpit and a processing plant control room side by side and the parallels are striking: both demand split-second judgment from people carrying enormous responsibility for human safety, capital assets, environmental stewardship and brand reputation. Where the two diverge is in how that responsibility is codified. Aviation institutionalised recurring training and mandatory proficiency checks, so crews are prepared for abnormal situations before they arise in service. Manufacturers are left interpreting OSHA expectations that lack clear certification guidelines and enforcement.

The numbers are not close

Operators log an average of 20 training hours a year against a recommended minimum of 60 from multiple industrial bodies, and the hours actually completed keep trending down. Fewer than half of plant operators today are formally trained on a digital twin or an operator training system — the direct analogue of the flight simulator, and the only place where abnormal-situation training can happen without consequence.

Five gaps that compound

  • Consumption gap. Facilities still run analog — and in places pneumatic — controls even where more than 95% of field devices are digitally native, because usability was prioritised over optimisation. The HART diagnostics are already paid for and left unused, because nobody trained the technicians to implement them through installation, maintenance and troubleshooting.
  • Design gap. You cannot expect top-quartile performance from assets that were never engineered to support it — especially when training is booked as an unnecessary expense rather than a lifecycle performance investment. The decisions taken at the design phase get paid for several times over.
  • Performance gap. Nearly a quarter of manufacturing losses — unplanned shutdowns, scrap, slowdowns — trace back to inadequate training. Repeat failures are most often gaps in troubleshooting and in system-wide procedural understanding, and an understaffed, undertrained plant burns the time that should have gone to root-cause work.
  • Skills gap. A quarter of the industrial workforce is over 55 and only 8% is under 25. On the current trajectory, roughly half of those critical roles will not be filled.
  • Training gap. Adult Learning Theory research consistently shows 70% of traditional classroom instruction is forgotten within 24 hours, and 90% of new knowledge can fade within a week without hands-on reinforcement for students to revisit and use those skills.

What actually works

Classroom instruction paired with hands-on reinforcement — not one in place of the other — and room to fail safely. The 3Ds of training, describe, demonstrate, do, forge the muscle memory; scenario-based training consistently beats sequential, step-by-step instruction because it layers deductive reasoning on top of practice, and that is the difference between a troubleshooter and someone chasing symptoms. It is the same reason pilots log more simulator hours than flight hours.

Experitec turned those principles into the Performance Learning Platform: a factory on wheels. A working miniature process — pumps moving fluid through real equipment, pressure, temperature, level and flow instruments measuring it, control valves manipulating it, a modern control system closing the loops, and the asset-management, calibration and diagnostic tools the learner will meet in a real plant. It began as internal onboarding for young engineers, then was extended to manufacturers and educators chasing the same outcome.

The evidence that this matters is not in the survey data but in the schedule. Ask any outage manager how many days are added to loop checkouts because field devices were left out of service. That is a training failure surfacing as a turnaround overrun — and it is fixed by practice, not by another slide deck.

What this asks of the people who own the loops

  • Your loop drawings are the curriculum. If the training package does not open with the P&ID of the unit the learner actually works on, it is teaching a plant that does not exist.
  • The diagnostics you configured are the exercise. HART status, valve signature, sensor drift: these are the abnormal situations worth rehearsing, and they are already sitting in your instrument database.
  • Hours are not competence. If nobody on the shift can explain what a drifting zero does to a material balance, the training did not happen — whatever the logbook says.
