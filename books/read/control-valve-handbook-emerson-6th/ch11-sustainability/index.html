11.1 Sustainability

Sustainability has become the main pivot in relation to energy transition from traditional fossil sources to a diverse and more sustainable mix of energy sources. Several conversations remain open and under discussion as to what sustainability targets to be set and ways of achieving those targets. The key sustainability issues that are important to the process control world, includes decarbonization, emissions management, electrification, and energy efficiency and optimization. Technologies such as integrated architectures that provide critical insights into operations unit by unit or across entire industries will help support sustainability and decarbonization efforts. Intelligent field devices, control and optimization software, and process simulation tools can help companies meet their sustainability targets. Companies are substituting less carbon-intensive fuels such as natural gas and hydrogen, as well as employing renewable energy from wind, solar, hydroelectric, geothermal, and green fuel sources. While companies invest in new, cleaner energy alternatives, they must also look for ways to cut emissions in the short-, mid-, and long-term. Specifically, they are looking at available today to: prevent leaks in the first place; produce low to zero emissions by using lower carbon intensity sources; and run processes with more efficient combustion and lower emissions.

Carbon, Capture, Utilization and Storage (CCUS) processes extract point-source carbon emissions and sequesters them underground. The technology has the potential to remove 90 to 99% of an industrial facility's carbon emissions. It also supports scaling up production of low-carbon hydrogen by converting emissions into the alternative fuel source. According to the International Energy Agency, CCUS is the only group of technologies that contributes both to reducing emissions in key sectors directly and to removing CO2 from the atmosphere to balance the emissions that are the hardest to prevent. A crucial part of reaching the net-zero emissions goals that a growing number of governments and companies have set for themselves. Capturing carbon at the point of emissions and either utilizing it in products or sequestering it safely in underground geological formations will be critical to getting to a net-zero world.

11.2 Energy Reduction and Efficiencies

Energy efficiency and optimization remains a critical sustainability strategy for the world. Only about one-third of the primary energy produced today actually performs the desired function — heating and cooling a home, moving a vehicle, or cooking food. Two-thirds of the energy that goes into the energy system is lost through mediums like heat escaping through windows, brakes applied in vehicles, electrical line losses over long distances, various energy transformations, and unoptimized processes. There is a major opportunity to continue enhancing energy efficiency as reflected in many of the roadmaps developed to deliver tomorrows energy systems.

Energy intense industries are under pressure to become more sustainable and reach significant reduction in Greenhouse Gas emissions. There are also many potential renewable energy processes and technology solutions, like renewable (green) hydrogen, carbon capture, biofuels, biochemicals, and plastic recycling that show substantial reduction or elimination of Greenhouse Gas emissions. Solutions that enable the environmental sustainability progress cover four strategic areas: Energy Source Decarbonization, Electrification and System Integration, Energy Efficiency and Optimization, and Emissions Management.

11.3 Decarbonization

Decarbonization has gained prominence with increasing global temperatures over recent years, as against the goal of the Paris Agreement for limiting the temperature within 2C until 2050. Carbon mitigation has become an important and urgent issue in society, infiltrating into various societal fields including politics, culture, economy, environment, and industry. The Paris Climate Summit in 2015 reached an agreement declaring the objective of keeping the increase in global average temperature to well below 2C above preindustrial levels within the century and further to pursue efforts to limit the efforts to 1.5C (IPCC, 2014). Several industries in the chemical, power, oil and gas, hybrid, mining, cement, steel, paper, and pulp are exploring plausible pathways towards decarbonizing these heavy and carbon intensive industrial processes. As a result, most of these industries have identified and developed decarbonization pathways that will lead to total net-zero carbon emissions by 2050. To meet the internationally agreed goal of limiting global climate warming to a maximum of 1.5C versus the preindustrial period. Industries are considering two general methods.

  • Mitigation of carbon sources: massive penetration of renewable resources and efficiency improvement for carbon intensive industrial processes.
  • Deployment of CCUS technologies and reinforcement of carbon sinks such as afforestation and reforestation.

11.4 Greening OF

Greening OF is focused on a company or organization setting in-house initiatives and goals to reduce greenhouse gas emission. Greening OF also involves engaging upstream partners in making sure they are reducing greenhouse gas emissions in the products they provide to you as a customer. An example of ways companies can show strong commitment and investment in Greening OF is with treasure hunts at all locations looking for opportunities to reduce energy consumption.

Initiatives can be implemented to help improve internal sustainability performance. By enlisting energy providers, supply chain partners and logistics services to support similar objectives of greening their processes. Some sustainable initiatives that have significant impact on greenhouse gas reduction:

  • Automated lighting shutdown

Getting to net-zero and implementing decarbonization roadmaps within the four walls of a company lead to catalyze emission reductions where we can have the greatest impact — with suppliers and partners.

11.5 Greening By

As technology enablers of the world's low-carbon transition, positive environmental and climate contributions will come from the adoption of new energy efficient technologies and equipment, whether its controls systems, software, or hardware. The opportunity to support sustainability and decarbonization goals with products and services —whether it is upgrading to environmental packing, electric solution or valve condition monitoring, — technology and products will be an important part of decarbonizing the process world going forward. Any industry segment that has fugitive emissions or greenhouse gas emission is a candidate for greening their process, from power, energy, chemicals, metals production, cement, to the cold chain, waste management, as well as heating and cooling in commercial and residential buildings. But additional public and private sector efforts will be critical to make energy grids smarter, produce lower carbon, and be more resilient.

11.6 Greening With

As the world continues to accelerate towards total net zero carbon emissions, it is always important to share as a global and local community on what technologies should look like in the future to meet this demand and what adjustments or improvements must be made to accomodate these changes. This approach involves participation in leading industry forums, collaborating with research institutions to drive innovation, and enabling proactive engagement with governments worldwide. Environmental sustainability involves many uncertainties, but one thing is clear — the scale, scope, and complexity of the low-carbon transition is such that no one stakeholder (government, business, communities, or academia) can manage it alone. Multi-stakeholder alliances are essential to overcome its challenges.

Scope 1, 2, and 3 Emissions

The greenhouse gas protocol categorizes a company's greenhouse gas footprint into three different scopes. Scope 1 emissions are direct emissions from owned or controlled sources, such as emissions from comfort heating in our offices or from industrial applications in our manufacturing sites. Scope 2 emissions are indirect emissions from the generation of purchased electricity. These emissions are considered indirect because even though the emissions are a result of our activities, the emissions occur at the point of generation, typically a fossil fuel source like coal or natural gas.

Some companies have reported on Scope 1 and Scope 2 emissions but have not fully measured on Scope 3 emissions. Scope 3 emissions are all other indirect emissions that occur within the value chain. Scope 3 is typically a significant contributor to total organization emissions, as it acts as a “catch all” for any energy-related emissions not included in Scope 1 or Scope 2, and includes 14 other categories of emissions, including upstream and downstream emissions. Downstream emissions stem from distribution, from customers use of the products in their homes and facilities, and from how products are treated at end of life. Upstream emissions stem from the manufacturing and materials used in the parts that are purchased, or the direct emissions of suppliers. Scope 3 also includes miscellaneous emissions stemming from indirect activities like business travel and employee commuting.

We know from the industry averages that upstream purchased goods and services does tend to have a large chunk of Scope 3 emissions, the average is that this piece contributes just about 20-30% of the total Scope 3. Also, use of products sold tends to be around 60-65%, business travel and employee commute will be about 2-3% and logistics (in and outbound) around 2-3% as well.

In general, the precision around the Scope 3 emissions will continue to get better as more data is recorded, for this category and for the other categories. The objective here, is that while companies are not being asked to report out on Scope 3 today, some are being proactive and measuring this information now, with the expectation that they will be asked to report out on this information in the very near future.

11.7 Net-Zero Ambitions

As the world grapples with huge and urgent challenges posed by climate change, industry is rising to these demands by discussing and addressing activities that would help mitigate negative impacts on the environment. Companies establish targets to reach net-zero greenhouse gas emissions across Scope 1, Scope 2, and Scope 3 by completing indepth analysis of their total emissions and using this information to set a baseline year. Once the baseline year has been established, the company then can set a goal to reduce greenhouse gas emissions with a target year to be net-zero. To ensure our goals follow the latest climate science, it is best to be aligned with an internationally recognized organization like Science Based Target initiatives's (SBTi) Net-Zero Standard to set goals. SBTi is the world's leading organization in driving the adoption of science-based targets. A robust net-zero design requires the absolute reduction of greenhouse gas emissions by at least 90%, allowing for high-quality carbon neutralization in other parts of the ecosystem for any residual emissions, which cannot be otherwise abated.

Once a company or organization set greenhouse gas emission reduction targets, it is important to monitor and measure progress on a yearly basis. It is crucial to drive the sustainability and decarbonization agenda within a company to maintain motivation and interest in this all important and rewarding area of sustainability. The energy transition and decarbonization applies to green and brownfield projects along with MRO opportunities allowing customers the ability to green their processes by reducing greenhouse gas emissions in support of total net-zero goals by 2050.

11. 8 ESG

Investing in firms that champion environmental, social, and governance (ESG) issues has been discussed for decades but is now moving to the forefront of corporate initiatives. ESG may have transitioned to reality in 2004 when the United Nations Secretary, Kofi Annan, asked 50 major financial institution Chief Executive Officers to join an initiative for finding ways to integrate ESG concepts into global markets. In the 2006 UNs Principles for Responsible Investment (PRI) report, the term ESG was coined that explained how championing ESG factors made good business sense, created sustainable markets, and benefited society. Since that time, the movement has gained traction, with many investors and stakeholders actively demanding that companies engage in ESG improvements and publicly show their progress in key areas.

The calls have been so great that the U.S. Securities and Exchange Commission (SEC) is creating reporting guidelines so companies can provide consistent and accurate accounts of their ESG programs, alongside their standard financial reports. “At its simplest, ESG provides an umbrella framework to consider a company's impact and dependencies on the environment and society as well as the quality of its corporate governance.”

11.9 ESG - Approach by Companies

While societal and governance issues apply to nearly all companies, petrochemical companies are naturally more focused on environmental issues. There is a great deal of public pressure on the industry to decarbonize product streams and reduce emissions of pollutants, especially greenhouse gases. To accomplish these goals, most firms are utilizing a multipronged approach. One major initiative is the use of recycled and renewable feedstocks to create low-carbon alternative fuels. Such feedstocks might include soybean oil, corn oil, beef tallow, white grease and used cooking oils, as well as oils from algae, rapeseed and carinata. Other processes collect biogas from various agricultural and waste sources and then convert those potential greenhouse gas emissions to saleable biomethane for injection into natural gas pipelines. Another source of environmental improvements comes from the reduction of intentional and fugitive methane emissions. Remote oil and gas production areas and pipelines often use natural gas as a motive force for instrumentation and valve actuation, and methane is vented with each stroke or bled continuously.

Also, most control valves and/or pumps in chemical service tend to emit pollutants through packing and seal leaks. All these emissions sources can be substantially curtailed and even eliminated through proper equipment design, replacement with electric actuators, or improved packing designs. Another enabler of significant greenhouse gas emissions reduction are new designs in valve actuation that allow natural gas-actuated valves to be replaced with low and even zero-emissions alternatives. Often sold as retrofit kits, these upgrades provide dramatic reduction in methane and greenhouse gas emissions. Additionally, some automation suppliers offer a variety of low-bleed alternatives for natural gas-powered instrumentation, as well as very low power electrical alternatives, which can run on the power provided by small solar systems. In many cases the savings generated from the reduced methane emissions, reduced testing requirements, and additional natural gas production, pay for the instrument upgrade. These equipment types often meet stringent methane emissions regulations imposed by local governments.

11.10 Maximizing ESG through Collaboration

While both oil and gas industry and automation suppliers are making significant strides towards achieving their environmental goals, best progress often results from a collaboration between these two groups. This is already happening in some areas, with oil and gas specialists working with automation leaders to address the most pressing challenges by designing innovative methods to address ESG issues.

Another area of collaboration can be found in joint partnerships between automation leaders and universities. Automation suppliers routinely fund sustainability forums, award grants, and provide scholarships to further research in a variety of environmental fields. These efforts often result in next generation instrumentation and chemical processes, yielding significant environmental improvements.

The most successful outcome for our planet results from collaboration between industries. These types of collaborative efforts are already underway, with benefits being realized now, and more to come soon. Improvements immediately benefit society and the environment, but they also allow a company to define its role and choose the best path forward to meet ESG goals.

11.11 Conclusion

Increasing public pressure, stringent environmental regulations, and efforts towards sustainability and decarbonization has placed immense demand on most industries to identify and develop decarbonization pathways that will lead to total net-zero carbon emissions by 2050. These efforts will help meet the internationally agreed goal of limiting global climate warming to a maximum of 1.5C versus the preindustrial period.

11.12 Commonly Used Terms in Sustainability

  • Carbon neutral means that any CO2 released into the atmosphere from a company's activities is balanced by an equivalent amount being removed.
  • Net-zero carbon emissions mean that an activity releases net-zero carbon emissions into the atmosphere.
  • Total Net-Zero = Net Zero
  • Carbon-neutral and net-zero are two similar terms. In both cases, companies are working to reduce and balance their carbon footprint. When carbon-neutral refers to balancing out the amount of carbon emissions, net-zero carbon means no carbon was emitted from the get-go, so no carbon needs to be captured or offset.
