10.1 Basic Valve Types

There are numerous valve types, styles, sizes, and shapes available for use in industry. Even though there are dozens of valve varieties to choose from, the primary purpose of valves remains the same; that is to stop or start flow, or to regulate flow.

Regulation of flow includes: throttling, prevention of flow reversal, and relieving or regulating pressure within a system. Selection of valves for a system is based on the valve's intended service and design function. There are eight basic valve designs available:

  • Gate
  • Globe
  • Check
  • Diaphragm
  • Ball
  • Butterfly
  • Plug
  • Relief

10.1.1 Gate Valves

Gate valves are used for on/off service and are designed to operate fully open or fully closed. Because of excessive vibration and wear created in partially-closed gates, the valves are not intended for throttling or flow regulation. Gate valves are available in solid wedge, flexible wedge, split wedge, flexible split wedge, double-disk, parallel slide and parallel slide with eyelet styles. A typical gate valve and its major parts are shown in Figure 10.2. See Tables 10.1 and 10.3 through 10.7 of this chapter for sizes and dimensions.

Wedge-type gate valves have a tapered wedge that wedges between two tapered seats when the valve is closed. The solid-wedge design (Figure 10.2) is widely used and is suitable for air, gas, oil, steam, and water service. Flexible wedge gate valves are used in services that have a tendency to bind the solid-wedge design due to excessive variations in temperatures.

The design of the flexible wedge (shown in Figure 10.4) provides good seating characteristics (opening and closing) for a wide range of temperatures while providing positive shutoff.

Split-wedge and double-disk gate valves have disks or wedges that are made of two pieces. In the split-wedge design (Figure 10.5), the last turn of the handwheel forces the two disks against the tapered seats. The double-disk gate valve has parallel disks and seats. Closure of the double disk is accomplished by a spreader or wedge which forces the parallel disks against the seats. The double-disk gate valve is shown in Figure 10.6.

Friction, which causes wear on seats and disks, is kept to a minimum in both the split-wedge and double-disk gate valves because the seat and disk are in contact only on closing the valve.

When installing either the split-wedge or double-disk gate valve, the stem of the valve must be vertical (valve upright) which ensures the disks do not jam apart before closing.

The design of flexible split wedges mixes the advantages of parallel slide and flexible wedge executions. The special flexible internal construction, which is made in two pieces as parallel slides, allows valves to be closed by position. As a result, the requested torque is limited, as actuator sizing. The higher the medium pressure, the higher the valve tightness will be without jamming. The wedge shape minimizes the mating time between seats and wedge assembly, so low wear and lasting tightness are expected. When closing by position, tightness is ensured also at low pressure, zero leakage at 6 bar air.

The design of parallel slides with eyelet operates in exactly the same way as standard parallel slides, but when in the open position it has an inbuilt eyelet follower that closes the gap between the seats.

The eyelet provides the following:

  • Protects the seats from the flow.
  • Protects the disk from the flow.
  • Reduces turbulence in the integrated space, thereby providing a lower pressure loss and less noise.

The valve is considered to seal by position. This type of application is more suitable for higher pressure, larger sizes, and high velocity applications.

Body to bonnet junction of all isolation valves can be:

  • Bolted bonnet with bolts and nuts applied to all classes from 150 to 1500.
  • Pressure seal applied only to high pressure classes from 900 to 4500.

Pressure seal operation could be described as:

  • An axial force that increases as the internal pressure increases are applied to the gasket.
  • This force compresses the gasket, which deforms radially and axially.
  • In the radial direction it is pressed against the wall of the body and the cover, thus achieving the required surface pressure and associated sealing force.
  • The ring above the gasket absorbs the axial force and transfers it to the segment rings.
  • The segment rings are fitted in a groove in the body, thus transferring the axial force to the body.
  • The cover is pre-stressed by studs so that the gasket is deformed and the sealing effect achieved even when the internal pressure is low.

In summary, the bolted bonnet is the common solution for classes from 150 to 1500, whereas the pressure seal is used only in high-pressure classes from 900 to 4500, where the axial force generated by internal pressure becomes the main part of the sealing mechanism. 10.1.2 Globe Valves

Globe valves, unlike gate valves, are used in applications requiring frequent operation and/or throttling of flow. The design of the globe valve (shown in Figure 10.9) keeps seat erosion to a minimum, while making it an easy valve to service. When flow begins in the globe valve design, the disk moves completely away from the seat, thus minimizing erosion and wire drawing. See end of this chapter for sizes and dimensions.

Globe valves are available in three body styles: angle body, Y-pattern and T-pattern body, or straightway body (most common). Often available with a non-return function, referred to as a stop-check or non-return valve. In this instance, the valvehead is not fixed to the stem, so when in open position in the event of a flow reversal the valvehead will close and perform a check valve function.

All three body styles are suited for throttling, but each has its own flow characteristic and service applicability.

The choice of body style therefore depends on the direction of the piping, the allowable flow restriction and the nature of the medium. The straightway body is the default where no special requirement exists; the angle body replaces a bend and saves space; and the Y-pattern is preferred where a smooth flow path and low pressure loss are required.

Angle body valves provide for a 90 degree change of direction (see Figure 10.10), which in some installations saves space, material, and installation time. The inner design of the angle body valve offers less flow restriction than the conventional T-pattern, but more than the Y-pattern globe valve.

Because of the 45-degree stem inclination from the run, Y-pattern globe valves give very little flow restriction. Figure 10.11 shows a typical Y-pattern globe valve. The Y-pattern globe valve is ideally suited for applications requiring almost full flow in a valve, but with the characteristics of a globe valve. Applications for the Y-pattern globe valve include boiler blow-offs and services where mud, grit, and/or viscous fluids may be encountered.

The main types of globe valves according to their seat arrangements are:

  • Conventional disk
  • Plug disk

Conventional disk globe valves use a short, tapered disk that fits into a matched tapered seat for closure and throttling (shown in Figure 10.12). When flow conditions have a tendency to deposit or coke on valve seats, the conventional disk valve design is often preferred. The narrow disk used in the conventional disk valve will usually break through deposits, giving positive seating, rather than packing the deposits.

The plug disk differs from the conventional disk in that the plug disk and seat arrangement are longer and more tapered. The longer area of the plug and seat give the plug valve maximum resistance to flow-induced erosion. An example of a plug disk globe valve is shown in Figure 10.13.

A T or Tee pattern globe is one of the most common configurations. Its name derives from the inverted T shape of the valve. They are suitable for low pressure, high pressure, small bore, and large bore applications. This valve is considered as sealing by torque. Generally, the pressure will be from below the valvehead, and the torque applied to the valve acts against the pressure. An example of a t-type globe valve is shown in Figure 10.14.

A Y or Wye pattern globe is a common confirguration. Its name comes from the inclined Y shape of the valve. They are most commonly used on high pressure applications due to the smoother flow path compared to a T pattern that provides a lower pressure loss. This valve is considered as sealing by torque. Generally, the pressure will be from below the valvehead with the torque applied to the valve acting against the pressure. An example of a y-type globe valve is shown in Figure 10.15.

An angle pattern globe valve is a less common configuration of globe valves on isolation duty, more often associated with control functionality. They are considered as sealing by torque. Generally, the pressure will be from below the valvehead with torque applied to the valve acting against the pressure. An example of a angle type globe valve is shown in Figure 10.16.

Main characteristics of valve stop functions are as follows:

Stop Function: valvehead and stem connected; valve can only be closed by operation of handwheel / actuator.

Stop-Check Function: valvehead and stem not connected; valve can be closed by operation of handwheel / actuator; flow reversal.

10.1.2.1 Valve Guiding Types

The key to reliable performance is ensuring that the closure elements remain aligned in the correct orientation. There are two main fundamental approaches to this - body guided and stem bonnet guided.

Body guiding is commonly used on larger globe and non-return valves. This is normally achieved by using guiding of the valvehead on the body.

Advantages: large contact area to provide stability of the valvehead.

Disadvantages: it may require hard-facing for high wear applications; it can be subject to fouling and binding if system conditions are not maintained, i.e. scale; a larger valvehead is often required. On NRV applications, if the valvehead seizes to the body, then the globe valve functionality can be lost also.

10.1.2.2 Stem Bonnet Guiding

Stem bonnet guiding is common on smaller diameter valves and is achieved by guiding the stem within the bonnet. The orientation of the closure elements is by an external anti-rotation device.

Advantages: it removes the guiding from the direct effects of the system media; can reduce the size / weight of valvehead; generally no need for hard-facing on components; on NRV designs, allows the valve to be used as a globe valve in the event of seizure.

Disadvantages: it may require a larger stem diameter for big size valves.

10.1.3 Check Valves

Check valves are used to prevent flow reversal in piping systems and connected equipment. The three most common designs of check valves are:

  • Swing check
  • Tilting disk
  • Lift check

These three designs differ mainly in how the disk returns to its seat: the swing check uses a hinge and gravity, the tilting disk uses an offset hinge location for quick closure, and the lift check uses vertical motion in the manner of a globe valve. The choice among them depends on the allowable pressure loss, the installation orientation and the risk of shock closure in the system.

The swing check valve design (shown in Figure 10.21) consists of a hinged disk that swings open when flow starts in the desired direction and swings closed in flow reversal situations. Because of this swinging action of the disk, it is important to install all swing check valves so that the disk closes positively by gravity. When fully open, the swing check offers less flow resistance than the lift check valve design.

In flow situations where shock closure and/or disk chatter are encountered, other varieties of swing check valves can be used to help minimize the problems. See Tables at the end of this chapter for sizes and dimensions.

Swing check valves with outside lever and weight arrangements (shown in Figure 10.22) or spring-loaded disks can facilitate immediate closure in flow reverse. This immediate closure minimizes the possible damages of shock and disk chatter in systems. The tilting-disk swing check valve is another type of swing check valve that is used to help prevent slamming (see Figure 10.23).

It can be used as an alternate design to many of the same applications where swing check valves are considered. The valve is opened by the flow direction, with the flow passing below as well as over the disk, and is closed by the combination of gravity and flow reversal. The flow over the disk provides greater stability of the disk in service. Generally, the advantages of this design provide the most convenient benefits above 6 in. and also on higher pressure and low flow rate applications, where there is a risk of disk stability.

The lift check valve design (shown in Figure 10.24) is used in situations where pressure drop is not considered critical. The flow pattern through the valve corresponds to that of the globe valve. Lift check valves are available in horizontal and vertical designs.

10.1.4 Preheater Protection Valves

The preheater system is designed to preheat the feedwater prior to it entering the boiler, to improve effeciency. This is achieved by taking steam from the system and using it to heat the incoming feedwater. This process is carried out in a heat exchanger, often referred to as preheater or feedwater heater, which consists of a number of tubes that contain high pressure water that are surrounded by relatively low pressure steam. In the event of a tube fracture, the high pressure water will overcome the low pressure steam and there is risk that water will force itself back down into the steam system. To avoid this, a water level indicator system is installed in the preheater and when this is triggered there needs to be a system to effectively isolate the high pressure water from the preheater. There are a number of ways to do this, but one system is using 3-way inlet and outlet valves. These can be either motor operated or medium operated, i.e. using the feedwater pressure to operate the valves.

Basic Valve Application

Normal Operation: water enters the inlet valve, then exits to the heater. The heated water enters the outlet valve from the heater, then enters the outlet valve and, finally, on to the boiler.

Bypass Operation: valves are closed. The water now passes over the top of the inlet valve seating and exits via the bypass outlet piping into the outlet valve, thereby isolating the heater from the pressure.

10.1.5 Reheater Isolation Valves

The design of both coal-fired and gas-fired systems is used to transfer heat from the combustion process into steam for the power generation process. At some point in the life of the boiler, it may be necessary to carry out a hydro-test to confirm the integrity of the boiler. This may be due to certification, or to check maintenance work, such as boiler tube replacement.

There are generally two sections of the boiler that are used to generate steam and are therefore subject to hydro-test. Normally, tests are performed on:

  • Main steam section
  • Reheater section

The main steam section is usually relatively easy to isolate, as it often has valves in the main steam and feedwater piping sections, which can be used to isolate in the main steam section in the event of a hydro-test requirement. However, the reheat section can be more difficult to isolate, due to the location and proximity to the turbine. The reheat lines are used to recover additional energy to increase efficiency and therefore, normally are used at lower pressures and on larger piping to minimize pressure losses through the piping. This is one of the reasons why there is often a reluctance to install valves in these lines, unless specifically required, because pressure loss could reduce efficiency.

When a hydro-test needs to be carried out, there are often few isolation points that would allow the hydro-test to be contained within the reheat section of the boiler. This is generally not a concern of the OEM, but of the end-user who will need to complete the work in years to come.

A common solution would be to cut the pipe and install flanges and blinds that will allow isolation of the reheat section. This can be a costly solution.

Typical Solution

A reheat isolation solution consists of an empty valve body sized with a full bore to minimize pressure loss. The valve is fitted with a large pressure seal access cover to allow the installation of a blind when required. This is normally fitted with an O-ring seal, but can also be fitted with a graphite gasket for steam test applications, if required.

Once installed, the closure is preloaded to the seal. The pressure seal closure is then reinstated and the valve can then be pressurized to the required test pressure. This pressure acts on the blind and as the pressure increases, so does the sealing load on the blind. Following the test, the plate can be removed and the valve reinstated to an empty full bore line for normal operation.

10.1.6 Turbine Extraction Valves

Turbine extraction valves are a swing check valve / tilting disk valve with a side / top mounted single-acting pneumatic actuator. Application: Extraction service and HP exhaust service in power plants with IP / LP steam turbine start-up. It's function is to prevent the reheat steam from returning to turbine on trip.

Main Features

  • Double protection, including positive closure and power-assisted closure.
  • Fast closure.
  • Positive tight seating.

10.1.7 Bypass Valves

In larger valve situations where high pressures and/or high temperatures are encountered, a smaller bypass valve is often installed to equalize pressure and/or to allow the pipe downstream to warm up before the larger valve is opened. 10.1.8 Diaphragm Valves

Diaphragm valves (sometimes referred to as saunders valves) are designed to control flow in corrosive services where line content could adversely affect valve components. Other applications for diaphragm valves are in services where contamination from outside sources cannot be tolerated; for example, the pharmaceutical and food industries.

Diaphragm valves differ from other valves in that the body of the valve and line content is sealed off from all moving parts of the valve by a flexible diaphragm. This flexible diaphragm seal prevents stem packing leakage of line content and flow contamination by packing lubricants.

Even though there are many variations of diaphragm valve designs, most can be classified as either weir-type or straightway-type.

Weir-type diaphragm valves are the most common type of diaphragm valve used. Figure 10.31 shows a typical weir-type valve along with is major components. The weir-type diaphragm valve incorporates a raised section (weir) halfway through the valve, which acts as a closure point for the flexible diaphragm. Because of the way the weir is formed in the body, diaphragm movement is shortened, which in turn prolongs diaphragm life and reduces overall maintenance.

Straightway-type diaphragm valves have no weir incorporated in the valve design. Figure 10.32 shows a typical straightway valve. This design gives the valve an uninterrupted passageway suited for flows which are viscous or contain solids.

There are many types of diaphragm materials available, depending on service and temperature conditions. Because longer diaphragm movement is needed in the straightway-type valve compared to the weir-type, material selection is limited for the straightway-type valve.

10.1.9 Pinch Valves

The pinch valve, like the diaphragm valve, uses a flexible diaphragm in the closing and opening of the valve. Pinch valves use a flexible hollow sleeve which is pinched closed to stop flow by manual or power methods. A simple, air-operated pinch valve is shown in Figure 10.33.

Pinch valves are ideally suited in services which carry suspended matter, slurries, and solid powder flows.

10.1.10 Ball Valves

The ball valve, as the name indicates, contains a ball-shaped plug within a valve body which regulates flow. The ball has a circular hole or flow-way through its center and when turned one-quarter of the way, the flow stops. Ball valves come in three general patterns:

  • Venture-port
  • Full-port
  • Reduced-port (sometimes referred to as regular-port)

The port patterns indicate the inside diameter of the ball flow-way. Ball valves may also be classified by their body style. Body styles comes in one-piece and multi-piece bodies. A typical reduced-port (or regular-port) ball valve with a multi-piece body is shown in Figure 10.34.

10.1.11 Butterfly Valves

The butterfly valve offers a simple design that is lightweight, compact, and inexpensive, particularly in larger valve sizes. It consists of a flat, circular disk hinged in its center, which closes or fully opens with a quarter turn. Seating for the disk is supplied by metal seats or resilient types of material like elastomers and plastics. Because of the advances in seating material, butterfly valves have found general acceptance in the oil, gas, chemical, water, and process fields. The valve is often used in place of a gate valve, but has the added advantage of flow regulation.

Butterfly valves are available in two basic body types:

  • Wafer-type
  • Double flanged-type

The wafer-type (shown in Figure 10.35) is mounted between two flanges and is held in place by flange bolts.

In situations where dismantling of equipment or lines may require disconnecting of one of the holding flanges, a lug-wafer butterfly valve can be used. The lug-wafer butterfly valve (shown in Figure 10.36) has tapped lugs through which the flange bolts are screwed and hold the valve in place, even when one flange is removed. In most wafer butterfly valves, the elastomer seal also acts as its own flange gasket.

The double-flanged butterfly (shown in Figure 10.37) incorporates two flange ends which are bolted individually into the pipework or equipment flanges. Gaskets are used between the valve ends and connecting flanges.

10.1.12 Plug Valves

Plug valves (also known as cock valves) consist of a tapered or parallel side plug which can be rotated a quarter turn within a valve body. The quarter turn gives full closure or fully open operation of the valve. There are two basic types of plug valves available:

  • Lubricated plug
  • Non-lubricated plug

One variation of the lubricated plug is shown in Figure 10.38. The lubricated plug differs from the non-lubricated plug in that it provides a means to lubricate the seating surfaces of the valve. This lubrication helps to eliminate valve seizing while still providing a positive seal. Lubricated plugs should not be used if flow contamination may be a problem.

The plug valve design also lends itself to multi-port valve arrangements as shown in Figure 10.39.
