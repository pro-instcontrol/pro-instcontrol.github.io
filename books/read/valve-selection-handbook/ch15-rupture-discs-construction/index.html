Rupture Discs I — Types and Construction

Peter Smith & R. W. Zappe · Valve Selection Handbook, 5th ed. (2004)

Chapter 15 · 34 source pages · 33 figures · companion notes

Review and application

A rupture disc is a non-reclosing relief device and must be replaced after bursting. Despite the economic consequence, discs can outperform relief valves where reaction or overpressurization is too rapid for valve inertia, where even minute leakage to atmosphere cannot be tolerated, where the fluid is extremely viscous, where solids would deposit under a valve disc and disable it, or where low temperature would cause a valve to seize.

Discs may be installed in series or parallel, or combined with relief valves. Two in series prevent corrosive leakage if the first disc leaks, although premature failure of the first is likely to burst the second; they also form a quick-opening device when the interspace is depressurized. A parallel disc acts as secondary relief. An upstream disc keeps corrosive fluid out of the valve, prevents atmospheric leakage, prevents deposits at the seat and reduces valve maintenance. A downstream disc keeps corrosive vent-system fluid out of the valve; both positions may be used together.

Limitations in liquid systems

When a ductile disc bursts in gas, expanding gas opens it in milliseconds. In liquid service this requires a sufficiently large gas pocket; for rapid full opening, the recommended upstream gas volume is at least ten pipe diameters of the connected pipe. In a totally liquid-full system heated at constant volume, pressure first only deforms the disc; the added volume may temporarily stop further rise, but a forward-acting disc finally fails at rated pressure. Only limited reverse-buckling types burst at rated pressure in liquid-full service and the maker should be consulted. Brittle graphite discs open instantly in any service. Regardless of type, a gas pocket reduces pressure rise from liquid volume change and damps peak impulse loads in pulsating service.

Construction materials

Common ductile materials are stainless steel, Inconel, Monel and nickel, with aluminum, tantalum, platinum, gold, silver and Hastelloy B and C also used. Titanium and Hastelloy should be selected only where no substitute exists because both tend to fail prematurely through brittleness. Ductile discs may carry FEP, TFE, PFA, epoxy, vinyl, lead or polyethylene coatings and linings. Recommended maximum temperatures are: aluminum and silver 125 °C, nickel and Monel 425 °C, Inconel 535 °C, stainless steel 480 °C; lead 120 °C, PVC 80 °C, FEP 215 °C, TFE or PFA 260 °C.

Brittle discs are almost entirely graphite, although cast iron and porcelain have been tried. Common graphite is made from low-ash petroleum coke calcined at high temperature, mixed with pitch, formed into blocks and heat treated. The porous brittle result is vacuum impregnated with phenolic or furan resin. Pure graphite is exfoliated powder compressed into an impermeable flexible sheet; it may absorb some liquid, so a process-side coating is sometimes needed, with temperature then limited by the coating.

Temperature, burst pressure and heat shields

Temperature changes material strength and therefore burst pressure. The relationship differs even between discs of the same material but different construction. Figure 6-1 is only qualitative for solid-metal forward-domed discs from one maker; each material lot has its own relationship established by the manufacturer. Reverse-buckling discs are considerably less affected by temperature.

Heat shields protect the process side from radiation, or radiation and convection, without interfering with rupture. Overlapping stainless flats allow pressure on both sides and fold open with the discharge. A spool may act as a heat sink with a second shield at its far end. Figure 6-2 is a holder packed with loose amorphous-silica wool for graphite discs. The shield fragments and is discharged on rupture, so it cannot be combined with a vacuum or back-pressure support that fragments could block. It suits dry gases only because moisture absorption changes wool density and may collapse the filling.

Application parameters

  • Rated or marked burst pressure is the average from bursting at least two test discs and must not exceed the vessel's maximum allowable burst pressure under the code.
  • Burst tolerance: up to 2.76 barg (40 psig), no more than ±0.14 barg (±2 psig); above that, no more than ±5%; some types offer ±2%.
  • Manufacturing range is the agreed band around the specified burst pressure that permits economic selection of foil without excessive finding tests, expressed as minus or plus/minus percentage or in pressure units.
  • Performance tolerance combines manufacturing range and burst tolerance. Operating ratio is maximum operating pressure divided by minimum burst pressure, typically 70% or less up to 90% by type and temperature.
  • Damage ratio is damaged-disc burst pressure divided by undamaged burst pressure. Reversal ratio is reversed-installation burst pressure divided by correct-installation burst pressure. A lot is a single group of same type, size, pressure and temperature limits from material of the same identity and properties.
  • Deflagration burns below the speed of sound in the medium; detonation propagates a combustion zone above that speed; explosion is rupture of an enclosure from internally developed deflagration pressure.

Metal discs: tension-loaded forward-acting types

Ductile metal discs fall into forward-acting tension-loaded and reverse-acting compression-loaded families. Forward-domed and flat discs are tension-loaded. A solid forward-domed disc is formed from flat stock by fluid pressure on its underside, generally above 70% of burst pressure, giving a hemispherical shape (Figure 6-3). Above the predoming pressure the dome grows; near 95% of burst pressure the centre thins locally and the disc bursts with some fragmentation. Normal pressure is commonly limited to 70% of rated burst pressure or less.

Thin foils may partially or fully collapse under vacuum or superimposed back pressure. Vacuum supports must closely fit the concave face (Figure 6-4); supplementary permanent supports may be needed for back pressure (Figure 6-5). Inadequate support allows alternate reverse flexing and stretching, producing the turtle-backing wrinkle pattern (Figure 6-6) and poor life. In liquid-full service the disc may first open as a slit and then open further as pressure rises (Figure 6-7). Low-pressure discs use protective rings, an integral vacuum support and a handling cap (Figure 6-8).

A slotted and lined forward-domed disc has three layers: a dome with pierced holes at each end, a fluorocarbon or exotic-metal seal, and a vacuum support where required (Figure 6-9). Cross-scored discs use thicker material, raising operating ratio to 80% or 85%, with a predictable non-fragmenting opening when combined with a fluorocarbon seal (Figure 6-10). For polymerization service the holder avoids the crevice where growth starts, and a vacuum support cannot be fitted (Figure 6-11).

Two slotted and lined flat discs serve one-way or two-way flow. Figure 6-12 is a low-cost environmental seal for transport and storage tanks or downstream of relief valves; it clamps directly between class 150 flanges without a separate holder and has a maximum operating ratio of 50%. Figure 6-13 protects low-pressure systems, withstands full vacuum, suits gas and liquid-full service and reaches an 80% operating ratio.

Metal discs: compression-loaded reverse-buckling types

In reverse-buckling discs, fluid loads the convex side in compression. Buckling pressure is governed by Young's modulus, which is more reproducible and less temperature-sensitive than tensile strength. Buckling occurs at much lower stress, so discs are thicker and easier to make to close tolerances over a wide temperature range. Rising temperature lowers material strength but expands and stiffens the dome, partly compensating. Reversal alone does not burst the disc; cutting devices, scoring or expulsion complete opening. Advantages include low burst pressures, operating ratios up to 90% and above, optional zero manufacturing range and ±2% tolerance, excellent cyclic and pulsating performance and longer fatigue life.

Knife-blade discs (Figure 6-14) suit gas or liquid with a large gas pocket; in totally liquid-full systems reversal is too slow, so the disc rests on the blades and is cut only after a substantial pressure rise, a known cause of vessel ruptures. Blades must be kept sharp and checked regularly. A teeth ring (Figure 6-15) pierces the disc at buckling for considerably lower burst pressure. A disc cross-scored on the concave side (Figure 6-16) opens in pie-shaped sections without knife assistance.

Discs with a partial circumferential score shear along the line and fold around a pivot (Figures 6-17, 6-18). In Figure 6-19 the score is perforated for extremely low burst pressure; the perforations are sealed by an O-ring energized as soon as pressure is applied, and the discs may also be used in liquid-full systems.

The slip-away disc (Figure 6-20) is expelled from the holder on buckling rather than torn. Its narrow flat seating is sealed by an O-ring or gasket with no special torque; an arresting device prevents travel along the vent line, allowing upstream installation. Operating ratio can reach 95%, sizes run DN 25 to DN 1200, but liquid-full service is unsuitable except in larger sizes and vacuum support may be needed. The slotted, lined disc with buckling bars (Figure 6-21) has a teeth-shaped slot and tabs that buckle and break so the disc hinges open while the teeth ring cuts the seal. It offers zero manufacturing range, tolerance down to ±2%, 95% operating ratio, DN 25 to DN 600, up to 120 barg at 22 °C and a 550 °C maximum.

Domed two-way discs act forward in one direction and reverse in the other. Figure 6-22 combines a perforated forward-domed disc with a partial score for overpressure, a PTFE seal that fails under overpressure and subnormal pressure, and a reverse buckling-bar disc for subnormal pressure. Figure 6-23 uses a perforated forward disc sealed by a reverse-acting Teflon disc, with a supporting girdle and knife blades; subnormal pressure presses the Teflon onto the blades, while overpressure puts both layers in tension and ruptures them.

Graphite rupture discs

Resin-impregnated graphite is brittle, ruptures with almost no deformation, homogeneous and weaker than metal; discs are thicker but can hold very small burst tolerances. Pure graphite is flexible and used for reverse-buckling discs, tolerating higher temperatures because it has no resin impregnation. Both suit gas and liquid-full services.

Monoblock discs (Figures 6-24, 6-25) are one-piece devices combining a flat bursting membrane with the mounting flange. The non-armored DN 25 to DN 600 type has a 90% operating ratio, −70 to 180 °C service and up to 500 °C with a heat shield, but needs controlled flange-bolt torque. The armored type has a bonded circumferential steel ring that keeps unequal piping stress from reaching the membrane. The two-way version (Figure 6-26) bursts at up to 10 bar in either direction with a minimum 0.7 barg difference between ratings.

Figure 6-27 has a replaceable graphite membrane in a controlled-depth recess, making installation insensitive to torque, but reverse supports reduce free vent area to about 50% of full bore. Figure 6-28 is a pure-graphite reverse-buckling disc, thicker and easier to handle, suited to chemicals and typically 550 °C. Above 1.2 barg it supports full vacuum, with ±5% burst tolerance above 1.0 barg.

Holders, clean-sweep assemblies and quick-change housings

Early flat-faced holders cut discs on the sharp upper edge and allowed slippage and leakage. Radiusing the inside corner solved cutting; a 30-degree conical seat wedges the disc and solved slippage (Figure 6-29). Overtightening can thin foils or deform the conical lip, so maker torque must be observed and stiffening rings protect fragile edges. Flat seats with tongue and groove remain common in Europe and the Far East for low pressure. Reverse-buckling discs do not slip under compression and may use flat faces, but supporting edges must not deform the dome or its edge.

The clean-sweep or viscous Tee assembly (Figure 6-30) serves services where viscous product could build up and plug the passage; the sweeping main flow reduces this problem. Quick-change housings (Figures 6-31, 6-32) allow rapid replacement by hand or by a pneumatic sliding gate. O-rings seal the positioned disc, and once system pressure is restored they form a powerful interlock between housing and disc holders.

Common accessories include jackscrews to separate the holder flange from a separately supported discharge pipe, eyebolts for lifting heavy holders, baffle plates that deflect discharge to protect personnel and absorb reaction force, and burst indicators giving local or remote visible or audible signals, optionally intrinsically safe in explosive atmospheres.

Double-disc assemblies (Figure 6-33) warn of product leakage from corrosion of the process-side disc or act as quick-opening devices. The interspace needs a telltale device, commonly an excess-flow valve and pressure gauge: a pinhole in the first disc raises interspace pressure and actuates the warning. If premature failure of the second disc cannot be accepted, its burst pressure must be raised after discussion with the manufacturer.
