Check Valves

Function of check valves

The prime function of a check valve is to protect mechanical equipment in a piping system by preventing reversal of flow. This is especially important for pumps and compressors, where backflow can damage internals, cause an unnecessary system shutdown, and in severe cases shutdown of the whole plant.

Check valves generally need no operator: the valve is operated automatically by flow reversal, although in very special circumstances this one-way facility has to be overridden. A device may lock the closure plate(s) open, or the plates may be removed; the latter requires dismantling the valve, removing the plates and reinstalling it. Check valves are automatic valves that open with forward flow and close against reverse flow.

Check valves are needed to prevent return flow, maintain prime after the pump stops, enable reciprocating pumps and compressors to function, and prevent rotary pumps and compressors from driving standby units in reverse. They may also be needed in lines feeding a secondary system where pressure can rise above that of the primary system.

Grouping of check valves

Check valves are grouped according to the way the closure member moves onto the seat:

  • Lift check valves: the closure member travels normal to the plane of the seat (Figures 4-1 to 4-7).
  • Swing check valves: the closure member swings about a hinge mounted outside the seat (Figures 4-8 to 4-10).
  • Tilting-disc check valves: the closure member tilts about a hinge near but above the centre of the seat (Figure 4-11).
  • Diaphragm check valves: the closure member is a diaphragm that deflects from or against the seat (Figures 4-12 to 4-14).

Operation of check valves

Check valves operate in a way that avoids excessively high surge pressure on closing and rapid fluctuating movements of the closure member. To avoid surge, the valve must close fast enough to prevent a significant reverse-flow velocity from developing before sudden shut-off; that velocity is the source of the surge pressure.

The rate at which forward flow retards varies widely. If several pumps run in parallel and one fails suddenly, its outlet check valve must close almost instantaneously. If a single pump fails and the delivery line is long with low outlet back pressure and pumping elevation, a slow-closing valve is satisfactory.

Rapid fluctuating movement causes excessive wear and early failure. Size the valve for a flow velocity that holds the closure member firmly against a stop; mount pulsating-flow valves as far as practical from the pulsation source and locate them where flow disturbance is minimal. The first step in selection is to recognise the operating conditions.

Assessment for fast closing

In most practical applications fast closing can be assessed only qualitatively. Three criteria serve as a guide:

  • Travel from fully open to closed should be as short as possible; a smaller valve is potentially faster than a larger one of the same design.
  • Inertia should be as low as possible while closing force is high enough to respond to declining forward flow; light construction may need a spring to augment the weight closing force.
  • Avoid restrictions around the moving closure member that retard free closing movement.

Mathematics in check valve operation is of recent origin. Pool, Porwit and Carlton describe a calculation for hinged-disc valves that sets up the disc equation of motion with the fluid deceleration characteristic of the system; certain physical constants of the valve must be known. It determines reverse-flow velocity at sudden shut-off, from which surge pressure is calculated as in Chapter 2. Manufacturers can use mathematics to design valves for critical applications and predict surge pressure.

Design of check valves

Lift check valves

Figures 4-1 to 4-7 represent a cross section of the lift check family. They need only a short lift for full opening, minimal where the seat passage is ring-shaped as in Figures 4-4 and 4-6, so lift checks are potentially fast closing.

Most lift checks guide the closure member so the seatings mate. Dirt in the guide can hang up the member, and viscous liquids cause lazy operation or hanging, so these types suit only low-viscosity fluids essentially free of solids. Figure 4-5 overcomes this with a ball that travels without close guidance and rolls into the seat for alignment.

Figure 4-2 is designed where low surge pressure is critical. A conical extension progressively throttles flow as the valve closes, and a dashpot acts in the final closing moments. A closing spring is deliberately omitted because spring breakage was considered a hazard for the intended service.

Figure 4-6 is for gas service only. Depending on flow it acts as a constant-flow valve, staying fully open despite minor fluctuations, or as a pulsating-flow valve opening and closing with each pulse. Constant-flow types follow centrifugal, lobe and screw compressors, or reciprocating compressors with low enough pulsation to avoid disc flutter. Pulsating-flow types follow reciprocating compressors whose pulses open and close the valve each cycle; built on compressor-valve principles, they withstand repeated seating impacts. The maker advises which type to use. Their behaviour comes from minimum lift with multiple ring-shaped seat orifices, low plate inertia, frictionless guidance and a spring matched to the duty.

Figure 4-7 combines a lift check and a stop valve. It resembles an oblique globe valve with the closure member disconnected from the stem: raise the stem and it acts as a lift check; lower and press it firmly onto the closure member and it acts as a stop valve.

Lift checks must be mounted so the closure member's weight acts in the closing direction, except some spring-loaded low-lift types where the spring dominates. Figures 4-1 and 4-5 mount horizontally only; 4-2 vertically upflow only; 4-3, 4-4 and 4-7 horizontally or vertically upflow; and 4-6 in any position, including vertical downflow.

Swing check valves

Conventional swing checks have a disc-like closure member swinging about a hinge outside the seat, as in Figures 4-8 and 4-9. Disc travel is longer than in most lift checks, but dirt and viscous fluids do not easily hinder rotation. In Figure 4-9 the closure member is an integral part of the rubber body gasket, steel-reinforced, opening and closing by bending a rubber strip connecting it to the gasket.

As swing check size grows, disc weight and travel eventually become excessive. Above about DN 600 (NPS 24) they are often multi-disc designs, with several conventional discs on a multi-seat diaphragm across the flow passage.

Swing checks should be mounted horizontally; vertical mounting is possible if the disc is prevented from reaching the stalling position. Fully open in that position, the disc's weight closing moment is very small, so the valve tends to close late. A lever-mounted weight or spring loading overcomes the slow response.

Figure 4-10 is a double-disc swing check with two spring-loaded D-shaped discs on a rib across the bore. It shortens the path travelled by the disc centre of gravity and cuts weight by about 50% against a single-disc valve of the same size; with spring loading, response to retarding flow is very fast.

Tilting-disc check valves

A tilting-disc check such as Figure 4-11 has a disc rotating about a pivot between its centre and edge, offset from the seat plane. It drops into the seat on closing and lifts out on opening. The centre of gravity of the disc halves travels only a short path, so the valve is potentially fast closing; this one is also spring-loaded for quick response to retarding forward flow.

Figure 3-77 may serve as a butterfly valve, a tilting-disc check valve or a combined tilting-disc check and stop valve depending on the drive. Tilting discs cost more and are harder to repair than swing checks, so their use is normally restricted to duties swing checks cannot meet.

Diaphragm check valves

The diaphragm checks in Figures 4-12 to 4-15 are less well known but deserve attention. Figure 4-12 has a perforated cone-shaped basket supporting a matching diaphragm, clamped between flanges or pipe unions. Flow through the cone lifts the diaphragm off its seat; when forward flow stops the diaphragm regains its shape and closes fast. A notable use is purge-gas lines feeding slurry or gluey service, where diaphragm valves remain reliable while other valves hang up quickly.

Figure 4-13 uses a pleated annular rubber diaphragm. When closed, a lip rests with pleats closed against a core in the passage; forward flow opens the pleats and retracts the lip. Elastically strained in the open position with short lip travel, it closes extremely fast and suits widely varying flow. Its differential pressure is limited to 10 bar (145 lb/in²) and operating temperature to about 74 °C (158 °F).

The closure member of Figure 4-14 is a flexible sleeve flattened at one end; that end opens on forward flow and recloses against reverse flow. The sleeve comes in many elastomers, externally reinforced with nylon plies like a car tyre. Its soft bore can embed trapped solids, making it especially suitable for suspended solids or slurry. Sizes range from DN 3 (NPS 1/8) to DN 3000 (NPS 120) for tidal gates.

Figure 4-15 applies the same valve as a tidal gate: when the outside tide is higher, the flattened sleeve prevents seawater flowing back into the outfall.

Dashpots

Dashpots damp movement of the closure member. Their most important use is where flow reverses very fast: if the valve cannot close fast enough to prevent substantial reverse-flow buildup before sudden closure, a dashpot acting in the final closing movements can considerably reduce surge pressure.

Selection of check valves

Most check valves are selected qualitatively by comparing required closing speed with the valve's closing characteristic; this works well in most applications. Sizing is also critical. For a critical application, consult a reputable manufacturer.

Check valves for incompressible fluids

For incompressible fluids, valves are chosen primarily for closing without unacceptably high surge pressure when reverse flow is suddenly stopped; low pressure drop is normally secondary. First assess qualitatively the required closing speed (Chapter 2, page 41); then select the valve type likely to meet it, as discussed in the operation section.

Check valves for compressible fluids

Compressible-fluid valves may be selected on a similar basis. Valve flutter can be a problem for high-lift checks in gas service, and a dashpot may be needed. Where gas flow fluctuates rapidly, compressor-type check valves such as Figure 4-6 are a good choice.
