Fundamentals I — Fluid Tightness and Sealing Mechanism

Peter Smith & R. W. Zappe · Valve Selection Handbook, 5th ed. (2004)

Chapter 2 · 6 source pages · 2 figures · companion notes
Key terms. fluid tightness · leakage criterion · leakage capillary · leakage rate · steam class / atom class (Lok) · zero leakage · sccs · contact angle · surface tension · waviness / roughness · yield strain · sealant · search gas.

The fundamentals of a particular type of valve relate to its sealing characteristics: in-line seat sealing when closed and, where applicable, stem sealing which should prevent potential leaks into the atmosphere. For process systems handling hazardous fluids — harmful to both the atmosphere and personnel — stem sealing is considered to be of more importance.

Valve seals Chỗ kín của van

One of the duties of most valves is to provide a fluid seal between the seat and the closure member. If the closure member is moved by a stem that penetrates into the pressure system from the outside, another fluid seal must be provided around the stem. Seals must also be provided between the pressure-retaining valve components. If the escape of fluid into the atmosphere cannot be tolerated, the latter seals can assume a higher importance than the seat seal — so the construction of the valve seals can greatly influence the selection of valves.

Leakage criterion Tiêu chuẩn rò rỉ

A seal is fluid-tight if the leakage is not noticed, or if the amount of noticed leakage is permissible. The maximum permissible leakage for the application is known as the leakage criterion. Fluid tightness may be expressed either as the time taken for a given mass or volume of fluid to pass through the leakage capillaries, or as the time taken for a given pressure change in the fluid system; in practice its reciprocals are used — the leakage rate or the rate of pressure change.

For the atom class, a technical report of the Jet Propulsion Laboratory, California Institute of Technology, defines zero leakage for spacecraft requirements: zero leakage exists if surface tension prevents the entry of liquid into the leakage capillaries. Zero gas leakage as such does not exist. Figure 2-1 shows an arbitrary curve constructed for use as a specification standard for zero gas leakage.

Proving fluid tightness Chứng minh độ kín

Most valves are intended for duties for which steam-class fluid tightness is satisfactory. The proving tests are normally carried out with water, air, or inert gas, and are applied to the valve body and the seat and — depending on the construction — also to the stuffing-box back seat, but they frequently exclude the stuffing-box seal itself. With water, the leakage rate is metered as volume-per-time or liquid droplets-per-time. Gas leakage may be metered by conducting the leaking gas through water or a bubble-forming leak-detector agent and counting bubbles per time unit — permitting metering down to 1×10-2 or 1×10-4 sccs (standard cubic centimeters per second), depending on the skill of the operator. Lower, atom-class leakage rates are detected using a search gas with a search-gas detector. Specifications for proving leakage tightness may be found in the valve standards of Appendix C; atom-class testing methods are described in BS 3636.

Sealing mechanism Cơ chế làm kín

Sealability against liquids Kín với chất lỏng

Sealability against liquids is determined by the surface tension and the viscosity of the liquid. When the leakage capillary is filled with gas, surface tension can either draw the liquid into the capillary or repel it, depending on the contact angle formed by the liquid with the capillary wall — a measure of the degree of wetting of the solid, i.e. the relative strength of the attractive forces exerted by the capillary wall on the liquid molecules compared with the attraction between the liquid molecules themselves. The opposing forces are in equilibrium when:

(2-1)πr²·ΔP = 2πrT·cosθ ⟺ ΔP = 2T·cosθ/r

where r is the capillary radius, ΔP the capillary pressure, T the surface tension and θ the contact angle between solid and liquid. If the contact angle is greater than 90°, surface tension can prevent leakage flow; if it is less than 90°, the liquid is drawn into the capillaries and leakage flow starts at low pressures.

The tendency of metal surfaces to form a contact angle greater than 90° depends on the presence of a layer of oily, greasy or waxy substances that normally cover metal surfaces. When this layer is removed by a solvent, the surface properties alter, and a liquid that previously was repelled may now wet the surface. The classic example: kerosene dissolves the greasy surface film — a valve that was fluid-tight against water may leak badly after the seatings have been washed with kerosene. Wiping the seating surfaces with an ordinary cloth may be sufficient to restore the greasy film, and with it the original seat tightness against water.

Once the leakage capillaries are flooded, the capillary pressure becomes zero — unless gas bubbles carried by the fluid break the liquid column. If the capillary diameter is large and the Reynolds number of the leakage flow exceeds its critical value, the flow is turbulent. As the diameter decreases and the Reynolds number falls below its critical value, the flow becomes laminar — and from Poiseuille's equation the leakage flow varies inversely with the viscosity of the liquid and the length of the capillary, and proportionally to the driving force and the capillary diameter. For high viscosity and small capillary size, the leakage flow can become so small that it reaches undetectable amounts.

Sealability against gases Kín với khí

Sealability against gases is determined by the viscosity of the gas and the size of the gas molecules. As the capillary diameter decreases and the Reynolds number drops below its critical value, the leakage flow again becomes laminar (Poiseuille). When the capillary diameter approaches the free mean path of the gas molecules, the flow loses its mass character and becomes diffusive — the gas molecules pass through the capillaries by random thermal motion. The capillary may decrease finally below the molecular size of the gas, but even then flow will not strictly cease: gases are known to be capable of diffusing through solid metal walls.

Mechanism for closing leakage passages Cơ chế đóng các kênh rò rỉ

Machined surfaces have two texture components: a waviness with comparatively wide peak spacing, and a roughness of very small irregularities superimposed on the wavy pattern. Even for the finest surface finish, these irregularities are large compared with the size of a molecule. If the material of one of the mating bodies has a high enough yield strain, the leakage passages formed by the surface irregularities can be closed by elastic deformation alone — rubber, with a yield strain approximately 1,000 times that of mild steel, provides a fluid-tight seal without being stressed above its elastic limit. Most materials, however, have a considerably lower elastic strain, so the material must be stressed above its elastic limit to close the leakage passages.

If both surfaces are metallic, only the summits of the surface irregularities meet initially, and small loads suffice to deform the summits plastically. As the area of real contact grows, the deformation becomes plastic-elastic. When the gaps formed by the surface waviness are closed, only the surface roughness in the valleys remains; closing those remaining channels requires very high loads that may cause severe plastic deformation of the underlying material. The intimate contact between the two faces needs, however, to extend only along a continuous line or ribbon to produce a fluid-tight seal; radially directed asperities are difficult or impossible to seal.

Central idea: tightness is not a yes/no state but a leakage criterion chosen for the duty — flow-control valves accept nominal leakage while spacecraft demand the atom class. For liquids the gateway to leakage is the contact angle and surface tension (Equation 2-1): a thin greasy film decides whether a valve is water-tight, and kerosene can destroy it. For gases, once the capillary shrinks to molecular size the flow becomes diffusive and can only be stopped by plastically deformed metal-to-metal contact along a continuous ribbon.
