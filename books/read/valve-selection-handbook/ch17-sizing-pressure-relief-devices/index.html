Why correct sizing matters

Optimum sizing of pressure relief devices is essential to protect a piping system from overpressurization safely. An undersized device cannot relieve effectively; an oversized one adds unnecessary material and labor cost. Each flow regime has its own formula or procedure for finding the required nozzle area or flow rate.

  • Gas or vapor: subsonic / non-choked and sonic / choked flow.
  • Liquid flow.
  • Mixed-phase flow: gas with liquid droplets, liquid with gas bubbles, and liquid flashing to vapor through the valve.

At the time of publication, statutory bodies and standards organizations had not issued firm recommendations for mixed-phase sizing equations. The equations are presented in SI and customary imperial units; nomenclature is in Table 7-1 and Figure 7-1.

The subscripts at the marked points are used consistently in the flow equations: source vessel (1), vessel nozzle (E), valve inlet (N), valve outlet (2), discharge pipe outlet (3) and stream temperature (T). This distinguishes absolute pressure at the nozzle face, in the chamber between nozzle and outlet, and at the discharge pipe end.

Gas and vapor

Gas and vapor sizing equations rest on the ideal-gas laws with isentropic flow through the nozzle. Departure from ideal behavior is corrected by the compressibility factor Z, evaluated at inlet conditions. If Z cannot be determined, Z = 1.0 is commonly used and is conservative where pressure and temperature are not too high.

The ratio of absolute downstream to upstream pressure across the nozzle sets the regime. Above about 0.5 flow is subsonic; at about 0.5 and below it becomes sonic. The exact transition depends on the isentropic coefficient k:

(7-1)PCF / P1 = [2 / (k + 1)]k/(k−1)

Pcf is the critical nozzle-flow pressure. Table 7-2 lists critical pressure ratios and coefficient C for k from 1.01 to 2.00. For sonic flow, area follows from mass or volumetric flow in equations 7-2 to 7-6; in SI mass-flow form:

(7-4)A = 131.6 W C K P1 Kb √(T Z / M)

For subsonic flow, equations 7-7 to 7-11 apply, with factor F read from Figure 7-2 against pressure ratio and k. For F, the back pressure must be P'2 in the chamber between nozzle and valve outlet, not simply the flange pressure unless the maker supplies a conversion. If P'2 cannot be found, use a discharge coefficient determined under API Standard 2000.

Figure 7-3 gives Kb for the effect of built-up and superimposed back pressure on the lift of bellows-balanced gas or vapor valves. Kb stays at 1.0 for low back pressure and then falls, with the decline depending on 10% or 20% overpressure.

Dry saturated and superheated steam

In the United States, dry saturated steam relief valves were historically sized by Napier's empirically formulated choked-flow equation. Up to about 105 bara (1515 psia), Napier agrees with general gas equations using k = 1.3. At higher pressures it can grossly oversize, which led Thompson and Buxton to introduce KN.

(7-12)A = W / (51.5 K P1 KN KSH)

For P1 below 1515 psia, KN = 1. Above that range up to 3215 psia, KN comes from the empirical expression or Figure 7-4. The superheat factor KSH is taken from Table 7-3. SI form equation 7-13 uses the corresponding limits of 105 bara and 221.7 bara.

Liquid flow

For liquid service, nozzle area depends on flow, discharge coefficient K, overpressure, back-pressure and viscosity corrections, and the pressure differential across the valve. The SI mass-flow form is equation 7-15:

(7-15)A = 0.621 W / [K KP KV KW √((P1 − P2) ρ)]

Kp in Figure 7-5 applies to valves needing 25% overpressure for full lift. At lower overpressure Kp must be used; below 10% the tendency to chatter is heightened. For valves fully open within 10% overpressure, the certified K must come from the manufacturer. Above 25%, capacity changes only with overpressure itself.

Kw in Figure 7-6 is the back-pressure factor for bellows-balanced liquid valves; product-specific values must come from the maker. Conventional valves are affected by built-up back pressure, but capacity correction for it is not commonly allowed.

For viscous service, iterate: first solve non-viscous flow and choose the next standard orifice; then compute Reynolds number from equation 7-16, 7-17 or 7-18; read Kv from Figure 7-7 and apply it to the initial area. If the corrected area exceeds the chosen orifice, repeat with the next larger size. The Saybolt expression is not recommended below 100 SU.

Inlet pressure loss and inlet piping

Discharge capacity responds to pressure at the valve inlet, equal to set pressure minus inlet pressure loss. For sonic gas flow, mass flow falls in direct proportion to inlet loss; for subsonic gas and liquid, the change depends on the differential across the nozzle. A 3% inlet loss is commonly neglected for direct-acting valves, but any loss above 3% must be accounted for, especially with snap-acting flowing pilots.

Installation codes require cumulative non-recoverable inlet loss, including vessel-nozzle entry loss, not to exceed 3% of set pressure at rated mass flow. The Figure 7-8 procedure estimates the proposed resistance coefficient, computes 0.9Ae/(AnK), reads the coefficient for 3% loss and compares the two. The chart assumes k = 1.3 for gas and vapor; the dashed line applies to liquids regardless of P2/P1.

Discharge piping

Before sizing the discharge pipe, obtain the permissible outlet back pressure P2 from the valve manufacturer. For bellows-balanced valves it is the sum of built-up and superimposed back pressure. Compute the resistance coefficient of the proposed pipe and compare it with the coefficient for permissible built-up back pressure; redesign if the proposed line is more resistive.

For liquids, equation 7-19 expresses built-up back pressure relative to the differential across the valve; if the limit is 10% of set pressure, equation 7-20 gives the discharge-line ζ. For gas or vapor, calculate the critical pressure relation in equation 7-21, determine PT, then use P2/PT with Figure 7-9 to read the resistance coefficient for permissible built-up back pressure.

Rupture disc sizing

Nonviolent pressure excursions

Rupture discs serve systems with nonviolent excursions like those handled by relief valves, and systems subject to violent excursions from deflagration of gases or dusts in low-strength containers. The two regimes require different sizing approaches.

The ASME Code (1992) and API RP 520 Part I (1993) allow a simplified K = 0.62 method that neglects inlet and discharge pressure drops, provided the disc discharges directly to atmosphere, lies within eight pipe diameters of the vessel nozzle, has no more than five pipe diameters of discharge pipe, inlet and discharge nominal diameters at least the stamped NPS, and uses the manufacturer's minimum net flow area as A. Within those limits, equations 7-22 to 7-31 mirror the relief-valve equations.

ISO 6718 also permits a flow-resistance method requiring analysis of the whole system and a certified disc resistance factor KR. This approach is entering pending ASME and API revisions and the emerging European standard; it often needs software for speed and accuracy.

Violent pressure excursions

For vent panels on low-strength containers, sizing data come from simple models and vapor or dust explosion tests. Recommended procedures appear in the NFPA Guide for Venting of Deflagrations and VDI-Richtlinien 3673 (1995). Influencing factors include enclosure volume and shape, mixture homogeneity, maximum permitted venting pressure, static burst pressure of the panel, explosion severity as KST for dust or KG for gas, and use of nomograms.
