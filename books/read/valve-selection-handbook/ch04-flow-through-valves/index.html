Fundamentals III — Flow Through Valves

Peter Smith & R. W. Zappe · Valve Selection Handbook, 5th ed. (2004)

Chapter 4 · 19 source pages · 9 figures · companion notes
Key terms. resistance coefficient (ζ) · flow coefficient (Cv, Kv, Av) · velocity head · vena contracta · pressure recovery · Borda-Carnot loss · inherent / installed characteristic · cavitation · incipient cavitation · waterhammer · surge pressure · Joukowsky · bulk modulus · perforated diffuser · open area ratio (σ) · Mach number.

Valves may be regarded as analogous to control orifices in which the area of opening is readily adjustable. As such, the friction loss across the valve varies with flow as v ∝ (Δh)1/2 and v ∝ (Δp)1/2 (v flow velocity, Δh headloss, Δp pressure loss). For any valve position, numerous relationships between flow and flow resistance have been established, using experimentally determined resistance or flow parameters: the resistance coefficient ζ and, depending on the system of units, the flow coefficients Cv, Kv and Av. It is standard practice to base these parameters on the nominal valve size.

Resistance coefficient ζ Hệ số trở lực ζ

The resistance coefficient ζ defines the friction loss attributable to a valve in a pipeline in terms of velocity head or velocity pressure:

(2-7)Δh = ζ·v²/2g (coherent SI or imperial units)
(2-8)Δp = ζ·v²ρ/2 (coherent SI units) · Δp = ζ·v²ρ/2g (coherent imperial units)

where ρ is the fluid density and g the local acceleration due to gravity. The equations are valid for single-phase flow of Newtonian liquids, for both turbulent and laminar flow conditions, and may also be used for gas flow at low Mach numbers: as the Mach number at the valve inlet approaches 0.2, the effects of compressibility become noticeable but are unlikely to be significant even for Mach numbers up to 0.5.

Pressure recovery in converging/diverging passages Thu hồi áp suất trong đoạn co hẹp – phình rộng

In partially open valves and valves with reduced seat area (converging/diverging flow passage), the energy of the flow stream at the vena contracta converts partially back into static energy. Figure 2-8 shows the influence of the pressure recovery on the resistance coefficient of fully open venturi-type gate valves, in which the gap between the seats is bridged by an eye piece. The amount of static energy recovered depends on the ratio of the diameters of the flow passage (d²/D²), the taper half-angle (α/2) of the diffuser, and the length L of straight pipe after the valve throat in terms of pipe diameter (d = valve throat diameter, D = pipe diameter).

If the straight length of pipe after the valve throat is ≥ 12D, the pressure loss cannot exceed the Borda-Carnot loss:

(2-10)Δh = (vd − vD)²/2g

with vd the flow velocity in the valve throat and vD in the pipeline. This maximum pressure loss occurs if the taper half-angle α/2 > 30°; with a decreasing taper angle the pressure loss decreases and reaches its lowest value at α/2 = 4°. If the straight pipe after the throat is < 12D, the pressure loss increases and reaches its maximum when the static energy converted into kinetic energy gets completely lost:

(2-11)Δh = (v²d − v²D)/2g

The friction loss approaches this maximum value if the valve with a converging/diverging flow passage is mounted directly against a header.

Flow coefficients Cv, Kv and Av Các hệ số dòng Cv, Kv, Av

Cv states the flow capacity of a valve: the number of U.S. gal/min of water at 60 °F that will flow through the valve with a pressure loss of one pound per square inch at a specific opening position:

(2-12)Cv = Q·(Δp0/Δp × ρ/ρ0)1/2

where Q is in U.S. gal/min, Δp0 = 1 lb/in² (reference differential pressure) and ρ0 = 62.4 lb/ft³ (reference fluid, water). Because ρ/ρ0 = specific gravity G and Δp0 = 1, the equation is normally presented as:

(2-13)Cv = Q·(G/Δp)1/2

This relationship is incorporated in ISA standards S.39.1 to S.39.4 and IEC Publication 534-1, and applies to single-phase, fully turbulent flow of Newtonian liquids; for other flow conditions refer to IEC Publication 534-2, Parts 1 and 2.

Kv is a version of Cv in mixed SI units: the number of m³/h of water at 5–40 °C that will flow through the valve with a pressure loss of one bar:

(2-15)Kv = Q·(G/Δp)1/2

(contained in IEC Publication 534-1, same scope of application.) Av is a version of Kv in coherent SI units: the number of m³/s of water at 5–40 °C that will flow through the valve with a pressure loss of one Pascal:

(2-16)Av = Q·(ρ/Δp)1/2

Av is derived from Equation 2-8, presented as Q = A·(2/ζ)1/2·(Δp/ρ)1/2 with A the cross-sectional area (m²); the expression A·(2/ζ)1/2 is replaced by the single expression Av (IEC Publication 534-1).

Interrelationships between the coefficients Quan hệ tương hỗ giữa các hệ số

Resistance and flow coefficients are interrelated: if one coefficient is known, the others can be calculated (d = reference pipe bore, inches or mm):

(2-19)ζ = 889·d⁴in/Cv² · Cv = 29.8·d²in/ζ1/2
(2-20)ζ = 665.2·d⁴in/Kv² · Kv = 25.8·d²in/ζ1/2
(2-21)ζ = 512·d⁴in/(10⁹·Av²) · Av = 716·d²in/(10⁶·ζ1/2)
(2-22)Kv/Cv = 865×10⁻³ · Av/Cv = 23.8×10⁻⁶ · Av/Kv = 27.8×10⁻⁶

Relationship between resistance coefficient and valve opening position Đặc tính dòng và vị trí mở van

The relationship between the fractional opening position and the relative flow through the valve is denoted as the flow characteristic. When flow at all valve opening positions is taken at constant inlet pressure, the characteristic is referred to as inherent — Figure 2-9. In most practical applications, however, the pressure loss through the valve varies with opening position: Figure 2-10 illustrates a flow system incorporating a pump — the upper portion shows the pump characteristic (flow against pump pressure) and the system characteristic (flow against pipeline pressure loss); the lower portion shows flow rate against valve opening position — the installed valve flow characteristic, unique for each valve installation. When the valve is opened further to increase the flow rate, the pressure at the valve inlet decreases — so the required rate of valve opening is higher than indicated by the inherent characteristic.

If the pump and system characteristic shows that the valve has to absorb a high pressure drop, the valve should be sized so that the required pressure drop does not occur near the closed position, since this will promote damage to the seatings from the flowing fluid. This consideration frequently leads to a valve size smaller than the adjoining pipe.

Cavitation of valves Xâm thực của van

When a liquid passes through a partially closed valve, the static pressure in the region of increasing velocity and in the wake of the closure member drops and may reach the vapor pressure of the liquid; the liquid then vaporizes and forms vapor-filled cavities growing around minute gas bubbles and impurities carried by the liquid. When the liquid again reaches a region of high static pressure, the vapor bubbles collapse suddenly (implode) — this process is called cavitation. The impinging of the opposing liquid particles of the collapsing bubble produces locally high but short-lived pressures; if the implosions occur at or near the boundaries of the valve body or pipe wall, the pressure intensities can match the tensile strength of these parts — rapid stress reversals on the surface and pressure shocks in the pores finally lead to local fatigue failures that roughen the boundary surface until quite large cavities form.

Cavitation performance is typical for a particular valve type and customarily defined by a cavitation index. A convenient index used by the United States Bureau of Reclamation:

(2-25)C = (Pd − Pv)/(Pu − Pd)

where Pv is the vapor pressure relative to atmospheric pressure (negative), Pd the pressure in the pipe 12 pipe diameters downstream of the valve seat, and Pu 3 pipe diameters upstream. Figure 2-11 displays the incipient cavitation characteristics of butterfly, gate, globe and ball valves (water), compiled by the Sydney Metropolitan Water Sewerage and Drainage Board — they can serve only as a guide, since temperature, entrained air, impurities, model tolerances and the observer's judgment influence the test results.

The development of cavitation can be minimized by letting the pressure drop occur in stages. The injection of compressed air immediately downstream of the valve minimizes vapor-bubble formation by raising the ambient pressure — on the debit side, the entrained air interferes with the reading of any downstream instrumentation. A sudden enlargement of the flow passage just downstream of the valve seat can protect the boundaries of valve body and pipe: a chamber with a diameter of 1.5 pipe diameters and a length of 8 pipe diameters (including the exit taper) has proved satisfactory for needle valves in waterworks.

Waterhammer from valve operation Waterhammer từ vận hành van

When a valve is being opened or closed to change the flow rate, the change in kinetic energy of the flowing fluid column introduces a transient change in the static pressure in the pipe; with a liquid this is sometimes accompanied by a shaking of the pipe and a hammering sound — waterhammer. The transient pressure change does not occur instantaneously along the entire pipeline but progressively from the point at which the change of flow was initiated. If a valve at the end of a pipeline is closed instantaneously, only the liquid elements at the valve feel the closure immediately: their kinetic energy compresses the liquid and expands the adjoining pipe walls, while the rest of the column continues to flow at its original velocity until reaching the column at rest. The speed at which the compression zone extends towards the inlet end equals the velocity of sound in the liquid within the pipe. When the compression zone reaches the inlet, all liquid is at rest but at a pressure above normal; the unbalanced pressure creates a flow in the opposite direction, relieving the pressure rise and the pipe expansion; when this drop reaches the valve, the whole column is again at normal static pressure but still discharging towards the inlet — a wave of subnormal pressure starts at the valve; after the round trip, normal pressure and the original flow direction are restored — and the cycle repeats until the kinetic energy is dissipated in friction and other losses.

Joukowsky showed that instantaneous valve closure raises the static pressure by:

(2-26)ΔP = a·v·ρ/B
(2-26a)a = [K/ρ/B · (1 + K·D·c/(E·e))]^{1/2}

where v is the velocity of arrested flow, a the velocity of pressure-wave propagation, ρ the liquid density, K the bulk modulus of the liquid, E Young's modulus of the pipe wall material, D the inside pipe diameter, e the wall thickness, c the pipe restriction factor (c = 1.0 for unrestricted piping); B = 1.0 (coherent SI) or g = 32.174 ft/s² (imperial fps). For steel piping with D/e = 35 and water flow: the pressure wave travels at approximately 1,200 m/s (≈4,000 ft/s), and the static pressure increases by 13.5 bar per 1 m/s (≈60 lb/in² per 1 ft/s) of instantaneous velocity change.

If the valve closes within the time of a pressure-wave round trip 2L/a (L = pipeline length), the first returning wave cannot cancel the last outgoing wave — the pressure rise is the same as for instantaneous closure; this speed of closure is said to be rapid. Closure taking longer than 2L/a lets returning waves cancel a portion of the outgoing waves, reducing the maximum pressure rise — slow closure. To minimize undue surge pressures, stop valves should be operated slowly and uniformly with respect to the rate of change of flow velocity. Check valves are operated by the flowing fluid, so their closure speed is a function of valve design and the deceleration characteristic of the retarding fluid column: if the distance between the check valve and the point of pressure-wave reflection is long and the elevation and pressure there are low, the system tolerates a slow-closing check valve; if the distance is short and the pressure high, flow reverses almost instantaneously (e.g. multipump installations where one pump fails suddenly) and the check valve must close extremely fast. Guidelines for selecting check valves for speed of closure are given in Chapter 4.

If the surge pressure is due to a pump stopping, the calculation must account for the pump characteristic and the rate of change of pump speed after power cut-off. Fluid pressure and velocity as functions of time and location can be computed graphically or algebraically for simple cases; digital computers have made numerical methods convenient, with solutions to any desired accuracy (see Reference 25).

Where it is impossible or impractical to reduce waterhammer by adjusting the valve characteristic, consider changing the characteristic of the piping system: one or more surge protection devices at strategic locations — a standpipe containing gas in direct contact with the liquid, or separated from it by a flexible wall, or a pressure relief valve. Waterhammer effects can also be altered by deliberately changing the acoustic properties of the fluid: introducing bubbles of a non-dissolvable gas directly into the stream reduces the effective density and bulk modulus — a similar effect is achieved with gas enclosed in a flexible-walled conduit or hose running the length of the pipe. With even a small amount of gas present, pipe-wall elasticity becomes insignificant and the modified acoustic velocity is:

(2-27)a = (B·K/ρ)1/2

with the modified bulk modulus K = Kl/[1 + (Vg/Vt)·(Kl/Kg − 1)] and modified density ρ = ρg·Vg/Vt + ρl·Vl/Vt (Vg, Vl, Vt: gas, liquid and total volume). Figure 2-12 shows the effect of air content on the wave propagation velocity in water: a small amount of air drives the wave speed below the speed of sound in air (dry air, 20 °C, atmospheric pressure: 318 m/s).

Attenuation of valve noise Giảm tiếng ồn của van

Letting down gas by valves from a high to a low pressure can produce troublesome and, in extreme cases, unbearable noise. A major portion arises from the turbulence generated by the high-velocity jet shearing the relatively still medium downstream of the valve. A successful silencer is the perforated diffuser — a perforated flat plate, cone or bucket through which the gas is made to flow via numerous small orifices. The diffuser attenuates the low and mid frequencies of the valve noise but also regenerates high frequency noise in the perforations, which is more readily attenuated by the passage through the pipe and the air. A second benefit: the flow is distributed more evenly over the pipe cross section.

Ingard showed that the normalized acoustic resistance of a perforated flat plate mounted across the pipe is directly proportional to both the Mach number of the flow through the perforations and the factor ((1 − σ)/σ)², with σ the open-area ratio of the plate. Although this cannot be directly related to noise attenuation, the Mach number should be as large as possible and σ as small as possible: for practical purposes a maximum Mach number of 0.9 is suggested (lower if the available pressure drop across the diffuser is limited), and practical open-area ratios of 0.1–0.3 (below 0.1 → an excessively large diffuser; above 0.3 → too little attenuation). The peak frequency of the jet noise is also inversely proportional to the jet diameter — so from the noise-attenuation point of view the perforation diameter should be as small as possible; to avoid blocked nozzles, orifices with a minimum diameter of 5 mm are frequently used. If the flow velocity downstream of the silencer is high, boundary-layer turbulence along the pipe may generate noise comparable with the attenuated valve noise — experience suggests this is not a problem if the Mach number in the pipe is kept below about 0.3. Predicting valve noise and silencer performance is a complex matter — see References 29–35 of the source.

Central idea: four yardsticks for the same loss — ζ (resistance) and Cv/Kv/Av (flow) interconvert through the reference bore (Eqs. 2-19…2-22). Table 2-3 is typical only: ζ depends on the manufacturer. K1 rises steeply at small opening (Figures 2-4…2-7) — a valve absorbing a high drop should be sized smaller than the pipe so the drop is not taken near the closed position. Cavitation is measured by the index C (Eq. 2-25); instantaneous-closure waterhammer follows Joukowsky — ≈13.5 bar per m/s in steel water piping — with rapid vs slow closure judged against 2L/a, and even a little entrained air slowing the wave below the speed of sound in air (Figure 2-12). Gas let-down noise is combated with a perforated diffuser: Mach ≈ 0.9, σ = 0.1–0.3, orifices ≥ 5 mm, downstream pipe Mach < 0.3.
