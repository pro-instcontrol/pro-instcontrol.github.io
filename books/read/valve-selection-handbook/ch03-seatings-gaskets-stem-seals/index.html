Fundamentals II — Seatings, Gaskets and Stem Seals

Peter Smith & R. W. Zappe · Valve Selection Handbook, 5th ed. (2004)

Chapter 3 · 15 source pages · 1 figures · companion notes
Key terms. seating · galling · erosion · gasket · exfoliated graphite · spiral wound gasket · gasket factor (m) · blowout · stuffing box · gland · packing · Poisson's ratio · lip-type / squeeze-type / thrust packing · durometer · back-up ring · diaphragm · bellows.

Valve seatings are the portions of the seat and closure member that contact each other for closure. Because the seatings are subject to wear during the making of the seal, the sealability of the seatings tends to diminish with operation — a very important factor in selecting a valve for a specific process function.

Metal seatings Chỗ kín kim loại

Operational wear is not limited to soft-seated valves: metal seatings suffer too if the fluid is corrosive or carries particles. Metal seatings are prone to deformation by trapped fluids and wear particles, further damaged by corrosion, erosion and abrasion. If the wear-particle size is large compared with the surface irregularities, the finish deteriorates as the seatings wear in; if particles are small, a coarse finish tends to improve. Particle size depends on the material, the lubricity of the fluid and the contamination of the seatings with corrosion and fluid products — both reduce it.

Seating material must be selected for resistance to erosion, corrosion and abrasion; failing one requirement can make it completely unsuitable: corrosive action greatly accelerates erosion, and a material highly resistant to both may fail completely through poor galling resistance. The best material may also be too expensive for the class of valve — a compromise may have to be made.

Per Table 2-1, heat-treated AISI 410 (13Cr) is particularly impervious to steam erosion. If, however, the fluid lacks lubricity, type 410 in like contact offers only fair galling resistance unless the mating components differ in hardness. For steam and other non-lubricating fluids, a combination of 410 stainless and copper-nickel alloy is frequently used. Stellite (cobalt-nickel-chromium) has proved most successful against erosion and galling at elevated temperatures, and against corrosion for a wide range of corrosives. API Std 600 lists seating materials and combinations frequently used in steel valves.

Sealing with sealants Làm kín bằng sealant

Certain valves have the facility to periodically introduce sealants into the valve seat and stems to maintain an effective seal over an extended period. The leakage passages between metal seatings are closed by sealant injected after the valve is closed. The metal-seated valve relying completely on this method is the lubricated plug valve; other valve types also use sealant injection as an emergency seat seal after the original seal has failed.

Soft seatings Chỗ kín mềm

Soft seats are very effective but limited at high temperatures and pressures; proprietary manufacturers state the maximum and minimum design pressures and temperatures. One or both seating faces consist of a soft material (plastic, rubber) that conforms readily to the mating face — an extremely high degree of fluid tightness, achieved repeatedly. Limits: compatibility with the fluid, and temperature.

A sometimes unexpected limitation: when the valve shuts off a system suddenly filled with gas at high pressure, the high-pressure gas acts like a piston on the gas already in the system — the heat of compression can disintegrate the soft seating material. In globe valves, heat damage is combated by a heat sink — a metallic button with a large heat-absorbing surface ahead of the soft seating element. In oxygen service this may not suffice to stop the element bursting into flames; the inlet passage may have to be extended beyond the seat passage so its end forms a pocket where the hot gas accumulates away from the seatings. The main design consideration for soft seatings is to prevent the element from being displaced or extruded by the fluid pressure.

Gaskets Gioăng đệm (gaskets)

Flat metallic gaskets Gioăng kim loại phẳng

Flat metallic gaskets adapt to the irregularities of the flange face by elastic and plastic deformation. To inhibit plastic deformation of the flange face, the yield shear strength of the gasket material must be considerably lower than that of the flange. Free lateral expansion of the yielding gasket is resisted by the roughness of the flange face — the yield zone enters the gasket from its lateral boundaries while the remainder deforms elastically. If the face is rough enough to prevent slippage altogether (friction factor 0.5), the gasket will not expand until the yield zones meet at its centre. For gaskets of a non-strain-hardening material between perfectly rough flange faces, the mean gasket pressure is, after Lok, approximately:

(2-2)Pm = 2k·(1 + w/4t)

where Pm is the mean gasket pressure, k the yield shear stress of the gasket material, w the gasket width and t its thickness. With zero friction the gasket pressure could not exceed 2k — a high friction factor improves the load-bearing capacity. Lok showed that a friction factor below 0.5 but not below 0.2 diminishes the capacity only slightly; finely machined faces exceed 0.2, but aluminum gaskets on lapped faces reach only 0.05 — the degree of filling of the surface irregularities is then very low, so polishing the flange face is not recommended, even as sometimes done for important joints. Lok considered spiral grooves with a 90° apex angle and 0.1 mm depth (125 grooves per inch) representative for the steam class, and 0.01 mm (1,250 grooves per inch) for the atom class; the dimensional and pressure-stress relationships to achieve the desired filling are: steam class wt > 5 and Pm/2k > 2.25; atom class wt > 16 and Pm/2k > 5.

Gaskets of exfoliated graphite Gioăng graphit tách lớp

  • Highly impermeable to gases and liquids, irrespective of temperature and time.
  • Resists temperature extremes: −200 °C to 500 °C in oxidizing atmosphere, up to 3,000 °C in reducing or inert atmosphere.
  • Resists most reagents (inorganic/organic acids and bases, solvents, hot oils and waxes); exceptions are strongly oxidizing compounds (concentrated nitric acid, highly concentrated sulfuric acid, chromium(VI)-permanganate solutions, chloric acid, molten alkali and alkaline-earth metals).
  • Gaskets of initial density 1.0 conform readily to flange-face irregularities even at low surface pressures; further compression sharply increases resilience — a dynamically behaving seal, constant from lowest temperatures beyond 3,000 °C, absorbing pressure/temperature load changes and flange vibrations.
  • Suitable for sensitive flanges — enamel, glass, graphite.
  • Large or complicated gaskets can be built from overlapping segments; the lapped joints are not weak points.
  • Usable without misgivings in the food industry.

Common constructions: plain graphite; graphite with steel sheet inserts; inserts plus inner or inner-and-outer edge cladding; grooved metal gaskets with graphite facings; spiral wound. Because of the graphite structure, plain gaskets are sensitive to breakage and surface damage — steel-insert and spiral-wound types are commonly preferred, though the unrestrained flexibility of plain graphite facilitates sealing in some applications.

Spiral wound gaskets Gioăng xoắn ốc

A V-shaped metal strip spirally wound on edge with a soft filler inlay between the laminations; several turns at start and finish are spot-welded against unwinding. The strip provides resiliency compensating minor flange movements; the filler is the sealing medium flowing into the imperfections of the flange face. Manufacturers specify the installed compression for correct stressing and resiliency; the operating thickness is controlled by bolt loading, by the depth of a gasket recess, or by inner/outer compression rings — the inner ring also protects the gasket from erosion by the fluid, the outer ring locates it within the bolt diameter.

Load capacity at operating thickness is controlled by the number of windings per unit width — gasket density — so spiral wound gaskets are tailor-made for their pressure range. The diametrical clearance for unconfined gaskets (pipe bore to inner diameter, outer diameter to raised face) should be at least 6 mm (1/4 in); for confined gaskets 1.5 mm (1/16 in). Wrongly installed — protruding into the bore or over the raised face — sealing is severely impaired.

Windings are commonly stainless steel or nickel-based alloys (the inventory materials of most manufacturers), also special materials: mild steel, copper, even gold or platinum. For corrosive fluids or high temperatures consider stress corrosion and intergranular corrosion. Fillers are selected for fluid compatibility and temperature resistance: PTFE, pure graphite, mica with rubber or graphite binder, ceramic fiber paper. The filler also affects sealability: asbestos and ceramic-paper fillers require higher seating stresses than softer, more impervious fillers, and more care in selecting the flange finish.

In most practical applications the user must be content with commercially available finishes: for identical geometry, roughness typically varies 3.2–12.5 µm Ra (125–500 µin). Optimum sealing has been achieved with the finish described in ANSI B16.5, limited to 3.2–6.3 µm Ra. Roughness above 6.3 µm Ra may require unusually high seating stresses; finishes significantly smoother than 3.2 µm Ra may seal poorly — insufficient friction to prevent lateral displacement of the gasket.

Gasket blowout Gioăng bị thổi bay

Unconfined gaskets may blow out prior to a leakage warning when inadequately designed. This failure mode cannot occur if the friction force at the gasket faces exceeds the radial fluid force acting on the gasket:

(2-3)2µF ≥ P·t·πdm ⟺ F ≥ P·t·πdm/2µ
(2-4)F ≥ w·P·m·πdm (leakage begins to develop)

where µ is the friction factor, F the gasket working load, P the fluid gauge pressure, t the gasket thickness, dm the mean gasket diameter, w the width, and m the gasket factor — a measure of the sealing ability defining the ratio of residual gasket stress to the fluid pressure at which leakage begins; found experimentally. From Equations 2-3 and 2-4 the gasket is safe against blowout without prior leakage warning if:

(2-5)w ≥ t/(2µm)

Krägeloh regarded a gasket factor of 1.0 and a friction factor of 0.1 safe for most practical applications — the gasket width should be not less than five times its thickness to prevent blowout without prior leakage warning.

Valve stem seals Gioăng trục van

Compression packings Packing nén

Compression packings are the sealing elements in stuffing boxes (see Figures 3-17 to 3-19): a soft material stuffed into the box and compressed by a gland to seal around the stem. Packings must withstand temperature extremes, resist aggressive media, display a low friction factor and adequate structural strength, and be impervious to the fluid. Lubricants: oils and greases for water and aqueous solutions; soaps and insoluble substances for oil or gasoline. Liquid lubricants migrate under pressure, particularly at higher temperatures, causing the packing to shrink and harden — retightening is needed from time to time. To minimize this, the liquid content of stem packings is normally held to 10% of the packing weight. With PTFE, a solid lubricant became available for fibrous packings without liquid lubricant.

Asbestos is now avoided where possible, replaced by polymer filament yarns (PTFE, aramid) and pure graphite fiber or foil; other materials include vegetable fibers (cotton, flax, ramie — frequently PTFE-lubricated) and twisted and folded metal ribbons. Fibrous constructions in order of mechanical strength: loose fill, twisted yarn, braid-over-twisted-core, square-plait braid, interbraid; the latter three often contain metal wire within the strands for high pressure and temperature.

Sealing action of compression packings Tác dụng làm kín của packing nén

The sealing action is due to the packing's ability to expand laterally against the stem and box walls when stressed by gland tightening. The lateral stress of a confined elastic solid under an applied axial stress depends on Poisson's ratio:

(2-6)σ1 = σa·µ/(1 − µ)

(the source prints this in the form σ₁ = σa · µ/(1−µ); σ1 lateral stress, σa axial stress, µ Poisson's ratio = lateral expansion / axial compression.) The lateral stress equals the axial stress only at µ = 0.5 — bulk-incompressible. Soft rubber has µ ≈ 0.5 and transmits pressure much like a liquid. Solid PTFE: µ = 0.46 at 23 °C and 0.36 at 100 °C — transmitting 85% and 56% of the axial stress to the lateral faces respectively. Other packing materials are far more compressible in bulk, so their Poisson's ratio, where definable, is considerably below 0.5.

When such packing is compressed, axial shrinkage creates friction with the side walls that prevents full transmission of the gland force to the bottom — the axial packing pressure falls off quite rapidly (theoretical value calculable, applying to static conditions only; with the stem moving, the actual distribution is difficult to analyze). The distribution also depends on installation: a square cord bent around the stem initially assumes a trapezoid shape — inner-periphery pressure higher than outer.

When the fluid pressure at the bottom begins to exceed the lateral packing pressure, a gap develops between packing and lateral faces and the fluid enters; at low pressures the gland must finally be retightened to maintain the seal. At sufficiently high fluid pressure, sealing takes place just below the gland — where the fluid attempts to extrude the packing through the gland clearances — and the sealing action has become automatic. Fluid-pressure gradient readings along rotating-shaft stuffing boxes (Figure 2-3) confirm this: at low fluid pressure the gradient is near-uniform; at high pressure 90% of the pressure drop occurs across the ring just below the gland. For high pressures that ring is the most important one — select it for extrusion and wear resistance and install it carefully — and extra-long stuffing boxes serve no purpose for high-pressure duty.

If the packing is bulk-incompressible (soft rubber), the gland's axial pressure produces a uniform lateral pressure over the entire length; fluid pressure raises the lateral pressure by exactly that amount — sealing is automatic once initial interference exists. But rubber grips the stem and impedes operation unless the inner face is slippery — hence rubber packings are normally used as O-rings, which offer only a narrow contact face.

Corrosion of stainless steel valve stems by packings Ăn mòn trục van thép không gỉ do packing

AISI 410 (13Cr) stems corrode frequently where the face contacts the packing — usually during storage after the hydrostatic test, when the packing is water-saturated; placed into service immediately after testing, no corrosion occurs. H. J. Reynolds, Jr.'s investigations: the corrosion under wet packing results from the deaerated environment imposed on the steel surface — numerous small anodes at oxygen-deficient sensitive points of the protective oxide film, with the large passive masses acting as cathodes, produce galvanic-cell action within the metal. Graphite in the packing acts as a cathodic material and aggravates the attack through increased galvanic current density. Because of this mechanism, a non-corrosive packing built from non-corrosive ingredients is impractical — a corrosion inhibitor is required: of the anodic inhibitors, only those with an oxidizing anion such as sodium nitrite are efficient; cathodic protection by sacrificial metals such as zinc also works well; best protection with minimum effect on compression characteristics comes from homogeneously dispersed sodium nitrite plus a zinc-dust interlayer. High-chromium stainless steels — especially with nickel — show markedly better resistance to inhibited packing.

Lip-type packings Packing môi (lip-type)

Lip-type packings expand laterally through the flexibility of their lips, forced against the walls by the fluid pressure — permitting rigid construction materials unsuitable as compression packings. On the debit side, sealing is one-directional. Most valve-stem lip packings are virgin or filled PTFE; fabric-reinforced rubber and leather mainly for hydraulics. Most are V-shaped, fitting narrow packing spaces. PTFE/reinforced-rubber V-rings touch only near the lip tips, gaps letting fluid pressure act freely on the lips; leather rings lack that rigidity and are designed to fully support each other. PTFE/rubber V-packings have flared lips preloading the walls — only slight initial tightening is needed; leather rings have straight walls and need a higher axial preload. Where low friction matters (automatic control valves), the packing is often loaded from the bottom by a spring of predetermined strength to prevent manual overloading.

Squeeze-type packings Packing nén ép (squeeze-type)

The name applies to O-rings and the like: installed with lateral squeeze, relying on elastic strain to maintain the preload. When fluid pressure enters from the bottom, the packing moves toward the clearance gap between stem and back-up support and plugs the leakage path; on depressurizing it regains its configuration. Because elastomers have the necessary high yield strain, most squeeze packings are made of them. Extrusion is controlled by the clearance-gap width and elastomer rigidity (modulus of elasticity, conventionally expressed as Durometer hardness — the same durometer value may mean different moduli for different compound classes). Very small gaps are controlled by leather or plastic back-up rings fitted tightly around the stem. O-ring manufacturers supply tables relating durometer, clearance and the fluid pressure safe against extrusion.

Thrust packings Packing đỡ trục (thrust)

A packing ring or washer mounted between shoulders on bonnet and stem, the stem free to move axially against it. The initial seal is provided either by a supplementary radial packing (compression type) or by a spring forcing the stem shoulder against the thrust packing; fluid pressure then forces more intimate contact. Thrust packings are found frequently in ball valves (Figures 3-61 to 3-63, 3-65, 3-67).

Diaphragm valve stem seals Gioăng trục kiểu màng

Diaphragms are flexible pressure-containing valve covers linking the stem with the closure member — preventing any leakage past the stem to atmosphere except on fracture of the diaphragm. The shape may be a dome (as in Figure 3-7) or a bellows (as in Figures 3-6 and 3-39), depending on the application.

Central idea: metal seatings live or die by galling when the fluid lacks lubricity — pair materials of different hardness or use Stellite; soft seatings die by the heat of compression on sudden gas fill (Table 2-2). Flat gaskets gain capacity from flange-face friction (do not polish); blowout protection needs width ≥ 5× thickness (Eq. 2-5). Compression packing seals automatically only at high pressure — 90% of the drop concentrates in the ring below the gland (Figure 2-3) — and longer boxes do not help; 13Cr stems corrode in storage after hydrostatic testing through galvanic action, cured with sodium nitrite + zinc in the packing.
