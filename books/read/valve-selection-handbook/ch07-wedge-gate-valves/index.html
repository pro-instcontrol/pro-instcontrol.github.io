Wedge gate valves

Wedge gate valves differ from parallel gate valves in that the closure member is wedge-shaped instead of parallel, as shown in Figures 3-34 to 3-45. The purpose of the wedge shape is to introduce a high supplementary seating load that enables metal-seated wedge gate valves to seal not only against high but also low fluid pressures. The degree of tightness achievable is therefore potentially higher than with conventional metal-seated parallel gate valves. The upstream seating load is not normally high enough, however, to permit block-and-bleed operation of metal-to-metal seated wedge gate valves.

The bodies of these valves carry guide ribs or slots in which the disc travels. The main purpose is to carry the wedge away from the downstream seat except near the closed position, so as to minimise wear between the seatings. A second purpose is to prevent excessive rotation of the disc between the open and closed positions. If some rotation occurs, the disc initially jams on one side between the body seatings and then rotates into the correct position before travelling into the final seating position. Some types, such as Figure 3-45, can dispense with a wedge guide because the wedge is carried by a diaphragm.

Compared with parallel gate valves, wedge gate valves have some negative features:

  • Wedge gate valves cannot accommodate a follower conduit as conveniently as parallel gate valves can.
  • As the disc approaches the seat, the seatings may trap solids carried by the fluid. Rubber-seated wedge gate valves (Figures 3-44 and 3-45), however, are capable of sealing around small trapped solids.
  • An electrical drive must be torque-limited rather than travel-limited. Its torque must be high enough to wedge the wedge into the seats when closing against full differential line pressure; closing against zero differential pressure makes the wedging still higher. To permit reopening against full differential pressure and to allow for increased effort from thermal movements, the operator must be generously sized.

The limitations of wedge gate valves are otherwise similar to those of parallel gate valves.

Variations of wedge design

The basic type is the plain wedge of solid or hollow construction, as in Figures 3-34 to 3-39. It is simple and robust, but distortion of the valve body due to thermal and pipeline stresses may unseat or jam the metal-seated wedge — a failure more often experienced in valves of light-weight construction.

Sealing reliability of plain-wedge valves can be improved by elastomeric or plastic sealing elements in either the seat or the wedge. Figure 3-40 shows a seat with a PTFE insert standing proud of the metal face just enough to ensure a seal against the wedge.

Self-aligning wedges

Efforts to overcome the alignment problem of plain wedges led to self-aligning wedges such as Figures 3-41 to 3-43. The simplest is the flexible wedge of Figures 3-41 and 3-42, composed of two discs with an integral boss in between; it is sufficiently flexible to find its own orientation. Being simple and containing no separate components that could rattle loose in service, this construction has become a favoured design.

The self-aligning wedge of Figure 3-43 consists of two identical tapered plates rocking around a separate spacer ring. The ring may also be used to adjust the wedge assembly for wear. To keep the plates together the body has grooves in which the assembly travels; when wear takes place in the guides, the original guide tolerance can be restored by adjusting the spacer ring thickness.

Rubber-lined wedges

Rubber lining of the wedge, as in Figures 3-44 and 3-45, led to new seating concepts in which the seat seal is achieved partly between the rim of the wedge and the valve body. In this way the creation of a pocket at the bottom of the body could be avoided altogether, so the valves handle fluids carrying solids in suspension which would otherwise collect in an open cavity.

In the valve of Figure 3-44 the wedge carries two stirrup-shaped rubber rings facing the wedge rim at the bottom sides and the upper lateral faces. When closing, the rings seal against the bottom and sidewalls of the body and, by a wedging action, against the seat faces at the top. The wedge of Figure 3-45 is completely rubber lined and forms part of a diaphragm separating the operating mechanism from the fluid; when closing, the wedge bottom seals against the body bottom and body seats. The valve may also be lined with corrosion-resistant materials and is therefore widely used in the chemical industry.

Connection of wedge to stem

The wedge-to-stem connection usually consists of a T-slot in the top of the wedge receiving a collar on the stem. According to API 600, this connection must be stronger than the weakest stem section so that the wedge cannot become detached from the stem during operation.

The T-slot may lie in line with the flow passage, as in Figures 3-38, 3-39 and 3-41, or across it, as in Figures 3-36 and 3-42. The latter allows a more compact body and has therefore become popular for economic reasons; it also lowers the point at which the stem acts on the wedge. The T-slot must nevertheless be wide enough to accommodate guide play, including guide wear. Exceptions such as Figures 3-34 and 3-37 put the entire wedge thrust on the stem and so suit low-pressure applications only. In Figure 3-43 guide play is virtually absent, allowing the stem to be captured in the wedge.

Wedge guide design

Body guides commonly consist of ribs fitting into slots of the wedge, or slots receiving ribs of the wedge, as illustrated in Figures 3-42 and 3-43. Body ribs are normally unmachined for reasons of cost; their rough finish is unsuited to carrying a travelling wedge under high load. On opening, the wedge is therefore initially carried on the seat until the fluid load is small enough for the ribs to carry it. This may require considerable guide play, matched by the play in the T-slot suspending the wedge on the stem.

Once the body ribs begin to carry the wedge, the wedge must be fully supported. If the length of support is insufficient, the fluid force on the unsupported section may tilt the wedge into the downstream seat bore — a requirement sometimes not complied with, although some makers go to great lengths to ensure full support. There is no assurance that the wedge slides on the stem collar when opening: considerable friction in the T-slot/collar contact may tilt the wedge on the stem, and a tight fit with high fluid load may crack the claws forming the T-slot.

For critical applications, guides are machined to close tolerances and designed to carry the wedge over nearly the whole travel, as in Figures 3-42 and 3-43. In Figure 3-42 the wedge grooves are hard-faced and precision-guided on machined ribs welded to the body; the wedge is carried by the seat for only 5% of total travel. In Figure 3-43 the two wedge plates carry hard-faced tongues guided in machined body grooves, and guide tolerance is restored with the spacer ring.

Bypass and pressure equalizing

Wedge gate valves may need bypass connections for the reasons described for parallel gate valves on page 77. With a self-aligning double-seated wedge, thermal expansion of fluid locked in the body forces the upstream and downstream seatings into closer contact and raises cavity pressure. A similar situation may arise with soft-seated valves whose wedge can produce an upstream seat seal. Valves handling liquids of high thermal expansion, or where revaporation of trapped condensate can occur, may therefore need a pressure-equalizing connection as described for parallel gate valves.

Case study of wedge gate valve failure

Figures 3-46 to 3-48 show components of a DN 300 (NPS 12) class 150 wedge gate valve to API 600 that failed on first application. The valve was mounted in a horizontal line at 45° from the vertical for easy hand-operation of a gear drive. This position required the wedge to ride on a body rib, and the guide slot in the wedge had sharp edges. As the wedge travelled, those edges caught on the rough rib surface, causing the wedge to rotate until it contacted the opposite body rib and jammed. Further closing effort damaged the internals.

Further inspection showed that play in the wedge guides was larger than the possible travel of the wedge on the stem, so the stem had to carry the wedge tilted for part of its travel; the lengths of body rib and wedge guide were also far too short to support the wedge throughout its travel. This failure was not isolated but typical of a high percentage of installed wedge gate valves; all suspect valves had to be replaced before plant start-up.

Applications

Duty: stopping and starting flow; infrequent operation.

Service: gases; liquids; rubber-seated wedge gate valves without bottom cavity for fluids carrying solids in suspension; vacuum; cryogenic.
