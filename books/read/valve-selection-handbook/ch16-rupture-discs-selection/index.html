Selecting rupture discs

Rupture-disc selection depends on the pressure differential between operating pressure and the maximum allowable working pressure (MAWP). Manufacturing range, burst tolerance and recommended operating ratio must all fit within that differential. Figure 6-34 shows the levels for a disc as sole relief device, with +10%/−5% manufacturing range and ±5% burst tolerance.

The ASME Code allows rated burst pressure not to exceed MAWP but permits plus burst tolerance to do so. The minimum possible burst pressure can then be only 82% of MAWP; at a 70% operating ratio the operating pressure should not exceed 57% of MAWP. Above 2.75 barg (40 psig) some makers neglect the minus 5% tolerance when assessing minimum burst. With only a 10% differential, as is common in relief-valve installations, the only choice is zero manufacturing range, a 90% or 95% operating ratio and possibly reduced burst tolerance.

  • Service temperature and possible temperature excursions.
  • Fluid state: gas, vapor or liquid.
  • Cyclic or pulsating service, corrosiveness on both sides, vacuum or back pressure, and other operating conditions.
  • Some types cover only limited pressure and size ranges, so the manufacturer may need to be involved at the early design stage.

Rupture-disc device combined with a pressure relief valve

Rupture discs may isolate valve inlet or outlet from corrosive fluids before the valve is called upon to open, but must not impair the relief function. The following discussion is restricted to gas or vapor service.

At the valve inlet, the valve must pop in unison with the disc and leakage across a corroded disc must not pressurize the space between them. At the common 10% differential, discs with zero manufacturing range, ±2% tolerance and 90% or 95% operating ratio are available. Because valve set-pressure tolerance is ±3% and disc tolerance ±2%, the worst-case valve opening pressure can exceed disc burst pressure by 5%. The valve set pressure may need to be 3% below rated burst pressure if blowdown remains acceptable; setting no more than 5% above the disc has been reported to open it fully, but a lower valve setting is preferred.

  • The valve should be pop-acting and may be unbalanced unless back pressure demands a balanced type.
  • ASME requires a telltale indicator in the space between valve and disc; pressure build-up there raises the effective burst pressure.
  • ASME permits combination capacity at 90% of valve rated capacity or at a combination factor certified by test.

A downstream disc protects valve internals from corrosive discharge fluid. Leakage past the seatings can enter the chamber between them and the disc, so its upstream side also needs a telltale. When the valve opens, chamber pressure rises to disc burst pressure; assessment must include maximum rated burst pressure plus tolerance and superimposed back pressure. Valve and piping upstream of the disc must withstand it. This build-up may impede a conventional valve, so a balanced valve is suggested; with discs both upstream and downstream, choose a balanced pop-action valve.

An inlet disc combination may also be used in liquid service if a substantial gas volume between disc and liquid level ensures the valve opens in unison with disc burst. Designers must follow the applicable design code.

Reordering and user responsibility

  • When reordering, always quote the lot or batch number to obtain the correct replacement. The requested burst pressure need only fall within the ordered disc's manufacturing range, so rated or marked burst pressure can vary between orders of the same disc.
  • Plant personnel must understand handling, storage, installation and maintenance requirements. Installation must never be entrusted to someone unaware of those requirements and their consequences.
  • Each plant should keep records of installed disc location and specification, failure dates and history, agreed replacement periods, and ordering and storage instructions. Only personnel trained in selection, installation and maintenance should specify, procure and install rupture discs.

Explosion venting

Many industrial powders and flammable gases form explosive mixtures when dispersed in air. On ignition, pressure rises almost instantly and can rupture weak enclosures without adequate venting. Rectangular or circular vent panels are sized to keep enclosure pressure below a safe level (Figure 6-35). Figure 6-36 shows pressure versus time: Pstat is vent-opening pressure, Pred the reduced explosion pressure, Pult the enclosure's ultimate sustainable pressure and Pmax the unvented explosion pressure.

In a dust deflagration, far more dust is present than oxidant needed to burn it completely. Venting mixes unburned dust with fresh air and can form a fireball; one test extended at least 4 m below the vent and about 15 m horizontally (Figure 6-37). Personnel enveloped by such a fireball might not survive. Protected plant must therefore be positioned to shield personnel and prevent secondary explosions.

Where vented equipment must stand inside a building, locate it near an exterior wall with ducts as short as possible, preferably no more than 3 m. For dust hazard class KST up to 250 bar·m/s, a vent panel may be combined with a tubular flame trap or flame-quenching vent system (Figure 6-38). Gas entering at up to 1500 °C leaves below 100 °C, reducing escaping gas volume by about ten times compared with unrestrained venting. An integrated ceramic filter mat retains all solids. This permits venting inside closed rooms; passages past the trap need only shielding against surprise effect.
