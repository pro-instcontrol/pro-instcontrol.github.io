Parallel gate valves

The closure member of a parallel gate valve consists of two parallel discs with a spreading mechanism in between. The force that presses the disc against the seat is controlled by the fluid pressure acting on either a floating disc or a floating seat. In twin-disc parallel gate valves this force may be supplemented with a mechanical force from a spreading mechanism between the discs.

One advantage of parallel gate valves is their low resistance to flow, which in the case of full-bore valves approaches that of a short length of straight pipe. Because the disc slides across the seat face, parallel gate valves are also capable of handling fluids which carry solids in suspension.

This mode of operation also imposes some limitations on the use of parallel gate valves:

  • If fluid pressure is low, the seating force may be insufficient to produce a satisfactory seal between metal-to-metal seatings.
  • Frequent valve operation may lead to excessive wear of the seating faces, depending on the magnitude of fluid pressure, width of the seating faces, lubricity of the fluid to be sealed and the wear resistance of the seating material. For this reason, parallel gate valves are normally used for infrequent valve operation only.
  • Loosely guided discs and loose disc components will tend to rattle violently when shearing high-density, high-velocity flow.
  • Flow control from a circular disc travelling across a circular flow passage becomes satisfactory only between the 50% closed and the fully closed positions. Parallel gate valves are therefore normally used for on-off duty only, though some types have been adapted for flow control, for example by V-porting the seat.

The valves shown in Figures 3-25 to 3-28 are conventional parallel gate valves; those in Figures 3-29 to 3-32 are conduit gate valves. The latter are full-bore valves which differ from the former in that the disc seals the valve body cavity against the ingress of solids in both the open and closed positions. Such valves may therefore be used in pipelines that have to be scraped.

Conventional parallel gate valves

Among the best-known conventional types is the parallel slide gate valve of Figure 3-25. Its closure member consists of two discs with springs in between; the springs keep the upstream and downstream seatings in sliding contact and improve the seating load at low fluid pressures. The discs are carried in a belt eye which prevents their unrestrained spreading as they move into the fully open position. The flow passage is venturi shaped, and the gap between the seats of the fully open valve is bridged by an eyelet to ensure smooth flow.

This construction offers economy, reduced operating effort and lower maintenance cost; the only disadvantage is a slight increase in pressure loss across the valve. The seating stress reaches its maximum when the valve is nearly closed, when the pressure drop is near maximum but only part of the seating area is in contact. As the disc travels from three-quarter closed to nearly closed, the flowing fluid tends to tilt the disc into the seat bore, so heavy wear may occur in the bore and on the outer edge of the disc. The seating width must therefore be appropriately wide — paradoxically small enough for high seating stress yet wide enough to limit wear. The fluid tightness achieved still satisfies the steam-class leakage criterion provided the fluid pressure is not too low.

Parallel slide gate valves have further advantages: the seatings are virtually self-aligning and the seat seal is not impaired by thermal movements of the body. When the valve has been closed cold, thermal extension of the stem cannot overload the seatings. Nor is high accuracy in disc positioning necessary when closing, so an electric drive can be travel limited — an economical and reliable arrangement. For this reason larger power stations often prefer these valves as block valves. They are also used for many other services such as water, in particular boiler feed water, and oil.

A variation used mainly in the United States (Figure 3-26) has two discs with a wedging mechanism between them: on contact with the bottom of the valve body the wedge spreads the discs, and on opening it releases them. The wedge angle must be wide enough for the wedge to be self-releasing, so the supplementary seating load is limited. To prevent premature spreading the valve must be mounted with the stem upright; if it must be mounted stem-down, the wedge needs spring support. Its performance largely matches that of the parallel slide gate valve, but solids and sticky substances in the fluid may interfere with the wedging mechanism and thermal extension of the stem can overload the seatings. It is used mainly in gas, water and oil services.

Conventional parallel gate valves may also be fitted with soft seatings, as in Figure 3-27. The closure member is a disc carrying two spring-loaded floating seating rings, each with a bonded O-ring on the face and a second O-ring on the periphery. On closing, the face O-ring contacts the body seat and produces the initial fluid seal; fluid pressure on the back of the ring then forces the seatings into still closer contact. Because the unbalanced area on the back of the floating rings is smaller than the area of the seat bore, the seating load for a given pressure and valve size is smaller than in the previously described valves. The O-ring nevertheless gives a high degree of tightness even at low fluid pressures, and this sealing principle also permits double block and bleed.

The knife gate valve of Figure 3-28 is designed to handle slurries, including fibrous material. It owes this ability to the knife-edged disc, which cuts through fibres, and to the virtual absence of a valve body cavity. The disc travels in lateral guides and is forced against the seat by lugs at the bottom; an O-ring seat seal may be added when a high degree of fluid tightness is required.

Conduit gate valves

The four types in Figures 3-29 to 3-32 are representative of conduit gate valves. All four are provided with floating seats that are forced against the disc by the fluid pressure.

The seats of the valve in Figure 3-29 are faced with PTFE and sealed peripherally by O-rings. The disc is extended at the bottom to receive a porthole; when the valve is fully open, the porthole engages the valve ports so that the disc seals the body cavity against the ingress of solids. The floating-seat action also permits double block and bleed, and a temporary seat seal can be produced by injecting a sealant into the seat face if the seat seal fails in service.

The valve in Figure 3-30 differs in that the disc consists of two halves with a wedge-shaped interface. The halves are interlinked so that they wedge apart at the fully open or closed positions but relax in the intermediate position to permit travel. Depending on the use, the floating-seat faces may be metallic or provided with a PTFE insert. To prevent ingress of solids into the body cavity during all stages of disc travel, the floating seats have skirts between which the disc travels. This valve likewise permits double block and bleed, and a temporary seat seal can be provided by sealant injection.

The sealing action of the valve in Figure 3-31 depends on a sealant fed to the downstream seat face each time the valve is operated. The floating seats carry reservoirs filled with sealant and topped by a floating piston. The entire valve chamber is filled with a grease which transmits fluid pressure to the top of the downstream piston. The closure member consists of two discs spread apart by springs. Both sealant and body grease can be replenished from outside while the valve is in service; each reservoir filling lasts for more than 100 valve operations. This mode of sealing achieves a high degree of fluid tightness at high fluid pressures.

The valve in Figure 3-32 is designed especially for the hydraulic transport of coal and ore and for heavily dust-laden gases. The gate is a heavy wear-resistant plate with a porthole at the bottom, sliding between two floating seats highly pre-stressed against the disc by disc springs. The seats have skirts for the full disc travel to prevent entry of solids into the body cavity. The disc and seat faces are metallic and highly polished, and the valve chamber is filled with a lubricant that ensures lubrication of the seating faces.

Valve bypass

The seating load of larger parallel gate valves (except those with floating seats) can become so high at high fluid pressures that friction between the seatings makes it difficult to raise the disc from the closed position. Such valves are therefore frequently provided with a valved bypass line used to relieve the seating load before opening. There are no fast rules about when to employ a bypass; the manufacturer's recommendation may be sought, and some gate-valve standards contain recommendations on the minimum bypass size.

In the case of gases and vapours, such as steam, that condense in the cold downstream system, pressurization of the downstream system can be considerably retarded; the bypass line should then be larger than the minimum recommended size.

Pressure-equalizing connection

In the conventional double-seated parallel gate valves of Figures 3-25 and 3-26, thermal expansion of a liquid trapped in the closed valve chamber forces the upstream and downstream discs into more intimate contact with their seats and raises the chamber pressure. The higher seating stress in turn makes the discs harder to raise, and the pressure may quickly become high enough to leak at the bonnet flange joint or deform the valve body. Valves used with liquids of high thermal expansion must therefore have a pressure-equalizing connection linking the chamber to the upstream piping.

Pressure rise may also be caused by revaporation of trapped condensate, as when these valves are closed against steam. Both the chamber and the upstream piping are initially pressurised and filled with steam; eventually the steam cools, condenses and is replaced to some extent by air. On restart, steam enters the upstream piping and, since the upstream seat is not normally fluid-tight against upstream pressure, enters the valve chamber. Some of the new steam condenses initially until the body and piping reach the saturation temperature. The steam then begins to boil off the condensate. Without a pressure-equalizing connection the expanding steam forces the discs into closer contact and raises the chamber pressure. Its magnitude depends on the water temperature and the degree of filling of the chamber with water, and may be obtained from Figure 3-33.

The pressure-equalizing connection may be a hole in the upstream disc or other internal or external means. Some makers of the valve types in Figures 3-25 and 3-26 combine the bypass line with a pressure-equalizing line when the valve is intended for steam.

Duty: stopping and starting flow; infrequent operation.
