Diaphragm valves

Diaphragm valves are flex-body valves in which the valve body consists of a rigid and a flexible section. The flexible section is a diaphragm which, together with a compressor, forms the closure member. The seat is provided by the rigid body section: either a weir across the flow passage, as in Figure 3-93, or the wall of a straight-through passage, as in Figure 3-94.

Diaphragm valves share the pinch valve's advantage: a flow passage not obstructed by moving parts and free of crevices. They may therefore be put to similar uses, including the sanitary handling of foodstuffs and pharmaceuticals.

Weir-type diaphragm valves

The weir is designed to reduce diaphragm flexing to a minimum while keeping a smooth, streamlined passage. Flexing stress is therefore minimal and diaphragm life correspondingly long. The short stroke also permits plastics such as PTFE, which would be too inflexible for longer strokes; the back of such diaphragms is lined with an elastomer to promote uniform seating stress on closing.

Weir-type valves may be used in general and high vacuum service, although some makers require a specially reinforced diaphragm for high vacuum. Because the diaphragm area is large compared with the passage, fluid pressure imposes a high force on the raised diaphragm; the resulting closing torque limits valve size. Weir types like Figure 3-93 are typically made to DN 350 (NPS 14); larger valves up to DN 500 (NPS 20) use the double-bonnet assembly of Figure 3-95.

A T-configuration body is also available, as in Figure 3-96, in which a branch joins the main passage without impeding pipeline flow. Its main function is sampling duty, where a true sample of the flowing fluid must be assured.

The stem of the Figure 3-93 valve is of the rising type. A handwheel shroud protects the external stem thread from dust and outside corrosion, sliding over a yellow lift-indicator sleeve which carries a prepacked lubrication chamber for long thread life. If required, an O-ring seal between stem and bonnet prevents fluid escaping to the surroundings should the diaphragm fail in service.

Conventional weir-type valves may also be used on horizontal lines that must be self-draining. This is achieved by mounting the valve with the stem about 15° to 25° up from the horizontal, provided the line itself has some fall.

Straight-through diaphragm valves

A straight-through passage requires a more flexible diaphragm than a weir type, so diaphragm material is restricted to elastomers. Their high flexibility and large area mean that high vacuum tends to balloon the diaphragm into the passage. The degree of ballooning varies by make, causing either a small acceptable loss of flow area or collapse of the diaphragm; in the latter case the bonnet must be evacuated to balance pressures. Consult the manufacturer for high vacuum service.

Straight-through valves come with full-bore and reduced-bore passages. A reduced-bore valve uses the bonnet assembly of the next smaller size: for example a DN 50 (NPS 2) reduced-bore valve is fitted with a DN 40 (NPS 1½) bonnet. The bonnet is otherwise similar to that of weir-type valves. The size range typically reaches DN 350 (NPS 14).

Construction materials

Diaphragm valve bodies are available in a great variety of materials, including plastics, to meet service requirements. The simple body shape also lends itself readily to lining with many corrosion-resistant materials, often giving a low-cost solution for an otherwise expensive valve. Because the diaphragm separates the bonnet from the flowing fluid, the bonnet is normally cast iron epoxy-coated inside and out; other materials are available on request.

Diaphragms are available in a great variety of elastomers and plastics. Makers' catalogs advise on diaphragm material selection for a given application.

Operational limitations and standards

Like pinch valves, diaphragm valves displace a certain amount of fluid when operated; they are positive-displacement valves. They must therefore not be installed as shut-off valves in lines carrying an incompressible fluid where the fluid has no room to move.

Applications

Duty for both weir and straight-through types: stopping and starting flow; controlling flow.

  • Weir type: gases and liquids that may carry solids, viscous fluids, leak-proof handling of hazardous fluids, sanitary handling of pharmaceuticals and foodstuffs, vacuum.
  • Straight-through: gases and liquids that may carry solids, viscous fluids, sludges, slurries that may carry abrasives, dry media, vacuum (consult manufacturer).

Stainless steel valves

Corrosion-resistant alloys

The least corrosion-resistant alloy is normally steel AISI type 304 (18 Cr, 10 Ni). AISI type 316 (18 Cr, 12 Ni, 2.5 Mo) resists a wider range, and makers often standardize on 316 as the least resistant alloy. Where a valve is welded into the pipeline, low-carbon grades (under 0.3% carbon) are better than stabilized grades. Flanged valves need welding only to repair a casting defect; because the repair is made before the 1100 °C (2000 °F) water-quench solution anneal, standard carbon grades are satisfactory for flanged valves.

Crevice corrosion

Practically all corrosion-resistant alloys are susceptible to crevice corrosion. Good designs therefore avoid threading any component that touches the corrosive fluid; seats are normally an integral part of the body. An exception is a seat clamped between two body halves as in Figure 3-12, but the gaskets must be cleanly cut to avoid crevices.

For screwed-in valves, seal welding improves the joint; thread sealants that harden after application also help. Flanged facings with crevices, such as tongue and groove, should be avoided. Porosity exposed to the corrosive fluid can likewise cause crevice corrosion: the body may corrode through at that point and leak grossly while the rest remains sound.

Galling of valve parts

Stainless steel in sliding contact, particularly austenitic grades of like composition, is susceptible to galling. The tendency falls considerably when the fluid has good lubricity and the seating surfaces retain lubricants and protective contaminants. Polished surfaces retain little and therefore gall more readily, so seating faces of stainless steel valves are usually very finely machined rather than polished.

When high galling is expected, as with dry gases, one seating face may be faced with stellite, which resists many corrosives well. Seizing of the stem in the yoke bush is commonly avoided by choosing dissimilar materials: Ni-Resist ductile iron D2 bushings give freedom from galling thanks to their graphite; if the bush is stainless, free-machining type 303 with a type 304 or 316 stem gives remarkable freedom from galling.

Light-weight valve constructions

U.S. efforts to cut stainless valve cost led to standards for 150 lb light-weight stainless steel valves. Their pressure ratings apply only to valves made from austenitic materials. The flanges are thinner than full-rating carbon steel flanges and have plain flat faces; many users object and request full-rating flanges with a raised face. Light bodies are more flexible, especially important for gate valves where body movement can unseat the disc: plain solid wedges are used only to DN 100 (NPS 4), and larger valves need self-aligning wedges.
