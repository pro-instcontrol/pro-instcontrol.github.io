Pressure Relief Valves II — Pilot-Operated and Operational Behavior

Peter Smith & R. W. Zappe · Valve Selection Handbook, 5th ed. (2004)

Chapter 14 · 14 source pages · 10 figures · companion notes
Key terms. pilot-operated pressure relief valve · main valve · pilot · direct-acting pilot · indirect-acting pilot · dome · pressure sense line · vent · flowing pilot · non-flowing pilot · snap action · modulating action · balanced / unbalanced pilot · full-bore valve · backflow preventer · unloader · blowdown.

Review

Pilot-operated pressure relief valves differ from direct mechanically loaded valves in that the system fluid is the medium for both opening and closing. They consist of a main valve — the actual pressure relief valve — and a self-actuated pilot that controls it as overpressure develops and recedes. The ASME calls the self-actuated form a pilot-operated pressure relief valve; a valve opened by an external power source is a power-operated relief valve.

Two families evolved. The U.S. type uses a pilot that directly controls three passages: the pressure sense line, the main-valve dome and the vent; this is the direct-acting pilot. The European type uses a spring-loaded safety valve as pilot, controlling the main valve by the discharged fluid; this is the indirect-acting pilot (EN 1268-1).

Direct-acting pilot

Figure 5-35 shows the basic layout. The piston is the closing member of the main valve and is controlled by the pilot. Below set pressure, system pressure acts on top of the piston while the vent is closed. Because the piston area facing the dome exceeds the nozzle area, the closing force grows with system pressure. At set pressure the pilot vents enough dome pressure for the pressure under the piston to open the valve; as overpressure recedes to the predetermined closing pressure, the pilot closes the vent and reapplies system pressure in the closing direction.

Pilot types and actions

  • Flowing pilots let system fluid continue bleeding to vent throughout the discharge cycle; non-flowing pilots stop the bleed into the dome when venting is required, reducing the chance of impurities entering the pilot. The non-flowing pilot is generally preferred.
  • On/off pilots admit full system pressure to the dome until set pressure, then vent it so the main valve pops fully open. When pressure falls to blowdown, the pilot reintroduces pressure and the valve drops closed.
  • Modulating pilots open and close in increments with demand. Dome pressure begins to decay at about 95% of set pressure; the valve starts to open at set pressure and may be fully open at about 105% of set pressure, reversing as pressure recedes.
  • Balanced pilots may vent directly to the main-valve outlet as long as back pressure does not exceed inlet pressure. Unbalanced pilots must vent to atmosphere at the pilot or to a preferred location; where outlet pressure can exceed inlet pressure, a backflow preventer is required.

Main valves: diaphragm, piston and flow direction

Main valves are classified by moving member as diaphragm, bellows or piston types. Diaphragm-type main valves are used only on low-pressure service (Figures 5-37, 5-38); if unwanted vacuum develops in the discharge vessel, the diaphragm lifts and protects the vessel from collapse. Figure 5-39 shows a piston-type main valve with a balanced pilot for gas, liquid and two-phase fluids.

Normally flow acts on the underside of the closure member as in Figure 5-35. Figure 5-41 reverses the flow through the main valve so the piston and guide lie in the pressure zone. They then remain at fluid temperature and avoid thermal shock on opening, while solids in the discharge do not strike the seating surface directly. Built-in dampers control piston acceleration and deceleration to limit mechanical shock to the valve and piping.

Standard and full-bore flanged steel main valves

API 526, covering end connections, nozzle sizes and pressure/temperature limits for flanged steel spring-loaded relief valves, has been extended to pilot-operated valves. Many U.S. makers nevertheless offer non-standard full-bore valves whose nozzle approaches the inlet pipe bore (Figure 5-36). Some bodies accept several orifice sizes by adjusting piston stroke with an adjustable length bolt; API 526 sizes D through T are covered. The piston carries a pressure-actuated drag ring to suppress rapid oscillation from resonant reinforcement of the assembly and inlet piping.

Figures 5-40 and 5-41 illustrate non-flowing snap-action pilots used mainly for gas, especially steam, including severe service with dirt, hydrates and high moisture. Figure 5-40 quotes set pressures from 25 to over 6000 psig (1.7 to over 400 barg) and continuous service temperatures from −253 °C to 260 °C. Figure 5-41 gives a maximum set pressure of 1200 psig (82.8 barg), maximum temperature 538 °C, adjustable blowdown, remote actuation and a dual-pilot option with an integral selector.

Accessories

  • Field test connection for in-service verification of set pressure.
  • Field test indicator for in-service verification of set pressure on modulating pilots only.
  • Backflow preventer to stop accidental reverse flow if outlet pressure exceeds inlet pressure.
  • Pilot supply filter, especially appropriate with flowing modulating pilots in liquid service.
  • Pressure spike snubber to attenuate pressure spikes downstream of reciprocating compressors.
  • Manual or remote unloader to open the main valve and depressurize the system.
  • Pilot lift lever for manual testing of valve operation; it does not permit checking set pressure.
  • NACE material option for sour gas service to MR0175, for both pilot and main valve.

Stable operation with on/off pilots

The pressure loss in the inlet pipe between the integral sense pick-up and the source must be less than pilot blowdown. Otherwise the valve recloses and reopens rapidly until it destroys itself or system pressure rises enough to stop cycling. Blowdown should be at least 2% greater than the inlet-pipe loss upstream of the sense point. Modulating pilots overcome this potential for cycling.

Remote sense-line length follows the distance from valve to sensing point, but the line must be routed so condensate cannot block the passage. Non-flowing pilots have been reported with 200 ft (60 m) lines using DN 25 piping to the applicable code. For steam and other condensing services the valve should be as close to the pressure source as possible. An undamped main piston can chatter from a single elbow or equivalent restriction. Full-bore valves may be stabilized with an inlet L/D of 5 and a well-rounded entry; for longer runs, use one larger pipe size with a concentric reducer below the valve.

In less than ideal installations, frictional resistance to piston movement can completely suppress resonant chatter; Figure 5-36 uses a pressure-activated drag device between piston and cylinder.

Stable operation with modulating pilots and service limits

  • Modulating pilots tolerate unfavorable installation better than on/off pilots because the valve opens in proportion to demand.
  • Flowing pilots are not preferred unless the medium is relatively clean, but suit steam because internal warm-up prevents sudden condensation and erratic action.
  • The non-flowing pilot is generally preferred.
  • Pop action is preferred for high rates of pressure rise or when expansion can form solids that plug the discharge header, such as CO₂ dry ice.
  • Modulating action is preferred when required capacity varies greatly, in liquid service, for oversized or modestly undersized valves, or when process phase can vary.
  • Consult the manufacturer if the fluid carries suspended solids or can freeze or condense at ambient temperature.
  • Do not use pilot-operated valves in abrasive or viscous services above 5000 SSU, or where coking, polymerization or corrosion of wetted parts can occur, unless specially designed with a clean external medium protecting the critical internals.

Indirect-acting pilots

Two principal forms exist: main valves that open when deenergized and those that open when energized. Figure 5-42 shows a main valve and a spring-loaded pilot designed for steam. As the pilot opens on rising overpressure, its discharge acts on a check valve that vents the main valve dome. System pressure on the unbalanced piston area opens the main valve, while a small continuous bleed enters the dome. When overpressure recedes the pilot closes, the check valve recloses the dome vent, and the bleed closes the main valve. A perforated cone in the outlet acts as a diffuser-type silencer.

Figure 5-43 shows a typical installation with three control lines: two indirect-acting pilots as in Figure 5-42 and one solenoid control valve. Isolation valves on each side of the pilots and solenoid are interlocked so that one line at a time can be isolated for inspection and maintenance. The number of control lines is chosen by the user or required by the code of practice.

The main valve in Figure 5-44 opens when energized. Its closure member is a cylinder-shaped vessel moving on a central piston; the pilot is a bellows-sealed direct spring-loaded relief valve that may have supplementary loading and solenoid-assisted lift. At set pressure the pilot opens and admits system pressure through a central tube to the top of the piston; pressure build-up opens the valve while a small hole bleeds to the outlet. At blowdown the pilot closes, pressure above the piston bleeds to outlet, and a spring assists sudden closure. In both indirect forms the closure member lies in the system pressure zone, avoiding thermal shock and direct solids impact on seating faces; both are widely used in conventional and nuclear power stations.
