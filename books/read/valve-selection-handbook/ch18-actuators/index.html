Why use an actuator

A manually operated valve relies on plant personnel to supply the energy to open, close or position the closing element. Where manual operation is not an option, an additional device called an actuator is fitted. With supplied energy it can operate the valve by pushbutton or automatically through process systems.

  • Control of the process system.
  • Inaccessibility or remote location.
  • Emergency shutdown or fail-safe requirements.
  • Excessive valve operating torque.
  • Safety.

Actuation methods fall into linear, part-turn and multi-turn groups. Each can be powered hydraulically, electrically or pneumatically. The engineer must ensure enough torque and thrust to move and seat the valve at the minimum available supply, and to hold open, closed or intermediate position under the worst flow conditions. The actuator must also complete 90°, 180° or multi-turn travel at the required speed and include a device that prevents excessive force under adverse supply conditions.

Valve operating forces

Linear valves

Linear valves open and close in reciprocating motion. An electric actuator may rotate a screwed stem, or a pneumatic or hydraulic cylinder may drive a rod directly. The actuator must provide thrust to overcome friction between the gland and the stem or rod entering the body.

  • Solid and flexible wedge gates: closing throttles the flow, raising differential pressure and forcing the gate onto the downstream seat; extra thrust is required.
  • Parallel slide valves: similar friction, but no extra seating allowance because pressure itself forces the slide onto the seat.
  • Screw-down globe valves are usually installed with flow under the disc, resisting closure; high additional thrust is needed for metal-to-metal sealing.
  • Control valves: plug shape, flow rate and differential pressure need separate thought.
  • Diaphragm and pinch valves: lining stiffness affects closing force; an oversized actuator must not rupture the internal membrane.

For diaphragm and pinch valves, closing force depends strongly on lining stiffness. The actuator must close tightly without overloading and rupturing the internal membrane.

Part-turn valves — 90 degrees

Most part-turn valves need only 90° to open or close. The short travel gives a clear power advantage over multi-turn valves.

  • Ball valves: consider the friction coefficient between metal ball and soft or metal seat, plus temperature, pressure, velocity and viscosity. Makers supply torque curves; breakout torque is critical for PTFE, PEEK and metal seats.
  • Butterfly valves: flow velocity strongly affects torque because the closure plate is aerofoil-shaped. Self-locking devices are common because flow reaction can move the plate.
  • Plug valves: lubricated and non-lubricated types differ; without pressure-assisted sealing, opening and closing torques are close. A parallel plug needs less torque than a tapered plug.

Reputable manufacturers give maximum torque under normal service. Extra torque is needed for dry gas, slurry, high velocity, high or low temperature and infrequent use. The maker can also state media consumption for a full stroke in both directions.

Pneumatic actuators

Pneumatic actuators are probably the most common because compressed air is cheap and available. They are generally less expensive than electric or hydraulic alternatives, simpler in design and easier to maintain. They suit part-turn valves but also linear valves; air is easy to store and useful where power capacity is limited or absent.

Supply is usually dry compressed air, though natural-gas designs exist. For a network of actuators, compressor and accumulator design matters. Valve operating time strongly affects supply capacity and tubing size.

Diaphragm types

Diaphragm actuators are usually linear, although rotary designs exist. A flexible plate is clamped between bolted casings forming two airtight chambers. Air into one chamber flexes the diaphragm and extends or retracts the stem; direct acting pushes down, reverse acting pushes up. A spring in the opposite chamber gives fail-safe return to open or closed on air failure.

Diaphragm actuators are cheap, basic and easy to maintain, and suit low thrust in lower pressure classes. Their height can be awkward on small valves in restricted locations, and casings become heavy on large valves.

Piston types and 90-degree mechanisms

A piston actuator has a cylinder and sliding piston sealed by elastomer O-rings. A cast yoke leaves room for positioners and accessories. It is usually double acting with air to both chambers and a positioner that supplies or bleeds air as required. Linear force goes directly to the stem or is converted to rotary force for part-turn valves.

  1. A scotch yoke has a piston, connecting shaft and rotary pin (Figure 8-2).
  2. Rack and pinion uses one or two pistons coupled to a rack driving the pinion (Figure 8-3).
  3. A trunnion/lever actuator is a mounted cylinder whose piston acts directly on a lever attached to the valve shaft.
  4. A cam actuator has double pistons linked by bars with a cam and shaft between them (Figure 8-4).

Vane actuators are common for 90° quarter-turn duty. A plate vane in an airtight chamber is integral with the rotary shaft. Air enters one chamber and exhausts the other, rotating vane and shaft (Figure 8-5). Advantages are low cost, few moving parts and low maintenance; the drawback is very low torque, limiting use to low-pressure service.

Advantages and disadvantages

  • Compressed air is convenient, relatively cheap and safely vented.
  • A piston is not self-locking and can work against a spring for single-shot emergency operation.
  • Air has no kinetic hammerblow to unseat a wedge and can drift because it is compressible.
  • Thermal insulation is needed if the actuator must operate during fire; insufficiently dried air can cause seizure or internal corrosion.
  • Air consumption can be high for large, high-torque valves.

Overall, the pneumatic actuator is perhaps the most widely used because of cost, available spares and the range of construction materials. Protective coatings are also easy to apply.

Electric actuators

Modern electric actuators allow local, remote and hand operation for many valve types and sizes. They comprise a reversible motor, control box, gearbox and limit switches. Main advantages are smooth operation, stability, very high torque and maintained thrust without compressible fluid problems. A three-phase unit with handwheel is shown in Figure 8-1.

Motors for multi-turn and part-turn valves may use single-phase or DC supply, but three-phase is most common for robustness and control. Outdoor motors must prevent internal moisture or condensation. High motor speed needs large gear reduction, with further reduction for part-turn valves.

On motor failure, a handwheel can be engaged by a lever clutch that returns automatically to motor drive when power returns. Electric actuators give a stiff, non-fluctuating stroke in a compact design, but cost more, are more complex, cannot easily store power and need cable protection at high temperature. Equipment in hazardous areas must be intrinsically safe.

Electric drive is excellent where accurate process control matters. Care is needed not to overestimate supply and choose a smaller, cheaper unit. Motors have fewer moving parts, lower maintenance and are generally energy efficient.

Hydraulic actuators

Hydraulic actuators deliver very high torque and fast stroking speed for large valves. The liquid is treated as incompressible, giving a stiff stroke. They are used where pneumatic or electric drives are unsuitable or unavailable.

  • Four basic designs: rack and pinion, hydraulic motor with worm and gear, rotary piston, and piston and cylinder.
  • Double-acting or spring-return types are available; pressure comes from a local power pack or remotely through small-bore tubing.
  • Hydraulic pressure can be varied for different torque outputs, giving more flexibility than electric or pneumatic power. Double-acting output is constant; spring-return types lose energy while compressing the spring.
  • Advantages: high operating forces, stored pressure, stable operation, and a compact body that is economical to coat and insulate.
  • Disadvantages: no hammerblow, costly tubing networks, long runs and pressure drop, close machining tolerances and poor fire survival. High thermal expansion may require a relief device.

Hydraulic and electrohydraulic actuators are robust, reliable and stiff. Well-built units are watertight and suit extremely wet environments such as offshore and subsea wellheads, but they are expensive, may be bulky and need special operating attention.

Sizing actuators for control valves

When sizing a control-valve actuator, the worst case must be considered with the valve open, closed or throttling. Incorrectly sized control valves operate erratically, leading to vibration and wear of the closure device and trim. At full stroke the actuator must overcome breakout torque and stem friction through the sealing components.

Available actuator force must always exceed the force needed to open, close and throttle the process service efficiently.

Actuator specification sheet

To ensure the actuator meets all process requirements and avoid confusion, an engineering specification sheet should be created for each actuated valve. It must contain technical information about both the actuator and the valve body. This aligns the design group and lets procurement place confident requisitions with suppliers.

Spare parts and maintaining actuated valves

Actuators are usually selected for a plant life of 20 to 25 years. A complex device cannot be expected to last that long without periodic maintenance and part replacement. Spare parts should therefore be considered when the actuator is purchased.

  • Commissioning spares: dirt can remain after flushing; keep soft seals and O-rings available.
  • Two-year spares: budgeted while still under warranty; include soft seals, O-rings, glands, closure discs or plugs, and spring sets.
  • Long-term spares: extend the two-year list and consider total replacement of long-lead items because failure is costly.

The maker should supply a manual covering safe operation and maintenance, stating intervals and parts needing lubrication, greasing or adjustment. Major failure within warranty requires work outside the normal maintenance regime by the manufacturer. Even after warranty, the original maker should be the first option considered.

For major failure, buying new equipment is often cheaper because parts and labor approach the purchase price. After repair, other parts are likely to fail soon afterwards.
