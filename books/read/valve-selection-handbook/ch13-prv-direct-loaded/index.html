Pressure Relief Valves I — Direct-Loaded

Peter Smith & R. W. Zappe · Valve Selection Handbook, 5th ed. (2004)

Chapter 13 · 37 source pages · 34 figures · companion notes
Key terms. pressure relief valve · direct-loaded · set pressure · overpressure · blowdown · popping pressure · reseating pressure · huddling chamber · safety valve · safety relief valve · bellows-balanced · superimposed / built-up back pressure · chatter · flutter · simmer · Belleville spring · oscillation damper · force/lift diagram.

Review

Early pressure relief valves were direct-loaded by a weight, with a globe-shaped tapered or flat disc. Escaping fluid imparted little kinetic energy, so lift was very small within allowable overpressure. In 1848 Charles Ritchie exploited gas expansion: a peripheral flow deflector and lip formed an annular chamber with a secondary orifice (Figure 5-1). As the valve opened, gas expanded but could not readily escape, static pressure rose sharply over an enlarged disc area and the valve popped open.

With only about 90° of deflection, part of the kinetic energy became lifting force and the valve opened only partially within normal overpressure. As overpressure receded, chamber pressure rebuilt and the disc huddled above the seat until operating pressure fell below set pressure; the difference is blowdown. Liquids cannot develop the sudden chamber pressure rise, so the valve initially opens only a little.

In 1863 William Naylor's lip turned discharge through 180° (Figure 5-2), giving maximum momentum lift, but lift still depended on rising overpressure. Modern designs combine both principles: a lip forms the annular chamber and secondary orifice while deflecting flow about 180°. Liquid relief valves based on this design now open fully within 10% overpressure.

Early valves used weights because good springs were hard to make and the rising spring load with lift was objected to. Weights soon became impractical; lever mounting helped but could not easily be enclosed against tampering. Spring loading overcame these drawbacks and can be matched to the fluid lifting-force characteristic, so nearly all direct-loaded valves are now spring-loaded except some low-pressure duties.

Safety valves

Safety valves such as Figures 5-3 to 5-6 primarily relieve steam in industrial boiler plants and steam systems. Their design protects the spring from excessive temperature that could drift the setting or relax the spring; an open bonnet lets steam leaking into it escape directly to atmosphere.

Figure 5-4 adds a cooling (lantern) spool between body and bonnet. Figure 5-6 incorporates an eductor formed by channels around the disc; when the valve pops, it evacuates the chamber above the disc to assist sharp opening and closing.

The disc is commonly guided in a sleeve with a screwed ring that changes flow deflection and reaction force under the disc. Lowering the guide ring lengthens blowdown; raising it shortens blowdown. The nozzle ring controls the difference between set and popping pressure: raising it narrows the secondary orifice, popping earlier but adding a little blowdown; lowering it has the reverse effect. The lower ring is factory set and should not be readjusted; the set-to-pop difference should not be eliminated. Figure 5-5 has no nozzle ring — geometry of seat and disc fixes the secondary orifice.

Open-bonnet safety valves are partially balanced by the stem guide and may tolerate about 20% built-up back pressure, subject to the maker. They are unsuitable for superimposed back pressure because of leakage around the stem into the bonnet; they should not be manifolded.

Safety relief valves

Safety relief valves are general-purpose valves for gas or liquid in the process industries. Since process fluids normally cannot be allowed to escape, the bonnet is vented to the outlet or sealed by bellows; process-steam types may have windowed bonnets like safety valves. On gas or vapour they pop open; on liquid they modulate and reach full lift at about 25% overpressure, or 10% in newer designs such as Figures 5-7 and 5-10.

The industry distinguishes conventional and balanced safety relief valves. Conventional types have a closed bonnet vented to the outlet (Figures 5-7, 5-8), so back pressure acts on the whole back of the disc. Before opening, back-pressure forces on the overhanging disc balance, but the area over the nozzle is unbalanced: superimposed back pressure raises set pressure by its own amount, so the bench setting must be lowered. They are therefore unsuitable for variable superimposed back pressure and should not be manifolded.

On opening, built-up back pressure creates a closing force on the unbalanced area, limiting conventional valves to short discharge lines with built-up back pressure around 10% of set pressure; high or adjustable blowdown designs may allow 15% or more. Constant superimposed back pressure can be compensated by spring setting for differential pressure, up to 50%. Figure 5-9 converts a valve to steam duty with an open bonnet, making it partially balanced for about 20% built-up back pressure; an ordinary lifting lever has the same effect.

Balanced valves (Figures 5-10 to 5-12) use a balancing bellows between disc and vented bonnet, exposing the unbalanced area to constant atmosphere. On opening, back pressure only closes the disc overhang; it also elongates and stiffens the bellows, resisting lift. They are rated at 20–40% back pressure and offered up to about 50% with reduced capacity. An auxiliary piston seal (Figure 5-11) guards against bellows failure and vents must lead to a safe place. Not every so-called balanced valve is truly balanced: API 526 valves with D and E orifices may be too small for balancing bellows and remain sensitive to variable back pressure.

Blowdown adjustment is commonly by a nozzle ring as in Figure 5-7, keeping the disc guide out of direct flow impact for solids-bearing fluids and, with balanced bellows, isolating guide and spring from the fluid. Raising the ring lengthens blowdown but lowers popping pressure and operating differential; lowering it shortens blowdown but raises popping pressure and simmer. Makers usually set the ring near the disc holder for stability; Figure 5-12 omits it. Figure 5-8 adjusts blowdown externally by restricting a guide hole, changing back pressure under the disc without moving set pressure; with soft seatings it thermally relieves small amounts of LPG, ammonia or refrigerants where metal-to-metal simmer could cause severe icing.

Liquid relief valves

Liquid relief valves usually differ only slightly in disc geometry. Figure 5-13 opens fully within 10% overpressure, stays stable over a wide range and has nozzle-ring blowdown adjustment. Figure 5-14 is balanced with two stem O-rings: the lower drag ring is energized by inlet pressure through bores in disc and stem and its friction prevents uncontrolled chatter; the upper ring seals against back pressure and a bonnet hole vents leakage.

Figure 5-15 is a proportional valve with a linear opening characteristic for wide load ranges. Its flared nozzle means flow changes cause only small disc movements; tests show internal friction checks oscillation even at rapidly accelerating mass flow. Figure 5-16 shows its opening and closing characteristics. Because liquids are nearly incompressible and dense, small inlet-velocity changes produce large pressure changes that immediately change lift, which in turn affects pressure. Liquid valves are therefore more prone to chatter than gas valves, though friction dampers such as Figure 5-14 can avoid it entirely.

Vacuum relief valves

Figures 5-17 to 5-19 show three direct-loaded vacuum relief valves, combined or combinable with positive overpressure relief. Figure 5-17 relies solely on the light disc weight; seat tightness comes from a sponge-rubber seal and a PTFE-coated guide rod. Its body can be combined with an overpressure valve.

Figure 5-18 combines vacuum and positive pressure relief for sanitary beverage, food and pharmaceutical service. Figure 5-19 is a breather valve carrying separate vacuum and overpressure valves whose discs swing in arcs on point-contact hinges; soft diaphragm seals let them reseat close to set pressure. It serves low-pressure storage tanks.

Valves with auxiliary actuator

The need for tighter seal near set pressure and for chatter suppression in difficult installations led to direct-loaded valves with auxiliary actuators, usually compressed air with electricity. On power failure the valve still operates unhindered as a spring-loaded valve. Three types evolved: assisted (anti-chatter), supplementary-loaded with restricted load (anti-simmer), and unrestricted supplementary loading and/or lift assistance.

In the assisted valve of Figure 5-20 and its piping (Figure 5-21), the assist device is inactive in normal operation, introduces unrestricted supplementary opening force when the valve is called upon to open, then deactivates. Power failure leaves the valve unhindered.

The restricted supplementary-loaded valve (Figures 5-22, 5-23) adds a permitted closing force in normal operation; when opening is called for, the load is removed and the valve behaves as a spring-loaded safety relief valve. If removal fails, the valve still opens fully against the load to deliver rated capacity within permissible overpressure. A mechanical fail-safe disconnects the loading device from the stem as overpressure rises, with a manual release lever. Control-power failure restores ordinary operation.

Figure 5-24 combines a spring valve with pneumatic loading and lift assistance and three identical control lines; interlocked valving lets one line be removed for test or repair without disturbing relief operation. Normally solenoid vents M1–M3 are closed and M4 vents chamber H, while air entering chamber B adds seating load. To open, pressure switches de-energise M1–M3 to atmosphere and energise M4 to admit air to H: B depressurises while H assists lift. The system gives close control and can be arranged for supplementary loading or lift assistance alone.

Figures 5-25 and 5-26 are high-pressure, high-capacity valves whose loading device is a Belleville disc spring stack, combined with an auxiliary pneumatic actuator and a control system like Figure 5-24; they are used in conventional and nuclear power plants.

Oscillation dampers

Oscillation dampers are friction devices acting on the stem. Figures 5-14, 5-27 and 5-28 show three combinations with direct-loaded valves. Figure 5-27 uses dry friction from lens-shaped hard electro-graphite elements packed in a chamber and spring-loaded against the stem, allowing the friction to be set for the application. The material has little dynamic friction scatter and a small static-dynamic difference, so force is fairly uniform over lift; low static friction can be allowed for when setting the valve.

At very small amplitudes or low frequencies, even little dry friction equals a large viscous damping effect, so it effectively prevents chatter without significant disturbance; beyond design limits it may not. Figure 5-28 uses viscous friction: close-fitting concentric cylinders with a narrow grease-filled gap glide relative to each other, producing speed-dependent forces. Force is adjusted by gap width and surface area; viscous damping self-adjusts and can prevent chatter regardless of disturbance size. Both designs were tested and certified by TÜV Germany.

Certification of valve performance

The ASME Boiler and Pressure Vessel Code requires manufacturers to demonstrate performance and capacity by test at an ASME-approved facility; certification is conducted by the National Board of Boiler and Pressure Vessel Inspectors. Methods are in ASME PTC 25-1994 (latest revision). The minimum ASME design pressure is about 15 psi (1.03 bar), so the rules generally do not cover lower pressures. Tests are at 3% or 10% overpressure, minimum 3 psi (0.207 bar), as the applicable code section requires.

  • Three-valve method: capacity certification of one model in one size at one set pressure.
  • Four-valve or slope method: capacity of one valve type in one size at various pressures.
  • Coefficient method: discharge coefficient of a type in various sizes over a pressure range.

All test values must fall within ±5% of the average; multiply by 0.90 for the certified value. For discharge coefficient the average actual value is Kd, the certified value K. The 0.90 derating covers minimal inlet losses, negative deviations up to 5%, wear and unforeseen factors. Capacities and coefficients are published by the National Board in the red book Pressure Relief Device Certifications. Blowdown of non-adjustable valves is recorded. During tests the valve must not flutter or chatter and must reseat properly. Determining permissible back pressure and its effect on flow is outside the performance test.

Force/lift diagrams

Force/lift diagrams such as Figures 5-29 to 5-33 predict the behaviour of spring-loaded valves. The straight dashed line is spring closing force; S-shaped curves are net fluid opening forces determined experimentally at constant inlet pressure. Curve shape varies by valve design.

Figure 5-29 shows rising inlet line loss decaying lift force at zero back pressure with lift restricted to l/d₀ = 0.22; the maker chose a spring characteristic giving full one-stroke opening at 3% inlet loss. Figure 5-30 shows built-up back pressure decaying lift force for an unbalanced conventional valve at zero inlet loss; to include 3% inlet loss the curves must be superimposed. Sufficient back pressure removes the ability to open fully in one stroke.

Figure 5-31 compares blowdown devices. In (a), screwing the guide ring down lengthens the sleeve, raising flow deflection so lift force grows nearly with lift and blowdown lengthens while popping pressure falls slightly. In (b), raising the nozzle ring toward the disc narrows the secondary orifice, making the disc hover longer as overpressure recedes: longer blowdown but higher popping pressure. The nozzle ring in (a) is factory set.

Figure 5-32 describes a valve that pops fully in one stroke (typical). At set pressure (A) it modulates open until B, where lifting and closing forces balance; a further rise makes lift force grow faster and the valve pops fully to C, reaching rated capacity at D. On falling pressure it holds full lift to S, then modulates closed to E. At E, set minus inlet pressure equals inlet-line loss plus reseating blowdown; if this is less than inlet loss the valve cannot close and reopens repeatedly, with acoustic interaction aggravating chatter until pressure drops or the valve destroys itself. The original also shows the corresponding lift/pressure diagram.

Figure 5-33 shows valves that pop only partly then modulate to full lift, a common characteristic based on API RP 520 Part I (1993) Figure 22. The difference between lift force at D and closing force at S measures back-pressure tolerance; under identical conditions the Figure 5-32 valve tolerates more back pressure. Tolerance can be improved by a less steep spring characteristic or a larger orifice with restricted lift; larger inlet and discharge piping also lower inlet loss and built-up back pressure.

Secondary back pressure and inlet loss

Secondary back pressure develops between seat and outlet flange and, for sonic outlet flow, is intrinsic to the valve. It decays lift force like outlet-pipe back pressure and may prevent rated capacity. It relates to the outlet-to-nozzle area ratio of API Std 526: the ratio for a 1½D2 valve is about 14 times that of an 8T10. An 8T10 steam valve has been reported not to reach capacity above 50 psig (3.5 barg) even without outlet piping; an 8T12 should be used. Use caution with low area ratios and consult the maker in doubt.

The accepted recommendation is that non-recoverable pressure loss between protected equipment and a direct-acting relief valve inlet should not exceed 3% of set pressure. On a process line the limit includes loss in the normally non-flowing inlet and the incremental process-line loss caused by relief flow, calculated at the 10% overpressure rating. Staying below 3% grows harder as inlet-to-nozzle area ratio falls; for a full-lift liquid valve with AE/AN = 0.9 the permitted resistance coefficient is only about 0.5, equal to a sharp-edged inlet. Use a larger inlet pipe or a valve with an oscillation damper after consulting the maker.

A grossly oversized pop valve is also unstable: if flow is below about 25% of valve capacity the discharge cannot hold it open and it cycles. Frequency depends on system volume and blowdown setting; limiting lift in consultation with the maker eliminates the instability.

Verification before and after installation

Before installation, verification is commonly only a bench set-pressure test; on the bench the set point is readily seen as first steady flow or lift. Only in-situ testing captures plant-specific effects. Plant noise interferes with audible detection, so popping pressure is often taken as set pressure. This matters little for high-pressure valves (opening difference often 1–2%), but low-pressure valves may pop up to 5% above true set pressure. This deviation must be allowed for when correlating operating, reseating and blowdown pressures; assessing behaviour may require raising plant pressure by another 5–10%.

Portable devices such as Figure 5-34 avoid overpressurising the plant by applying a measurable lifting force to the stem mechanically, hydraulically or pneumatically, without controlling system pressure. Tests last seconds and verify set pressure, popping pressure, blowdown after reseating, valve lift and spring rate. A laptop with software and printer delivers a pressure/lift diagram for full performance evaluation. Set pressure can be verified at any system pressure and on the bench; the other data require at least 80% of set pressure.
