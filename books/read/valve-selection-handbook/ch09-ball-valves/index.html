Ball valves

Ball valves are a species of plug valves with a ball-shaped closure member. The seat matching the ball is circular, so the seating stress is circumferentially uniform. Most ball valves also have soft seats that conform readily to the ball surface. From the sealing point of view the concept is excellent; the valves in Figures 3-60 to 3-68 are typical of those available.

The flow-control characteristic from a round port moving across a circular seat and the double pressure drop across two seats is very good. If the valve is left partially open for long under a high pressure drop, however, the soft seat tends to flow around the edge of the ball orifice and may lock the ball in position. Manually operated ball valves are therefore best suited to stopping and starting flow and moderate throttling; with automatic control the ball is continuously moving and this failure does not normally occur.

Because the ball moves across the seats with a wiping motion, ball valves handle fluids with solids in suspension; abrasive solids damage the seats and ball surface, and long tough fibrous material may wrap around the ball. To economise, most ball valves have a reduced bore with a venturi-shaped passage about three-quarters of nominal size. The pressure drop is so small that full-bore valves are normally not justified, except when the pipeline must be scraped.

Seat materials

The most important seat material is PTFE: inert to almost all chemicals, low friction, wide temperature range and excellent sealing. It also has a high expansion coefficient, susceptibility to cold flow and poor heat transfer, so the seat must be designed around these properties. Other plastics include filled PTFE, nylon and many more; as the material becomes harder, sealing reliability tends to suffer, particularly at low pressure differentials. Elastomers such as buna-N are also used but restrict fluid compatibility and temperature range and tend to grip the ball unless the fluid has sufficient lubricity. For services unsuitable for soft seatings, metal and ceramic seatings are used.

Seating designs

Intimate contact between the seatings may be achieved in a number of ways; four frequently used ones are:

  • Fluid pressure forces a floating ball against the seat — Figures 3-60 to 3-63.
  • Fluid pressure forces a floating seat ring against a trunnion-supported ball — Figure 3-64.
  • Mainly installed prestress between the seats and a trunnion-supported ball — Figure 3-65.
  • A mechanical force applied to ball and seat on closing — Figure 3-66.

Ball valves are also available in which the seal is achieved by a squeeze ring such as an O-ring. The first method, with seating load regulated by fluid pressure on the ball, is the most common; the permissible pressure is limited by the downstream seat ring's ability to withstand fluid loading at working temperature without permanent gross deformation.

Torsion seats and floating-ball variants

The seat rings of Figures 3-60 and 3-61 have a cantilevered lip: the ball initially contacts only the tip. As the upstream and downstream seats are pre-stressed against the ball on assembly, the lips deflect and put the rings into torsion. When closing against line pressure the downstream lip deflects further until the whole seat surface matches the ball. The seats thereby have spring action promoting good sealing even at low pressure, and their resilient construction keeps them from being crushed at high fluid loads.

The seat rings of Figure 3-60 also have peripheral pressure-equalizing slots: upstream pressure filters past the upstream seat into the body cavity, balancing that seat and reducing its contribution to total valve torque. Figure 3-61 uses an axial-entry body.

Figures 3-62 and 3-63 also ensure preload between the seatings. In Figure 3-62 the seat rings are supported on metal diaphragms acting as springs behind them, in a sandwich-split body. In Figure 3-63 a spring forces the ball and seat-ring assembly from the top into wedge-shaped seat back faces in a one-piece top-entry body.

Trunnion-supported balls

Figure 3-64 uses the second method: fluid pressure forces a floating seat ring against a trunnion-supported ball, the ring being sealed peripherally by an O-ring. Because the pressure-uncompensated area is small, seating load for a given rating can be regulated to the bearing capacity of the seat; the valve serves high pressures beyond the floating-ball range. A device rotates the seat rings slightly on each operation to distribute wear evenly, and a temporary seal can be made by injecting sealant if the seat fails.

Figure 3-65 uses the third method: the seal depends mainly on installed prestress between seats and trunnion ball, limiting torque at high pressures. Lips around the ball ports are radiused to reduce seating interference when open; interference increases as the ball moves closed. For double block and bleed the back of the seat rings must carry an elastomeric O-ring.

The fourth method applies mechanical force on closing to avoid most sliding between seatings. Figure 3-66 uses a cam mechanism that lifts the ball out of the seat before opening and forces it back after closing.

Pressure-equalizing connection

Double-seated ball valves may have a sealed body cavity in both open and closed positions: between the seats when closed, and possibly between ball and body when open. Liquid of high thermal expansion in these cavities may overstress components unless excess pressure is relieved. The cavity between ball and body is normally relieved to the flow passage through a hole in the top or bottom flank of the ball.

When closed, excess pressure between the seats is relieved in various ways. With floating seats as in Figure 3-64, excess body pressure opens the upstream seat seal where the pressure differential is least. In other double-seated valves the fluid pressure must overcome prestress between ball and upstream seat. Springing seat rings as in Figures 3-60 to 3-62 may let the upstream seal open without excessive pressure; more rigid rings may allow thermal expansion to create excessive pressure, and the upstream flank of the ball is then usually provided with a pressure-equalizing hole, permitting flow in one direction only. If the catalogue is silent, consult the maker. Such a connection is not normally standard except for cryogenic service, where plastics become rigid and resist opening.

Antistatic device

Polymeric seats and packings can insulate the ball and stem from the body, so ball valves may need an antistatic device for the reasons given for plug valves on page 98. Figure 3-67 shows a typical device of spring-loaded plungers: one between the stem tongue and the ball, a second between the stem and the body.

Ball valves for fire exposure

Soft seat and stem seals disintegrate under sufficiently long fire exposure. Valves for flammable fluids must be designed so that loss of the soft seals in an external fire does not cause gross internal or external leakage. Such designs provide emergency seat and stem seals that operate after the primary seals fail: a sharp-edged or chamfered secondary metal seat close to the ball, against which the ball floats after the soft rings disintegrate; the stuffing box may have auxiliary asbestos-based or pure graphite packing, or be made entirely of such material.

Numerous standards cover fire testing of soft-seated ball valves; the three basic ones are BS 5146, API 607 and API RP 6F. BS 5146 derives from OCMA FA VI, itself taken from an Esso Petroleum UK test specification. That test differed from earlier ones by requiring the valve to be open during the test with a flammable liquid in it, recognising that in a real plant fire many valves are open and must subsequently be closed. Several standards bodies are moving toward an international fire-test specification.

Attention must also be paid to the effect of fire on the whole fluid system — other valves, operators, pumps, filters, pressure vessels and especially pipe flanges, bolting and gaskets. Fire-tested valves are referred to as fire-safe, a term unacceptable to manufacturers from a product-liability standpoint.

Multiport configuration

Ball valves adapt to multiport configurations in a manner similar to plug valves discussed on page 98.

Ball valves for cryogenic service

Ball valves are used extensively in cryogenic services but their design must be adapted. A main consideration is the thermal contraction coefficient of the seat ring, normally higher than that of the stainless ball and body: the ring shrinks onto the ball, operating torque increases and in severe cases the ring may split. Installed prestress may be reduced to give the correct value at cryogenic temperature, though tightness at low pressure may then suffer at ambient temperature. Other means include flexible metal diaphragms, seat materials with much lower contraction such as graphite- or carbon-filled PTFE, or stainless rings with minimum PTFE inserts.

Because plastic seat materials become rigid at cryogenic temperature, surface finish and ball sphericity must be of a high standard for tight sealing. As with other cryogenic valves, the extended bonnet should be no more than 45° from upright for an effective stem seal.

Variations of body construction

Access to ball valve internals can be provided in various ways, leading to the body constructions of Figure 3-68. A one-piece body has the fewest joints subject to pipeline stress and is often chosen for hazardous fluids; the sealed variant is frequent for buried service. One-piece top-entry and split bodies give easy internal access; welded-in-line valves with top entry can be serviced in situ. Selection is often a matter of personal preference.

Face-to-face dimensions

U.S. manufacturers originally made flanged ball valve face-to-face dimensions to the nearest valve standard for minimum material — the gate valve standard — but class 150 and class 300 sizes DN 200 (NPS 8) to DN 300 (NPS 12) permitted only reduced-bore construction. In 1961 UK makers introduced flanged ball valves with a demand for full-bore types; when these would not fit gate-valve dimensions, regular-pattern plug-valve dimensions were adopted. Thus short and long series exist for class 150 and for DN 200–300 class 300 — one for reduced-bore, one for full-bore valves. At higher ratings gate-valve dimensions accommodate both.

The master standard for face-to-face dimensions is ISO 5752, covering all recognised dimensions worldwide; it does not define reduced or full bore except for class 300 sizes DN 200–300. Appendix C lists U.S. and British standards pertaining to ball valves.

Applications

Duty: stopping and starting flow; moderate throttling; flow diversion.

Service: gases; liquids; non-abrasive slurries; vacuum; cryogenic.
