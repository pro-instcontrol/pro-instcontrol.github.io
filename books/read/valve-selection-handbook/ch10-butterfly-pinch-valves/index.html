Butterfly valves

Butterfly valves are rotary valves in which a disc-shaped closure member is rotated through approximately 90° to open or close the flow passage. The valves in Figures 3-69 to 3-83 represent a cross section of the many variations available. The original butterfly valve was the simple pipeline damper, not intended for tight shut-off; it remains an important member of the butterfly family.

The advent of elastomers initiated rapid development of tight shut-off butterfly valves in which the elastomer seals between the disc rim and the body; their original use was for water. As more chemical-resistant elastomers appeared, butterfly valves spread through the process industries. Such elastomers must resist corrosion and abrasion, remain dimensionally stable and retain resiliency — they must not harden. A missing property may make the elastomer unsuitable, and manufacturers should advise on selection and limits.

Efforts to overcome elastomer limitations led to PTFE-seated and metal-seated tight shut-off butterfly valves, giving the family a wide pressure and temperature range through several sealing principles. Butterfly valves offer little resistance when fully open and sensitive flow control between about 15° and 70°. Severe liquid throttling may cause cavitation depending on vapour pressure and downstream pressure; this can be combated by sizing the valve smaller than the line so throttling occurs near half-open, and/or by taking the pressure drop in steps as discussed in Chapter 2. Closing too quickly in liquid service may cause excessive waterhammer, avoided by closing slowly.

Because the disc moves into the seat with a wiping motion, most butterfly valves handle fluids with solids in suspension and, depending on seat robustness, powders and granules. In horizontal pipelines the stem should be horizontal, and on opening the bottom of the disc should lift away from solids accumulated upstream.

Leakage classes and seating designs

By seat tightness butterfly valves fall into nominal-leakage, low-leakage and tight shut-off classes. The first two are used mainly for throttling or flow control; tight shut-off valves may be used for tight shut-off, throttling or flow control. Figure 3-69 shows nominal- and low-leakage valves with metallic seats and discs; where lower leakage is required, a piston ring on the disc rim is available.

Intimate contact between the seatings of tight shut-off butterfly valves is achieved in several ways:

  • Interference seating: the disc is jammed into the seat.
  • Force the disc against the seat about a double offset hinge, comparable to a tilting-disc check valve (page 154).
  • Pressure-energized sealing with O-rings, lip seals, diaphragms or inflatable hoses.

Most butterfly valves are interference-seated with a rubber liner, as in Figures 3-70 to 3-72. Where rubber is incompatible, a PTFE liner is backed by an elastomer cushion for resiliency. Common liner constructions are an unbonded U-ring slipped over the body and readily replaceable (Figure 3-70); rubber bonded to the body, limiting bunching as the disc rotates but not replaceable; and a U-liner bonded to a metal band for insertion into a split body (Figure 3-72). With a rigid material such as PTFE the body is split on the centreline so the liner can be inserted without manipulation.

To achieve full sealing capacity, rubber-lined valves must be installed correctly. The liner must be fully supported by weld-neck rather than slip-on flanges: with slip-on flanges the liner is unsupported between the valve bore and pipe outside diameter, tending to distort in operation and causing early wear and seat leakage. Put the disc near-closed while handling to protect its rim, then rotate it fully open before tightening flange bolts so the liner finds its undisturbed position. The liner flange already seals against the pipeline flange; extra rubber gaskets reduce liner support and sealing capacity.

Figure 3-74 shows the resilient element carried on the disc rim of an interference-seated valve: a heavy-section O-ring with a tail clamped to the disc. Adjusting the clamping force adjusts interference within limits. Because the element deforms against a wide face rather than the narrow disc edge, seating and unseating torques are lower; this make is produced only in larger sizes for relatively high fluid pressures.

Figures 3-75 and 3-76 rely on pressure-energized rubber. In Figure 3-75 an inflatable hose on the disc rim is internally reinforced by a metallic conduit and connected through the shaft to upstream or external pressure. The disc enters the tapered seat with the hose deflated for minimum seating torque, then the hose is pressurised for a fluid-tight seal, optionally topped up by hand pump; before opening it is deflated again for minimum unseating torque. The valve is made to the largest sizes in use. Figure 3-76 mounts a tubular diaphragm of T-cross-section in a body slot, pressurised on closing and depressurised on opening in the same manner.

High-performance butterfly valves

Extending temperature and pressure range produced a family of butterfly valves with metal-to-polymer or metal-to-metal seatings, including fire-tested versions, generally suitable for flow in both directions. The name denotes a wider pressure-temperature envelope than ordinary elastomer-lined valves. Figures 3-77 to 3-83 are examples; the drawings do not show every seal variation available.

Figure 3-77 forces the disc against the seat about a double offset hinge, comparable to a tilting-disc check valve. The disc is a slice from a tapered plug cut obliquely to the plug axis, giving tapered faces on an elliptical circumference. Its axis lies below the valve centreline and behind the seat face, so the disc drops into and lifts out of the seat with little rubbing. Seating load comes from applied closing torque plus hydrostatic torque on the unbalanced disc area. Metal-to-metal seatings or soft elements sealing against a metal body seat are available; the construction serves up to 35 bar and −200…650 °C.

Figure 3-78 resembles a ball valve using a wafer section: a U-shaped plastic seal ring sits in a body T-slot and is backed by an elastomeric O-ring. The O-ring gives initial compression; as the disc enters with slight interference, fluid pressure on the O-ring forces the ring closer to the disc rim. Several seat-ring constructions, including metal, suit different conditions.

Figure 3-79 also borrows from the ball valve, but the disc axis is offset not only from the seat plane but also slightly from the valve centreline. The disc cams progressively into contact and disengages quickly on opening, minimising rubbing. The seat is a PTFE lip with a tail clamped between body and retainer; once the disc enters with slight interference, fluid pressure forces the lip onto the disc rim whether the seat is upstream or downstream. Bidirectional sealing is aided by a small controlled axial movement of the disc. Metal and fire-tested seat constructions are available.

The double offset axis with bidirectional pressure-energized sealing also applies to Figures 3-80 to 3-82. Figure 3-80 uses a modified PTFE lip supported by a titanium ring; Figure 3-81 uses a PTFE-encapsulated elastomeric O-ring anchored in the body — the O-ring supplies elasticity while the PTFE envelope protects it from the fluid. Both have fire-tested versions.

Figure 3-82 combines the seat with a flexible membrane that returns it to its original position after each operation. One version embeds a pliant membrane in a T-shaped PTFE member; a fire-tested version uses a U-shaped metal member with a PTFE insert carried on a metal membrane. The PTFE insert seals under normal conditions; if fire destroys it, the metal seat takes over.

Unlike the preceding valves, Figure 3-83 relies on interference seating with a double offset disc. The seat may be plastic, elastomer, metal or a fire-tested plastic/metal composition for flammable liquids. Plastic and metal seats use a back-up wire winding that gives absolute rigidity when closed — achieving interference for tight shut-off at high and low pressures including vacuum — while leaving the seat unloaded and free to flex radially when open.

Butterfly valves for fire exposure

Butterfly valves handling flammable fluids that may be exposed to a plant fire must remain essentially fluid-tight internally and externally and be operable during and after the fire. Fire-tested versions of high-performance butterfly valves meet these conditions; test and evaluation requirements are similar to those for ball valves on page 108.

Body configurations

The preferred butterfly body is the wafer clamped between two pipe flanges: the bolts pulling the flanges together carry line-strain tension and place the wafer in compression, eased by tensile stress from internal pressure. A flanged body must carry all line-strain tension, with pressure tension cumulative. Since most metals accept compressive loads up to about twice their tensile limit, the wafer body is strongly favoured. If the downstream side is also a disconnection point while upstream remains pressurised, a cross-bolted wafer is unsuitable unless fitted with a false flange; a flanged body or lugged wafer with threaded lugs receiving screws from adjacent flanges is then used.

Torque characteristics

Operating torque has three components: Th, hydrodynamic torque from flow acting on the disc; Tb, bearing-friction torque; and Ts, torque to seat or unseat the disc. Hydrodynamic torque varies with opening and pressure drop. For a symmetrical disc it is the same for either flow direction and always opposes opening. For an offset disc as in Figure 3-84 it differs by direction and is lowest with flow toward the disc: flow toward the shaft opposes opening throughout, whereas flow toward the disc opposes it initially then reverses as opening increases. Bearing, seating and unseating torques always oppose motion; bearing torque follows the resultant hydrodynamic force, while seating and unseating torque is independent of flow.

For interference-seated valves up to DN 400 (NPS 16) at moderate velocity, seating and unseating torques normally dominate and suffice for sizing the gear. Their magnitude depends on fluid and operating frequency: good lubricity and frequent operation give lower torque than poor lubricity, solids or infrequent operation; manufacturers supply tables. Above DN 400 or at high velocity, hydrodynamic and bearing torques can greatly exceed seating torque, so the operator should be selected with the manufacturer.

Figure 3-85 summarises opening torque at constant pressure drop for symmetrical and offset discs. In most real installations pressure drop falls as the valve opens (Figure 3-86), reducing the corresponding torque; the maximum therefore shifts from near fully open to partly open (Figure 3-87). Torque is lowest for an offset disc with flow toward the disc, at the penalty of a somewhat higher pressure drop. For large valves at high velocity this characteristic can reduce operator cost.

Pinch valves

Pinch valves are flex-body valves consisting of a flexible tube pinched mechanically (Figures 3-88 to 3-90) or by fluid pressure applied outside the body (Figures 3-91 and 3-92). A principal advantage is a straight, crevice-free passage with no moving parts; the soft body can also seal around trapped solids. Pinch valves thus suit slurries and solids that would clog obstructed passages, and sanitary food and pharmaceutical handling. Depending on body material they also handle severely abrasive fluids and most corrosive fluids.

Open and enclosed pinch valves

Mechanically pinched valves may be open so the body can be inspected visually and physically in service; bulges and soft spots signal replacement, as in Figure 3-88. If escape from an accidentally ruptured body cannot be tolerated, the body must be encased, normally split along the flow axis for access although unit casings also exist. Figure 3-89 adds a 50% prepinched sleeve and removable drain plug for vacuum connection; Figure 3-90 uses a PTFE body with tear drops.

Flow control

Pinch valves give little flow control between fully open and 50% pinched because pressure drop there is negligible; further closing gives good control. Mechanically pinched control valves are therefore often 50% prepinched. With severely erosive fluid, avoid control near the closed position to prevent grooving of the body, and always close fluid-tight so leakage flow cannot groove it.

Fluid-pressure operated pinch valves are not normally suitable for manual flow control because any change in downstream pressure resets the valve position. Otherwise the characteristic of Figure 3-91 is similar to that of mechanically pinched valves.

The exception in Figure 3-92 closes iris-like: the rubber muscle exerts literally a 360° squeeze, giving an always round, always centred hole and equal-percentage control throughout. The iris lets larger particles pass in any position than any other valve, reducing slurry bridging when partly closed; the circular throttling orifice also reduces body grooving under severe abrasive throttling. The drawback is that it cannot close fully.

Body material and limitations

The body is made of elastomer, PTFE or similar materials, optionally fabric-reinforced. Natural rubber best resists flex cracking and also resists abrasive wear and corrosion by many fluids. With chemically active fluids, highly stressed rubber is attacked more readily; corrosion products may form a thin film far less flexible than the parent rubber, cracking on severe flexing and exposing fresh rubber until cracking goes through. Some synthetic rubbers resist this attack better and extend corrosive service; compounders seek corrosion resistance together with high tensile strength, softness for abrasion resistance and easy closure. To reduce flexing, the body inside may have opposing groove-like recesses or tear drops as in Figure 3-90 — a design that permits an all-PTFE sleeve.

The flexible body imposes limitations, sometimes overcome by special body designs or methods of operation:

  • Fluid-pressure operated pinch valves tend to collapse on suction duty; for mechanical pinch valves the body must be positively attached to the operating mechanism.
  • Pinch valves downstream of a pump should be opened before starting the pump. If closed, trapped air compresses and escapes rapidly when the valve cracks open; the following liquid column then strikes the body heavily, perhaps bursting it.
  • Pulsating flow makes the body pant and eventually fail by fatigue.
  • As the main shut-off in a system where liquid is locked with no room to move, the valve cannot close because it must displace liquid; forcing it may burst the body.

No national standards apply specifically to pinch valves.
