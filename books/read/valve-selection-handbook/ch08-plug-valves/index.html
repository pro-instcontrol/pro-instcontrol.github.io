Plug valves

Plug valves are rotary valves in which a plug-shaped closure member is rotated through increments of 90° to engage or disengage a porthole or holes in the plug with the ports in the valve body. The plug may be cylindrical, as in Figures 3-49 to 3-53, or tapered, as in Figures 3-54 to 3-58. Rotary valves with ball-shaped plugs are likewise members of the plug valve family but are conventionally referred to as ball valves and are discussed separately on page 108.

The port is commonly rectangular in parallel plugs and truncated triangular in taper plugs. These shapes permit a slimmer, lighter valve at the expense of some pressure drop. Full-area round-bore ports are normally used only if the pipeline has to be scraped or the nature of the fluid demands them; some plug valves are made only with round bores because of the method of sealing employed.

Plug valves are best suited to stopping and starting flow and flow diversion, though occasionally used for moderate throttling depending on the service and the erosion resistance of the seatings. Because the seatings move against each other with a wiping motion and are fully protected from the flowing fluid when open, plug valves are generally capable of handling fluids with solids in suspension.

Cylindrical plug valves

The use of plug valves depends in part on how the seal between plug and body is produced. For cylindrical plug valves four methods are frequently employed: a sealing compound, expanding the plug, O-rings, and wedging an eccentrically shaped plug into the seat.

The valve of Figure 3-49 is lubricated: the seat seal depends on a sealing compound between plug and body, introduced through the plug shank by a screw or injection gun. A defective seat seal can therefore be restored by injecting more compound while the valve is in service. With seating surfaces protected when fully open and damage easily repaired, lubricated plug valves are particularly suited to abrasive fluids. They are not intended for throttling, though sometimes used for it: throttling removes the compound from exposed surfaces, so the seal must be restored each time the valve closes.

Manual maintenance of the sealing compound is often a human problem; automatic injection overcomes it but adds installation cost. When the plug becomes immovable through lack of maintenance, wrong compound selection or crystallisation between the seatings, the valve must be cleaned or repaired.

In the valves of Figures 3-50 and 3-51 the seat seal depends on the plug expanding against the seat. The plug in Figure 3-50 has a packing sleeve tightened against the seat by a follower nut; the packing is usually compressed asbestos or solid PTFE. If retightened to restore tightness, this must be done with the valve closed so the packing does not expand into the flow passage. Made only in small sizes, the valve serves fairly high pressures and temperatures, typically isolating pressure gauges and level gauges.

The plug of Figure 3-51 is split into two halves spread apart by a wedge adjustable from outside. Narrow PTFE rings inserted in the plug faces provide the seal; this method of sealing and seat loading also permits double block and bleed. The valve is intended for duties requiring stainless steel and other expensive alloys, and handles slurries but not abrasive solids; very tacky substances also render the wedging mechanism inoperable. Within these limits it has proved very reliable under frequent operation.

Figure 3-52 shows a parallel plug valve whose O-rings are mounted on spigot-like projections of the upstream and downstream ports, forming the seats for the plug. On closing, fluid pressure passes the upstream O-ring into the cavity between plug and body and forces the downstream O-ring into intimate contact with the plug and port projections. Its main application is high-pressure hydraulic systems.

Efforts to eliminate most seating friction and to control sealing capacity by applied torque led to the eccentric plug valve of Figure 3-53, in which an eccentric plug matches a raised face in the body. This method of seating is easy to operate and handles sticky substances difficult for other valve types. The seating faces may be in metallic contact or lined with an elastomer or plastic together with the body lining.

Taper plug valves

Taper plug valves permit the leakage gap between seatings to be adjusted by forcing the plug deeper into the seat. Depending on design, the plug may rotate while in intimate contact with the body, or be lifted out before rotation and reseated after rotating through 90°.

Figure 3-54 shows a taper plug valve with unlubricated metal seatings. Because friction is high, the permissible seating load is limited to keep the plug freely movable; the leakage gap is therefore relatively wide and the valve seals satisfactorily only with liquids of high surface tension or viscosity. If the plug is grease-coated before installation, the valve may also be used for wet gases such as wet and oily compressed air.

The lubricated taper plug valve of Figure 3-55 resembles the lubricated cylindrical type in duty, differing only in plug shape, but has one operational advantage: if the plug becomes immovable after a long static period or neglected lubrication, injecting more sealing compound can lift it just enough to move again. The gland should then not be slackened as users sometimes do, but rather judiciously tightened. Conversely, manual overtightening can seize the plug.

In the valve of Figure 3-56 the lubricated taper plug is mounted inverted and divorced from the stem. Its position is adjusted by a screw in the valve cover while sealant is injected into the body at the stem end. To prevent fluid pressure driving the plug into the seat, the plug ends have balance holes admitting pressure to both end cavities. The valve may therefore be used at very high pressures without becoming inoperable.

Efforts to overcome lubricated-valve maintenance led to the taper plug valve of Figure 3-57, whose plug moves in a PTFE body sleeve. The sleeve keeps the plug from sticking, though torque can still be relatively high because of the large seating area and stress. Conversely, the large area gives good protection against leakage after local damage; the valve is rugged, tolerates abusive treatment, and the sleeve permits exotic materials that would otherwise bind. It is easily repaired in the field and requires no lapping.

The valve of Figure 3-58 eliminates most sliding between seatings by a hinged lever arrangement that lifts the plug out of the body seat before rotation and reseats it afterwards. The plug may be rubber faced and is normally self-locking by its taper. It is a multiport companion valve to the eccentric plug valve of Figure 3-53.

Antistatic device

In plug valves, polymeric seats and packings such as PTFE can electrically insulate the plug and stem from the body. Friction from the flowing fluid may then generate an electrostatic charge high enough to produce an incendiary spark, more so with two-phase flow. For flammable fluids the valve must have an antistatic device giving electrical continuity between plug, stem and body.

Plug valves for fire exposure

Plug valves exposed to a plant fire while handling flammable fluid must remain essentially tight internally and externally and be operable during and after the fire. This is especially difficult when the valve relies on polymers for seat and stem seals. Common practice is an auxiliary metal seat close to the plug, against which the plug can float after the soft seat disintegrates; soft stem packing is replaced by fire-resistant packing. Test and evaluation requirements are similar to those for ball valves on pages 108–109.

Multiport configuration

Plug valves adapt readily to multiport configurations such as Figure 3-59. They may be designed for transflow, where the second passage opens before the first closes, or non-transflow, where the first closes before the second opens. Transflow suits duties in which flow cannot be momentarily interrupted, for example the outlet of a positive-displacement pump without a relief valve. Non-transflow may be required when momentary flow from one port to the other is not permissible, for example at the outlet of a measuring vessel; the plug is not normally intended to shut off fluid-tight in the intermediate position. In most practical applications with fairly quick operation there is little difference between the two.

The direction of flow through lubricated multiport valves should be such that fluid pressure forces the plug against the port to be shut off. If pressure acts from the opposite direction, lubricated plug valves will not hold their rated pressure.

Face-to-face dimensions and patterns

Designers of U.S. and British standards for cast-iron and carbon-steel plug valves tried to make face-to-face dimensions identical to those of gate valves. To fit plug valves within those dimensions, concessions had to be made on flow area at the lower pressure ratings. Even so, class 150 plug valves are interchangeable with gate valves only up to DN 300 (NPS 12), which led to an additional long-series class 150 valve. The following patterns emerged:

  • Short pattern — reduced-area plug ports and face-to-face dimensions interchangeable with gate valves; sizes up to DN 300 (NPS 12) in class 150 only.
  • Regular pattern — ports larger than short or venturi patterns; face-to-face interchangeable with gate valves for class 300 and higher; class 150 uses a long series not interchangeable with gate valves.
  • Venturi pattern — reduced-area ports and approximately venturi body throats; interchangeable with gate valves for class 300 and higher; class 150 uses a long series.
  • Round-port full-area pattern — full-area round ports; face-to-face longer than short, regular or venturi patterns and not interchangeable with gate valves.

Applications

Duty: stopping and starting flow; moderate throttling; flow diversion.

Fluids: gases; liquids; non-abrasive slurries; abrasive slurries for lubricated plug valves; sticky fluids for eccentric and lift plug valves; sanitary handling of pharmaceuticals and foodstuffs; vacuum.
