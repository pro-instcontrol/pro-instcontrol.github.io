Functions of manual valves

A manual valve is a valve operated by plant personnel directly, by the use of either a handwheel/wrench or an on/off actuator in the case of shutdown valves. Certain automated valves with actuators are also supplied with handwheels to allow manual operation in the event of power failure.

Manual valves serve three major functions in fluid-handling systems: stopping and starting flow, controlling flow rate, and diverting flow. Valves for stopping and starting flow are frequently employed also for controlling flow rate, and vice versa, while valves for diverting flow are designed for that single purpose.

Grouping of valves by method of flow regulation

Manual valves may be grouped according to the way the closure member moves onto the seat. Four groups of valves are thereby distinguishable:

  • Closing-down valves — a stopper-like closure member is moved to and from the seat in the direction of the seat axis.
  • Slide valves — a gate-like closure member is moved across the flow passage.
  • Rotary valves — a plug or disc-like closure member is rotated within the flow passage, around an axis normal to the flow stream.
  • Flex-body valves — the closure member flexes the valve body.

Each valve group represents a number of distinct types of valves that use the same method of flow regulation, but differ in the shape of the closure member. For example, plug valves and butterfly valves are both rotary valves, but of a different type. Selection of valves

The method by which the closure member regulates the flow and the configuration of the flow path through the valve impart a certain flow characteristic to the valve, which is taken into account when selecting a valve for a given flow-regulating duty.

Valves for stopping and starting flow

These valves are normally selected for low flow resistance and a straight-through flow passage. Such valves are slide valves, rotary valves, and flex-body valves. Closing-down valves offer by their tortuous flow passage a higher flow resistance than other valves and are therefore less frequently used for this purpose. However, if the higher flow resistance can be accepted — as is frequently the case — closing-down valves may likewise be used for this duty.

Valves for controlling flow rate

These are selected for easy adjustment of the flow rate. Closing-down valves lend themselves for this duty because of the directly proportional relationship between the size of the seat opening and the travel of the closure member. Rotary valves and flex-body valves also offer good throttling control, but normally only over a restricted valve-opening range. Gate valves, in which a circular disc travels across a circular seat opening, achieve good flow control only near the closed valve position and are therefore not normally used for this duty.

Valves for diverting flow

These valves have three or more ports, depending on the flow diversion duty. Valves that adapt readily to this duty are plug valves and ball valves. For this reason, most valves for the diversion of flow are one of these types. However, other types of valves have also been adapted for the diversion of flow, in some cases by combining two or more valves that are suitably interlinked.

Valves for fluids with solids in suspension

If the fluid carries solids in suspension, the valves best suited for this duty have a closure member that slides across the seat with a wiping motion. Valves in which the closure member moves squarely on and off the seat may trap solids and are therefore suitable only for essentially clean fluids unless the seating material can embed trapped solids.

Valve end connections

Valves may be provided with any type of end connection used to connect piping. The most important of these for valves are threaded, flanged, and welding end connections.

  • Threaded end connections. Made as a rule with taper or parallel female threads screwed over tapered male pipe threads. Because such a joint contains large leakage passages, a sealant or filler closes the leakage passages; if the body material is weldable, screwed joints may be seal welded — necessary when mating parts have widely differing coefficients of expansion and the operating temperature cycles widely. Threaded-end valves are primarily used up to DN 50 (NPS 2); installing and sealing the joint becomes rapidly more difficult with size, though valves are available up to DN 150 (NPS 6). Couplings at appropriate points ease erection and removal: up to DN 50 (NPS 1) unions with a parallel-thread nut, larger couplings flanged. Codes may restrict the use of threaded-end valves depending on application.
  • Flanged end connections. Permit valves to be easily installed and removed from the pipeline, but flanged valves are bulkier and dearer than threaded-end valves. Because flanged joints are tightened by a number of bolts, each requiring less torque than a corresponding screwed joint, they can be adapted for all sizes and pressures. Above 350 °C (660 °F), however, creep relaxation of bolts, gasket and flanges can in time noticeably lower the bolt load; highly stressed joints may leak at these temperatures. Flange standards offer a variety of flange-face designs and recommend the face finish: a serrated finish gives good results with soft gaskets, metallic gaskets need a finer finish. Gasket design is discussed in unit 2.
  • Welding end connections. Suitable for all pressures and temperatures and considerably more reliable than flanged connections at elevated temperatures and other severe applications; removal and re-erection is more difficult, so their use is normally restricted to applications in which the valve must operate reliably for long periods, or which are critical or involve high temperatures. Welding-end valves up to DN 50 (NPS 2) are usually provided with welding sockets receiving plain-end pipes; socket-weld joints form a crevice with possible crevice corrosion for some fluids, and pipe vibrations can fatigue the joint — codes may restrict the use of welding sockets.

Valve ratings

The rating of valves defines the pressure-temperature relationship within which the valve may be operated; the responsibility for determining ratings has been left largely to the manufacturer. The U.S. practice of stating the rating of general-purpose valves as WOG (water, oil, gas) and WSP (wet steam pressure) is a carryover from the days when these were the substances generally carried: WOG refers to the room-temperature rating, WSP is usually the high-temperature rating. When both are given, a straight-line pressure-temperature relationship is generally understood to exist between the two points.

Some U.S. and British standards for flanged valves set ratings equal to the standard flange rating and specify the permissible materials for the pressure-containing parts. The rating of welding-end valves frequently corresponds to that of flanged valves, but standards may permit special ratings meeting the actual operating conditions. If the valve contains polymeric components, the pressure-temperature relationship is limited by the properties of the polymer; some standards (e.g. ball valves) specify P-T limits for the valve, and where they do not, it is the manufacturer's responsibility to state the pressure and temperature limitations.

Globe valves

Globe valves are closing-down valves in which the closure member is moved squarely on and off the seat; it is customary to refer to the closure member as a disc irrespective of its shape. By this mode of disc travel, the seat opening varies in direct proportion to the travel of the disc — ideally suited for regulating flow rate. In addition, the seating load can be positively controlled by a screwed stem, and the disc moves with little or no friction onto the seat, depending on the seat and disc design — the sealing capacity is therefore potentially high. On the debit side, the seatings may trap solids travelling in the flowing fluid.

Globe valves may of course be used for on-off duty provided the flow resistance from the tortuous flow passage can be accepted; some are designed for low flow resistance specifically for on-off duty. If the valve has to be opened and closed frequently, globe valves are ideally suited because of the short disc travel and the inherent robustness of the seatings to these movements. This wide range of duties has led to numerous variations designed to meet a particular duty at the lowest cost: Figures 3-2 through 3-11 show valves commonly used in pipelines for flow control; Figures 3-12 through 3-14 are specialty valves for special duties, as described in their captions.

Valve body patterns

The basic globe-body patterns are the standard pattern (Figures 3-2, 3-3, 3-5, 3-6, 3-9, 3-11), the angle pattern (Figures 3-4, 3-8) and the oblique pattern (Figures 3-7, 3-10, 3-12, 3-13). The standard pattern is the most common but offers, by its tortuous flow passage, the highest resistance to flow. If the valve is mounted near a pipe bend, the angle-pattern body offers two advantages: greatly reduced flow resistance, and fewer pipe joints — it saves a pipe elbow. The oblique pattern is designed to reduce the flow resistance to a minimum, particularly well achieved in Figure 3-10, which combines low flow resistance for on-off duty with the robustness of globe-valve seatings.

The specialty valves: Figure 3-12 shows an oblique-pattern globe with split body and seat-wiping mechanism for slurry service; Figure 3-13 a globe adapted for draining vessels with the seat flush with the bottom of the vessel; Figure 3-14 a three-way globe used as change-over valve in pressure-relief-valve installations — one PRV isolated while the second is in service.

Valve seatings

Globe valves may be provided with metal or soft seatings. With metal seatings, the seating stress must not only be high but also circumferentially uniform to achieve the desired fluid tightness. These requirements have led to a number of seating designs; those shown in Figure 3-15 are common variations.

Flat seatings (Figure 3-15a) align readily to each other without close disc guiding; if the disc is moved onto the seat without being rotated, the seatings mate without friction, so galling resistance of the material is unimportant. Distortion of seat roundness by pipeline stresses does not interfere with sealability as long as the seat face remains flat. If flow is directed from above the seat, the seating faces are protected from the direct impact of solids or liquid droplets.

Tapered seatings (Figure 3-15b, c, d) greatly increase the seating stress for a given load, but only if the seatings mate perfectly — the disc must not land cocked, must be properly guided, and the faces must be perfectly round, which is difficult to maintain in larger valves where pipeline stresses may distort the seat. Furthermore, as the seatings tighten, the disc slips deeper under friction, so the materials must be galling-resistant.

Narrow-faced tapered seatings (b) have particularly high stress but cannot guide the disc squarely; properly guided, they achieve an extremely high degree of fluid tightness — yet they are readily damaged by solids or droplets, so they serve mainly for gases free of solids and droplets. To improve robustness without sacrificing stress, the seatings in (c) are tapered with wide faces that guide the disc, the initial contact face being about 3 mm (1/8 in.) wide with the remainder of the seat bore tapered slightly steeper; as the load increases the disc slips deeper, widening the seating width — less readily eroded than (b), and the long disc taper improves the throttling characteristic. Performance may be further improved by hollowing out the disc (Figure 3-11) to give the disc shell elasticity to adapt to roundness deviations.

Ball-shaped seatings (d): a ball disc on a tapered seat; the disc can roll to some extent on the seat until aligned. Because contact approaches line contact, the stress is very high — but line contact is prone to erosion damage, so this construction serves only for dry gases free of solids, and is used mainly by U.S. manufacturers.

For fine throttling duty, the disc is frequently provided with a needle-shaped extension (Figure 3-4) or a V-port skirt (Figure 3-8 and the seatings in Figure 3-16). In the V-port design the seating faces separate before the V-ports open, largely protecting the faces against erosion. An example of soft-seating design is the valve in Figure 3-2: the soft insert is carried by the disc and may be renewed readily.

Connection of disc to stem

The stem may rotate while raising or lowering the disc, or be prevented from rotating — bearing on the disc-to-stem connection. Most globe valves incorporate a rotating stem for simplicity. If the disc is integral with the rotating stem — frequent in small needle valves (Figure 3-4) — the seatings mate while the disc rotates, possibly causing severe wear; such valves serve mainly regulating duty with infrequent shut-off. For all other duties with rotating stems the disc swivels freely on the stem; swivel discs should have minimum free axial play to prevent rapid reciprocating disc movements near the closed position, and if the disc is guided by the stem there should be little lateral play so the disc cannot land cocked on the seat.

With nonrotating stems — Figures 3-6, 3-7, 3-10, 3-11 — the disc may be integral with the stem (Figure 3-11) or a separate component (Figures 3-6, 3-7, 3-10). Nonrotating stems are required in valves with diaphragm or bellows stem seals (Figures 3-6, 3-7), and are used in high-pressure valves (Figures 3-10, 3-11) to facilitate power operators.

Inside and outside stem screw

The screw may be located inside the valve body (Figures 3-2 through 3-4) or outside (Figures 3-5 through 3-14). The inside screw permits an economical bonnet construction but cannot be serviced from outside — best suited for fluids of good lubricity; for the majority of minor duties it gives good service. The outside screw can be serviced from outside and is preferred for severe duties.

Bonnet joints

Bonnets may be joined to the body by screwing, flanging, welding or a pressure-seal mechanism; or the bonnet may be integral with the body.

  • Screwed-in bonnet (Figure 3-4): simplest and least expensive; but the gasket must accommodate rotating faces, frequent unscrewing may damage the joint faces, and the torque becomes very large for larger valves — restricted to sizes not greater than DN 80 (NPS 3).
  • Seal-welded or welded bonnet (Figures 3-6, 3-7): if the material is weldable, the screwed-in bonnet may be seal welded, or the connection made entirely by welding — economical and most reliable irrespective of size, pressure and temperature; on the debit side, access to the internals only by removing the weld, so these are used where the valve can stay maintenance-free for long periods, is a throw-away valve, or where sealing reliability outweighs access.
  • Bonnet with screwed union ring (Figures 3-2, 3-3): prevents any motion between the joint faces while tightening, so repeated unscrewing cannot readily harm the faces; size restriction as for screwed-in bonnets (DN 80).
  • Flanged bonnet (Figures 3-8, 3-9): tightening effort spread over a number of bolts, designable for any valve size and pressure; but increasingly heavy and bulky with size and pressure, and above 350 °C (660 °F) creep relaxation can lower the bolt load — critical applications may be seal welded.
  • Pressure-seal bonnet (Figure 3-10): the fluid pressure tightens the joint — the seal becomes tighter as pressure increases; frequently preferred for large valves at high pressures and temperatures.
  • Integral bonnet (Figures 3-5, 3-11): small valves avoid the bonnet joint altogether; access to the internals through the gland opening, which is large enough to pass the valve components.

Stuffing boxes and back seating

Figures 3-17 through 3-19 show three types of stuffing boxes typical for valves with a rising stem. The basic box (Figure 3-17) is an annular chamber containing the packing between the gland at the top and a shoulder at the bottom; the underside of the box carries a back seat which, with a corresponding seat around the stem, isolates the packing from the system fluid when the valve is fully open.

The box in Figure 3-18 is supplemented with a condensing chamber at the bottom — originally a cooling chamber for condensable gases such as steam; here it carries a test plug which may be removed to test the back seat for leak tightness. A third variation (Figure 3-19) has a lantern ring mounted between two packing sections, mainly with compression packings, serving four different purposes:

  • Injection chamber for a sealant or for extruded or leached-out lubricant.
  • Pressure chamber in which an external fluid is pressurized to equal or slightly above system pressure to prevent leakage of the system fluid — the external fluid must be compatible and harmless to the valve surroundings.
  • Sealant chamber in vacuum service, fed with an external fluid acting as sealant.
  • Leakage collection chamber from which leakage is piped to a safe location.

The lantern ring, however, increases the packing depth; sidewall friction reduces the gland input load as the depth increases, impairing seal integrity. Replacing the lantern ring with a spring is used in rotating pump-shaft seals to improve integrity.

Direction of flow through globe valves

The question of flow direction has two answers. If flow from above the disc could remove the disc from the stem or a component from the disc, flow directed from below the disc is mandatory. Hand-operated globe valves with rotating stem and metal seatings can then be closed fluid-tight without undue effort only if the fluid load on the underside of the disc does not exceed about 40–60 kN (9,000–13,000 lb); with a nonrotating stem and roller-bearing supported stem nut (Figures 3-10, 3-11), tight closure is possible against about 70–100 kN (16,000–22,000 lb), depending on the leakage criterion and valve construction. One advantage of flow from below: the stuffing box of the closed valve is relieved from the upstream pressure. On the debit side, closing against a hot fluid such as steam, thermal contraction of the stem afterwards can be just enough to induce seat leakage.

If flow is directed from above the disc, the closing force from the fluid on top of the disc supplements the stem force — greatly increasing sealing reliability. With a rotating stem the valve may be opened without excessive effort only if the fluid load does not exceed about 40–60 kN; with a nonrotating stem and roller-bearing stem nut, hand opening is possible against about 70–100 kN. If the load is higher, a bypass valve may have to be provided to pressurize the downstream system before the globe valve is opened.

Piston valves

Piston valves are closing-down valves in which a piston-shaped closure member intrudes into or withdraws from the seat bore (Figures 3-20 through 3-24). The seat seal is achieved between the lateral faces of the piston and the seat bore. When the valve opens, flow cannot start until the piston has been completely withdrawn — erosive damage occurs away from the seating surfaces. When closing, the piston tends to wipe away solids deposited on the seat, so piston valves may handle fluids carrying solids in suspension. If the seatings are damaged, piston and seat can be replaced in situ — the valve is like new without any machining.

Like globe valves, piston valves permit good flow control; for sensitive adjustment the piston may be fitted with a needle-shaped extension. They are also used for stopping and starting flow when the flow resistance of the tortuous passage is accepted.

Construction

The seatings are formed by the lateral faces of the valve bore and the piston. Fluid-tight contact is achieved by a packing forming part of the valve bore (Figures 3-20 through 3-22) or part of the piston (Figures 3-23, 3-24). Packings commonly used are compression packings based on compressed asbestos or PTFE, and O-ring packings.

In the Figure 3-20 valve the piston moves in two packings separated by a lantern ring: the lower packing is the seat packing, the upper seals the piston to the atmosphere; the bonnet serves as the gland, letting both packings be tightened through the cover bolts. Disc springs under the bolt nuts minimize packing-stress variations from thermal expansion. If a packing leaks, the seal is restored by retightening — while the valve is closed, to prevent unrestrained expansion of the seat packing into the bore. The Figure 3-21 valve differs only in the pressure-balanced piston.

The packing train of Figure 3-22 is likewise stressed through the bonnet with springs under the bolt nuts or between bonnet and packing; as the piston reaches the final closing position a shoulder on the piston contacts a compression ring on top of the packing, so further travel tightens the packing further. The Figure 3-23 valve carries the seat packing on the end of the piston, supported from below by a loose compression ring; in the final closing position the ring comes to rest on a shoulder in the seat bore, and further progression tightens the packing. Because the packing establishes interference only in the last closing stages, the operating effort is lower over part of the travel — the Ram-Seal principle. The Figure 3-24 valve also carries the packing on the piston, but the loose ring is replaced by a friction ring acting as a spring element pre-stressing the packing; as the piston enters the seat the ring comes to rest in the bore and further travel increases the packing stress.

National standards applying specifically to piston valves do not exist.

Duty: controlling flow · stopping and starting flow. Service: gases · liquids · fluids with solids in suspension · vacuum.
