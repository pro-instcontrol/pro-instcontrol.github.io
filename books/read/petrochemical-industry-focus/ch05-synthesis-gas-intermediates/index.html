Synthesis Gas and Nonhydrocarbon Intermediates

Not every petrochemical intermediate contains a carbon chain ready to crack. Hydrogen, sulfur, carbon black and synthesis gas are made from small molecules and then feed the rest of the complex.

Sources used. Chemistry of Petrochemical Processes (Matar, 2nd ed., 2000), ch. 4-5; Wiley Critical Content: Petroleum Technology, Fuels, Synthetic, Liquid; Petroleum Product Handbook, Section 12: Petrochemicals

The nonhydrocarbon intermediates

Matar identifies hydrogen, sulfur, carbon black and synthesis gas as the important intermediates that are not hydrocarbon compounds. Synthesis gas is a mixture of carbon monoxide and hydrogen; its ratio varies with feed, production method and end use. It can be made by steam reforming or partial oxidation of anything from methane to heavy residues, or by gasifying coal to a medium-Btu gas.

During World War II Germany gasified coal to make synthesis gas and then liquid hydrocarbons in the gasoline range by Fischer-Tropsch technology. Matar notes that the route is still used in South Africa, where coal is inexpensive, through SASOL I, II and III.

Hydrogen routes and numbers

Matar gives the energy baseline: splitting water into hydrogen and oxygen requires an enthalpy input of +286 kJ/mol, so electrolysis and diffusion purification are expensive. The most economical route described is steam reforming of natural gas or petroleum fractions over a promoted nickel catalyst at about 800°C: CH4 + H2O → CO + 3H2. Shift conversion then turns CO into CO2, and pressure swing adsorption removes carbon oxides.

For smaller, high-purity needs where methanol is available, steam reforming of methanol uses CH3OH + H2O → CO2 + 3H2 and produces 0.18 to 1.8 MMscfd of hydrogen. In refining, hydrogen is essentially a coproduct of catalytic naphtha reforming; in petrochemicals it hydrogenates benzene to cyclohexane and benzoic acid to cyclohexane carboxylic acid, both nylon precursors, and selectively hydrogenates acetylene in C4 olefin mixtures.

Ammonia, methanol and liquid fuels

Hydrogen as part of synthesis gas is a precursor for ammonia, methanol, Oxo alcohols and hydrocarbons from Fischer-Tropsch processes. Ammonia synthesis depends on shift conversion to adjust the hydrogen-to-carbon-monoxide relationship; methanol and higher alcohols sit on the same syngas platform.

Wiley's entry on synthetic liquid fuels broadens the picture: liquids made from sources other than natural crude include material from bitumen deposits, while syngas routes can make gasoline-range hydrocarbons. The boundary between a refinery, a gas-to-liquids plant and a petrochemical complex is therefore a matter of product destination, not of fundamentally different chemistry.

Why the gas platform sits inside the petrochemical site

A petrochemical site needs hydrogen for hydrotreating, for selective hydrogenation and for synthesis gas; it also needs sulfur recovery and often carbon black. Keeping these utilities inside the same boundary reduces transport of hazardous gas and lets the hydrogen balance be managed alongside the olefin and aromatics balance.

The instrumentation consequence is straightforward: an upset in the syngas unit is not a local utility problem. It propagates into hydroprocessing, into ammonia or methanol synthesis, and into the selective hydrogenation steps that protect polymer-grade olefins.
