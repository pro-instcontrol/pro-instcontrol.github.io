Olefins: Steam Cracking, Ethylene and Propylene

Refineries recover the olefins they happen to make; steam crackers manufacture olefins because petrochemical demand is far larger than refinery by-product supply.

Sources used. Chemistry of Petrochemical Processes (Matar, 2nd ed., 2000), ch. 3 and ch. 7-8; Wiley Critical Content: Petroleum Technology, Ethylene; Wiley Critical Content: Petroleum Technology, Propylene; Petroleum Product Handbook, Section 12: Petrochemicals

Why steam cracking dominates

Matar lists ethylene, propylene, butylenes and butadiene as the olefins and diolefins that matter most for petrochemicals. They can be separated from catalytic and thermal cracking gas streams, but demand is much greater than those operations produce. Most olefins and butadiene are therefore made by steam cracking hydrocarbons, with feed ranging from light paraffinic gas to petroleum fractions and residues.

The main reaction is bond breaking by free radicals and beta scission of C-C bonds, and it is strongly endothermic. Superheated steam supplies heat and lowers hydrocarbon partial pressure; it also reduces carbon deposits from high-temperature pyrolysis. Cracking ethane can be read as free-radical dehydrogenation with hydrogen as a coproduct; methyl and ethyl radicals then generate propene and 1-butene as side products.

Furnace variables and the coke penalty

The important process variables are reactor temperature, residence time, steam-to-hydrocarbon ratio and feed characteristics. Increasing temperature favours olefins, higher-molecular-weight olefins and aromatics, so operating temperature is chosen to maximise the desired products rather than simply to maximise conversion.

In a typical ethane cracker, fresh and recycled ethane are cracked with steam as diluent and outlet temperature is usually in the 800°C range. Coke deposits on radiant tubes raise tube metal temperature and pressure drop, cut yields, increase energy use and shorten coil life. Better alloys have allowed higher temperature, shorter residence time and lower pressure drop, but decoking remains a recurring operating event.

Ethylene: the largest-volume building block

Wiley calls ethylene the largest-volume petrochemical produced worldwide, noting that it has almost no direct end use and is used essentially as a chemical building block. It had been recovered from coke-oven gas in Europe since 1930, but became a large-volume intermediate in the 1940s as U.S. producers separated it from refinery waste gas and then moved to deliberate steam cracking.

Matar groups its derivative chemistry by reaction type: oxidation, oxidative carbonylation, chlorination, hydration to ethanol, oligomerization and alkylation. That list explains why an olefin unit is rarely standalone: its value lies in the chain of downstream units that convert a simple C2 molecule into ethanol, ethylene oxide, chlorinated products and higher olefins.

Propylene: old feedstock, new routes

Wiley describes propylene as perhaps the oldest petrochemical feedstock and one of the principal light olefins. Its traditional uses are alkylation or polymer-gasoline feedstock for octane improvement; large quantities also go to polypropylene and to chemicals such as acrylonitrile, propylene oxide, 2-propanol and cumene.

Propylene comes both from steam cracking and from catalytic cracking, which is why a refinery-FCC unit and a petrochemical olefin complex are often discussed together. Matar's chapter 8 adds the reaction families — oxidation, oxyacylation, chlorination, hydration, hydroformylation (the Oxo reaction), metathesis and alkylation — showing how one C3 olefin fans out into solvents, plastics and detergents.
