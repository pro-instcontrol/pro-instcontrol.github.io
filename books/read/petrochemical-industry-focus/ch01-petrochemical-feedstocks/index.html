Petrochemical Feedstocks and Refinery Integration

A petrochemical plant is a choice about molecules before it is a choice about equipment: methane, ethane, LPG, naphtha or gas oil each opens a different product tree.

Sources used. Chemistry of Petrochemical Processes (Matar, 2nd ed., 2000), ch. 1-3; Wiley Critical Content: Petroleum Technology, Part III, Petrochemical Feedstocks; Petroleum Product Handbook, Section 12: Petrochemicals; Handbook of Petroleum Processing, ch. 1-2: crude oil and refinery configuration

What counts as a petrochemical feedstock

Wiley's Petrochemical Feedstocks defines petrochemicals as substances isolated from or derived from natural gas or petroleum; coal and agricultural products remain very small sources. Natural gas is mostly methane, with C2-C7 paraffins in amounts that vary sharply from field to field. Non-associated gas normally carries more methane, while associated gas carries more of the heavier hydrocarbons.

Those heavier components are recovered as natural gas liquids. Ethane is separated for steam cracking to ethylene; propane and butane become LPG. Matar notes that before natural gas is used it must be treated to remove impurities and recover hydrocarbons heavier than methane. The cited U.S. gas consumption for 1998 was about 22.5 trillion ft3.

From raw material to intermediate

Matar organises the subject into primary raw materials, hydrocarbon intermediates and the processes that produce them. The intermediates are light molecules such as methane and ethane, heavier mixtures such as naphtha and gas oil, and families of paraffins, olefins, dienes and aromatics. The same crude fraction can be a fuel blend component or a chemical feedstock depending on the route chosen.

This is where refining and petrochemicals stop being separate subjects. Catalytic reforming supplies aromatics; cracking supplies olefins; gas processing supplies ethane and LPG. The refinery provides the molecules, and the petrochemical complex decides which of them become polymers, solvents or fibres.

Treating gas before it becomes feed

Raw gas contains variable amounts of hydrogen sulfide, carbon dioxide and water vapour. H2S must be removed because it reduces heating value and can solidify under the high pressure and low temperature used in transport; water is reduced to give a sweet, dry gas. Amine solutions are widely used, with concentration normally in the range of 15 to 30%.

Matar also describes physical solvent systems such as Selexol, which uses dimethyl ether of polyethylene glycol and contacts the gas countercurrently with descending solvent. Where heavy hydrocarbons are present, treatment includes their recovery as NGL. A petrochemical feed specification is therefore the result of gas processing, not just of the reservoir.

Feedstock choice is an economic decision

Wiley states the choice directly: feedstock selection is an economic decision, but it may be constrained by hardware limitations or by the availability of indigenous hydrocarbon supply. Ethane cracking favours ethylene; naphtha and gas oil produce a broader slate that includes propylene, C4s and pyrolysis gasoline. The same furnace design cannot treat every feed identically.

The economic evaluation chapter adds the long view: crude-oil price trends affect the prices of organic chemicals, and refining has become larger, more international and more closely integrated with petrochemical facilities. Between 1982 and 1997 the number of operating U.S. refineries fell from 300 to 164, largely through closure of smaller plants.
