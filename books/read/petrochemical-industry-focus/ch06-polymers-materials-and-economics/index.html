Polymers, Materials and the Economics Behind Them

The earlier units make molecules; this one explains why those molecules become materials — and why the industry keeps reorganising around feed, catalysts and markets.

Sources used. Chemistry of Petrochemical Processes (Matar, 2nd ed., 2000), ch. 11-12; Wiley Critical Content: Petroleum Technology, Synthetic Organic Chemicals, Economic Evaluation; Wiley Critical Content: Petroleum Technology, Petrochemical Feedstocks; Petroleum Product Handbook, Section 12: Petrochemicals

Two ways to build a chain

Matar defines a polymer as a macromolecule built from monomers. Addition polymerisation is a chain reaction used mainly with olefins and conjugated diolefins; initiators can be free radicals, anions, cations or coordination compounds. Ethylene with a coordination catalyst gives linear polyethylene, n CH2=CH2 → [–CH2–CH2–]n, while styrene polymerises mainly head-to-tail.

Condensation polymerisation joins two similar or different monomers and usually releases a small molecule such as water, as in esterification of a diol and a diacid; ring-opening condensation is the exception with no small molecule released. Branching and crosslinking then change physical and mechanical properties: branched polyethylene has a few long branches and many short ones, while crosslinking improves properties through multifunctional groups or irradiation.

From monomers to material families

Matar's polymer chapters cover thermoplastics and engineering resins, thermosetting plastics, synthetic rubber and synthetic fibres. Most high-volume thermoplastics are made by addition polymerisation; phenol-formaldehyde resins are classic thermosets; styrene-butadiene-styrene is a block copolymer rubber. Thermoplastic elastomers are more economical than thermoset rubber because fewer manufacturing steps are needed than vulcanisation.

Scale numbers in the text show how these materials became infrastructure: world production of all polyethylene grades was about 100 billion pounds in 1997. Gas-fluidised-bed polymerisation reduced investment relative to high-pressure LDPE processes and cut reactor purge and waste streams. SBS accounted for 70% of global styrene block copolymers, with global SBC capacity around 1.1 million tons.

Materials replacing metals and glass

Engineering thermoplastics are designed to replace metals and glass where strength and temperature resistance matter, offering light weight, low cost and ease of fabrication. Polycarbonates are transparent, strong and heat resistant. Automobile parts increasingly use polystyrene copolymers, polypropylene, polycarbonates and PVC, reducing both vehicle weight and fuel consumption.

Fibres follow the same logic: melt spinning is preferred to solution spinning for polyesters because of lower cost, and low-cost polypropylene fibres replace sisal and jute. The material choice is therefore a combined decision about chemistry, processing and final market, not simply about monomer price.

The economic layer

The Wiley economic evaluation chapter links petrochemicals to the wider economy: plastics, fibres, solvents, biochemicals, food chemicals and construction materials feed electronics, automotive, biotechnology, agriculture and environmental remediation. In 1997 the United States had 54 petrochemical establishments employing nearly 11,000 people, with aggregate shipments of $20.5 billion; Texas alone accounted for 21 establishments and $16.6 billion.

Catalysts appear as a strategic variable: they account for well over 60% of organic production and 90% of current organic chemical processes, and catalyst manufacture exceeded $10 billion across refining, polymerisation, chemicals and environmental remediation in 1999. Crude-oil price trends move organic chemical prices, and since 1960 the industry has become global. Feedstock, catalyst and market are one decision.
