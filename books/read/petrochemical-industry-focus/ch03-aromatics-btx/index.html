Aromatics: BTX, Extraction and C8 Separation

BTX is not a product you simply draw out of a tower. It is a family of molecules recovered from reformate or pyrolysis gasoline by solvent extraction, then separated by boiling point, crystallization and adsorption.

Sources used. Chemistry of Petrochemical Processes (Matar, 2nd ed., 2000), ch. 2 and ch. 10; Wiley Critical Content: Petroleum Technology, BTX Processing; Wiley Critical Content: Petroleum Technology, Benzene and Toluene; Wiley Critical Content: Petroleum Technology, Xylenes and Ethylbenzene; Petroleum Refining: Technology and Economics, ch. on catalytic reforming

BTX as a group of feedstocks

Wiley's BTX Processing notes that benzene, toluene and xylenes are the lowest-molecular-weight aromatic homologues and are very large-scale chemical feedstocks. They are usually produced together, so BTX is treated as a group even though BTX itself is not an article of commerce. It exists either as an important component of reformate or pyrolysis gasoline, or as separated and purified individual aromatics.

Matar adds that benzene was obtained mainly from coal tar before 1940; today it comes mainly from catalytic reforming, with pyrolysis gasoline and coal liquids as other sources. The aromatic ring is stable because of overlapping structures, which is precisely why these molecules are valuable as solvents and as precursors for phenol, TNT, nylons and plastics.

Solvent extraction from reformate

Benzene, toluene, xylenes and ethylbenzene are obtained mainly from catalytic reforming of heavy naphtha. Matar describes the Union Carbide Tetra process, which uses tetraethylene glycol as solvent: reformate containing aromatics, paraffins and naphthenes is contacted countercurrently with aqueous solvent, the rich solvent is cooled and stripped, and the raffinate is water-washed to recover traces of solvent before storage.

The extract, composed of BTX and ethylbenzene, is then fractionated. Benzene and toluene are recovered separately, while ethylbenzene and xylenes leave together as the C8 aromatics cut. The choice of solvent is governed by high affinity for aromatics, thermal stability and rapid phase separation.

The C8 problem

The boiling points of C8 aromatics lie in a very narrow range, so ordinary fractional distillation cannot separate them cleanly. Superfractionation is used to segregate ethylbenzene from the xylene mixture. p-Xylene is the most valuable isomer for synthetic fibres, and older fractional crystallization recovered only about 60% of it.

Industry moved to continuous liquid-phase adsorption separation. Adding an isomerization unit that converts o- and m-xylenes to p-xylene raises overall p-xylene yield to about 90%. Matar describes the Mobil isomerization process using a shape-selective ZSM-5 zeolite catalyst; some ethylbenzene is also converted to benzene in that step.

Downstream from the aromatic ring

Wiley treats benzene, toluene, xylene and ethylbenzene as separate large entries because each has its own derivative tree. Benzene feeds phenol, styrene and cyclohexane; toluene feeds TNT and benzoic acid; xylenes feed polyester precursors; ethylbenzene is dehydrogenated to styrene for polystyrene and rubber.

The practical point for an instrumentation reader is that composition control starts upstream. A reformer that changes severity, an extraction column that loses solvent selectivity, or an isomerization unit drifting off temperature will all show up as off-specification aromatics far downstream.
