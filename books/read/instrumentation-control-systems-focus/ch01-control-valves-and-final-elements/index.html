Control Valves and Final Elements

A control valve is a variable orifice. Everything else — actuator, fail mode, sizing formula and relief backup — follows from that simple fact.

Sources used. Pipeline Rules of Thumb Handbook (McAllister, ed., 2005), ch. 7: Control Valves; Surface Production Operations Vol. 1 (Arnold, 2nd ed., 1998), §2: Controlling the Process; Petroleum Engineers Handbook, Part 2 (Bradley, ed.), §16: Automation of Lease Equipment; NFPA 58, Annex E: Pressure Relief Devices

The valve as a variable orifice

Arnold's Surface Production Operations puts the function plainly: control valves are used throughout the process to control pressure, level, temperature or flow. For a given pressure drop across the valve, the larger the orifice, the greater the flow. Moving the stem upward lifts the plug off the seat and opens the annulus; moving it downward closes the opening. Chokes and other flow-control devices work on the same principle, whether the orifice is fixed or variable.

The most common way to move the stem is a pneumatic actuator. Instrument air or gas applied to the diaphragm overcomes a spring. The actuator action must be matched to the valve body so the required failure mode is achieved: on loss of instrument air, a fail-closed liquid valve must drive the stem toward full closure, while many pressure-control applications require the valve to fail open.

Cv sizing and the choked-flow limit

The Pipeline Rules of Thumb Handbook gives the working sizing relations. For liquids, the volume basis is Cv = q / √(G/ΔP) and the weight basis is Cv = W / (500 √(ΔP × G)). These equations exclude flashing, high-viscosity, two-phase flow and unusually high pressure drops. For gases and vapors other than steam, a simplified relation uses absolute inlet and outlet pressures; compressibility must be handled with the later, more complete equations.

A crucial limit: usable pressure drop for gas, vapor or steam sizing is capped at a fraction of absolute inlet pressure. Near that value the valve reaches critical (sonic) velocity, and further reduction of downstream pressure does not increase velocity or flow. Treating that ceiling as ordinary ΔP is a classic sizing error.

Actuation families and safety functions

The Petroleum Engineers Handbook classifies automatic control valves by the energy medium that actuates them: fluid-controlled, electrically controlled, and fluid-electric. Diaphragm operators and fluid cylinders dominate the fluid-controlled group; cylinders are normally paired with valve bodies needing 90° rotation. In fluid-electric systems, fluid energy operates the valve while electrical energy controls the fluid-energy source.

Not every automatic valve is a process-control valve. High-/low-pressure safety shut-in valves, excess-flow valves, pressure and temperature switches, and pump-off controls primarily perform safety functions. The handbook notes that excess-flow or low-pressure shut-in guards against a flowline break and surface property damage, while high-pressure shut-in prevents pressure above the allowable limit from building in the line; both are normally installed at the wellhead.

Relief and rupture backup

Pipeline Rules of Thumb Handbook devotes a separate run of rules to relief valve sizing, selection, installation and testing, followed by rupture-disc sizing and the resistance-to-flow (KR) method. NFPA 58 Annex E covers pressure relief devices in the LP-gas context. The point for an instrumentation engineer is that a control valve is not a safety device: the relief path and its setting must be designed independently of the control loop.
