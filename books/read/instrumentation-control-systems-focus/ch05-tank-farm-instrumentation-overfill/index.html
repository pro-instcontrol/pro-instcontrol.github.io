Tank-Farm Instrumentation, Overfill and Leak Detection

Tank instrumentation is where measurement, safety and law meet: a level signal can be inventory data, an overfill alarm, or evidence of compliance.

Sources used. Handbook of Storage Tank Systems: Codes, Regulations, and Designs (Geyer, ed., Marcel Dekker) — ch. 6 and sample specifications; 40 CFR Part 280.43/280.44; PEI RP100/200/300; UL/ULC; API 2350; NFPA 30/30A; UFC Article 79; BOCA F-3207; NACE and Steel Tank Institute recommended practices for corrosion control; Marshall E., Pipeline Corrosion and Cathodic Protection, 3rd ed.; Pipeline Rules of Thumb Handbook (2005), ch. 16: Instrumentation; Measurement Instrumentation for Upstream Oil & Gas (Emerson, 2026)

Prescriptive versus performance-based specifications

The Handbook of Storage Tank Systems uses an automatic tank gauge to show the difference. A prescriptive specification demands an integrated electronic gauging and monitoring system able to detect a 0.2 gal/hr leak, detect inventory loss of at least 1.0 percent of flow-through plus 130 gal monthly, use magnetostrictive probes in each tank, and provide an LCD display, printer, audible and visual alarms, battery backup, UL listing and even a named model. A performance-based specification instead requires a system meeting 40 CFR Part 280.43 and 280.44, UL listed and installed to PEI RP 100.

Neither approach is automatically right. The prescriptive route leaves little room for interpretation; the performance route relies on third-party standards that have already been researched. The handbook's own warning is that a cookie-cutter tank design is ill-advised, if not outright dangerous.

Overfill thresholds in the codes

The sample specifications put numbers on the alarm logic. NFPA 30A language calls for an audible alarm at 90 percent of tank capacity and an optional positive shut-off fill limiter at 95 percent, rated for the fill flow rate and pressure. Uniform Fire Code and BOCA language instead uses an audible or visual alarm at 85 percent, or a level gauge marked at 85 percent, with shut-off at 90 percent. The contractor must also provide a level indication accessible to the delivery operator.

The same handbook later examines the high-level alarm as a design choice: it may need to be outdoors near the tank where the driver hears it; it may require electrical wiring where mechanical methods do not; it is commonly set at 90 percent; and frequent triggering leads to complacency, much like a car alarm. Its advantage is that it can be an option on an automatic tank gauging system and works with suction or pressurized systems.

Monitoring and gauging system

The specification continues: leak detection for each tank interstice, continuous monitoring of primary and secondary containment, a probe in each interstitial monitoring tube, and a monitoring console with external alarms shown on the drawings. One electronic tank gauging and monitoring system should provide tank probes for liquid level, a control console with display and printer, means of water detection, external communications, and tank liquid level plus water measurement. Line-pressure leak monitors on product piping may be specified to detect 0.1, 0.2 or 0.3 gal/hr at 10 psi line pressure.

This is where the instrumentation engineer meets the standards map: 40 CFR Part 280, NFPA 30/30A, API 2350 for overfill protection, PEI RP100/200/300, UL and ULC listings, NACE corrosion-control practices and STI fabrication practices all appear in one specification.

Vents, overfill and fire consequence

The handbook connects instrumentation to physical protection. A pressure/vacuum vent stays closed except while product is added or removed; an emergency vent matters because an AST involved in a pool fire can generate pressure beyond a 2.5 psig design limit if the vent is disabled or wrongly sized. NFPA's review of AST fires found overfilling to be a common factor: once liquid leaves the tank or piping it forms a flammable vapour cloud, and an ignition source turns it into a pool fire or vapour-cloud explosion.

API 2350 is cited by model fire codes as the reference for overfill protection at bulk plants and terminals. The control system therefore closes the loop from level sensing to alarm, shut-off and pressure relief — with each layer belonging to a different code.
