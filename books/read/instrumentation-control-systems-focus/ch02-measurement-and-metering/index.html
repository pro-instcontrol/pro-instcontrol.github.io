Measurement and Metering Across the Chain

Measurement is never just a meter. It is a chain of fluid behaviour, sensor physics, correction factors and proving practice.

Sources used. Pipeline Rules of Thumb Handbook (2005), ch. 15: Measurement; Natural Gas Engineering Handbook (Guo & Ghalambor, 2005), ch. 10: Volumetric Measurement; Petroleum Engineers Handbook, Part 2, §16: Automatic Quantitative Measurement; Transport of Oil and Gas, §6.6: Fluid Volume Measurement

What changes the choice of meter

Pipeline Rules of Thumb Handbook lists the influences that shape a measurement decision: accuracy, electronic equipment, economics, fluid characteristics, standards, governmental influences and testing or proving practice. Their relative importance shifts over time, so a meter that was adequate in one era can become the limiting element when custody requirements tighten.

Multiphase flow and where uncertainty comes from

In multiphase pipe flow, the gas tends to race through the lower-resistance core while liquid is pushed toward the pipe wall; some liquid travels as droplets entrained in the gas. The velocity difference grows as gas volume fraction rises. A multiphase meter therefore has to resolve slip between phases, not merely read a single velocity, and its uncertainty grows with the very conditions that make the measurement valuable.

Orifice metering fundamentals

Natural Gas Engineering Handbook derives the orifice equation from the first law of thermodynamics and gives the AGA recommended gas-rate form: flow at base conditions depends on the orifice constant, differential pressure in inches of water at 60 °F, and absolute static pressure. The expansion factor Y corrects for density change as gas accelerates through the plate and cools adiabatically; it depends on differential pressure, absolute pressure, pipe diameter, plate diameter and tap type.

Flow through an orifice at constant pressure varies with the square root of differential pressure. At half the flow, ΔP is one quarter. Because a differential gauge cannot continuously record with acceptable accuracy below roughly one sixteenth of its range, one plate plus one gauge has a useful span from maximum capacity down to about one sixteenth of maximum. Choosing the plate is therefore a range decision as much as a sizing decision.

Charts, proving and meter families

Circular charts remain common even where digital recording exists. The typical 12-inch chart is either a uniform-scale direct-reading chart for differential pressure in inches of water and static pressure in psi, or a square-root-reading chart. Clocks rotate the chart at a set period. Proving against a master meter or prover closes the traceability loop; without it, a stable reading may simply be a stable error.

Positive-displacement meters trap a known volume between moving elements; inferential meters deduce volume from another property of the stream. Turbine meters turn the flowing gas into rotor speed, but filters ahead of them are almost a necessity for sustained accuracy. Elbow meters create very little differential pressure and are used mainly for control or indication rather than high-accuracy billing.

From gross volume to net volume

The Petroleum Engineers Handbook reminds that gross volumes must be corrected for water content, temperature, pressure and density before net volumes at standard conditions can be stated. It groups automatic liquid devices into positive-volume meters, positive-displacement meters and inferential meters, and describes the oil meter working with a capacitance probe and a net-oil computer to separate net oil from emulsion water. Measurement integrity is therefore a data problem as much as a hardware problem.
