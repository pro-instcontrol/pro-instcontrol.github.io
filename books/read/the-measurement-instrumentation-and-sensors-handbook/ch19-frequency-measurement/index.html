Frequency Measurement

Michael A. Lombardi · National Institute of Standards and Technology (NIST)

Chapter 19 · 15 figures, 4 tables, 28 references

This chapter is the English companion to the Vietnamese translation of Chapter 19, “Frequency Measurement” by Michael A. Lombardi (NIST). All fifteen figures are redrawn as vectors and the four tables (19.1–19.4) and equations 19.1–19.8 are reproduced. The Vietnamese prose is not repeated here.

Frequency is the rate of a repetitive event, f = 1/T, in hertz. Frequency calibration compares a device under test (DUT — usually a quartz, rubidium or cesium oscillator) against a reference that outperforms it by a test uncertainty ratio (TUR), preferably 10:1. The result states the DUT’s frequency offset and uncertainty relative to its nameplate frequency; the reference must be traceable to NIST through an unbroken chain, delivered in practice by transfer standards (WWV/WWVB, LORAN-C, GPS) because oscillators are disturbed by shipping and power cycling.

Section 19.2 — uncertainty and stability. Frequency offset f_offset = −Δt/T (Eq. 19.1); +1 µs over 24 h gives −1.16 × 10⁻¹¹ (Eq. 19.2); a 5 MHz oscillator high by 1.16 × 10⁻¹¹ is off by 5.80 × 10⁻⁵ Hz (Eqs. 19.3–19.4). Table 19.1 tabulates offset vs phase deviation and measurement period. Figure 19.1 shows two phase-comparison graphs (clean = TUR ≥ 10:1; wavy = TUR < 10:1). A 2σ test means a 95.4% probability the offset stays within limits. Stability is how well the frequency is held, not whether it is right (Figure 19.2); the time-domain statistic is the Allan deviation σ_y(τ) = √[(1/2(M−1))Σ(yᵢ₊₁−yᵢ)²] (Eq. 19.5), worked through in Table 19.2 (Eq. 19.6, σ_y(1s)=1.17×10⁻¹¹). Confidence ≈ 1/√M × 100% (Eq. 19.7). Figure 19.3 shows an Allan-deviation graph flattening at the flicker floor; Figure 19.4 relates accuracy and stability.

Section 19.3 — frequency standards. Quartz oscillators (Figure 19.5) use the piezoelectric effect; OCXO/TCXO/MCXO trade temperature performance for cost, and aging is a systematic drift (high-quality units age at 5 × 10⁻⁹/year). Atomic oscillators lock a quartz to an atomic resonance, f = (E₂−E₁)/h (Eq. 19.8): rubidium (6.834682608 GHz ⁸⁷Rb, 5×10⁻¹⁰…5×10⁻¹², $3–8k), cesium (9.192631770 GHz ¹³³Cs, primary standard, NIST-7 ±5×10⁻¹⁵, $30–80k, beam-tube life 3–10 y), and hydrogen maser (1.420405752 GHz, best short-term stability, >$200k). Table 19.3 compares all types.

Section 19.4 — transfer standards. Radio-path variation limits short-term stability but averages out for uncertainty. Table 19.4 gives traceability: HF (WWV) ±1×10⁻⁷, LF (WWVB, LORAN-C) ±1×10⁻¹², GPS ±5×10⁻¹³ over 24 h. WWVB (60 kHz) and LORAN-C (100 kHz, groundwave third-cycle tracking, TOC pulses) are described; Figures 19.6–19.7 show LORAN-C path noise (±200 ns) and a 96 h cesium calibration (cesium low by 3.4×10⁻¹²). GPS: 24 satellites, L1/L2, C/A and P codes, SA ~300 ns; Figures 19.8–19.9 show 100 s (<1 ns) and 1-week (550 ns range, cesium low by 5×10⁻¹³) comparisons.

Section 19.5 — calibration methods. Oscillator outputs come as sine, square, pulse or clipped sine (Figure 19.10); one sine cycle is 2π of phase (Figure 19.11). A phase comparison with a changing phase relationship gives the offset (Figure 19.12). A frequency counter is simple but limited by its timebase. The time-interval method uses a TIC (Figures 19.13–19.14): start/stop inputs, timebase, main gate and counter; resolution is improved by interpolation (1 ns common, 10 ps available). Dead time and divider instability limit short-term stability. The Dual Mixer Time Difference method (Figure 19.15) mixes the DUT and reference with a synthesizer to produce beat frequencies, then uses a zero-crossing detector, event counter and TIC — reducing noise to ±5 ps or less. Section 19.6 — future: cesium fountains, trapped-ion standards (±1×10⁻¹⁸), satellite-based transfer, LORAN-C phase-out, GPS improvement when SA ends.
