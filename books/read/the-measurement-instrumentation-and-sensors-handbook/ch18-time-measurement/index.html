Time Measurement

Michael A. Lombardi · National Institute of Standards and Technology (NIST)

Chapter 18 · 3 figures, 8 tables, 32 references

This chapter is the English companion to the Vietnamese translation of Chapter 18, “Time Measurement” by Michael A. Lombardi (NIST). All three figures are redrawn as vectors and the eight tables (18.1–18.8) and equation 18.1 are reproduced. The Vietnamese prose is not repeated here.

Time measurements fall into two categories: time-of-day (a labelled instant: year, month, day, hour, minute, second) and time interval (the elapsed time between two events). Time interval is one of the four basic measurement standards and can be measured with the highest resolution and lowest uncertainty. Timekeeping needs a periodic event (resonator), an energy source, a counter and a display — together a clock, and the resulting ordered scheme a time scale.

Section 18.1 — the evolution of clocks. Every clock has a resonator (the pendulum, the quartz crystal, the atom), an energy source, a counter and a display. Table 18.1 relates frequency uncertainty to timing uncertainty over 1 s, 1 h and 1 day. From sundials (3500 BC) through verge escapements (14th c., ±15 min/day), Huygens’s pendulum (1656, <1 min/day), Harrison’s marine chronometer (1761, ~1/3 s/day), the Shortt two-pendulum clock (1921, a few s/year) to the quartz crystal clock (1927, ±100 µs/day), each advance sought a more stable resonator. Quartz relies on a size-dependent mechanical vibration, is sensitive to environment, and so gave way to atomic oscillators. Table 18.2 summarises the evolution.

Section 18.2 — atomic oscillators use quantised energy levels: the resonance frequency is f=(E₂−E₁)/h (Eq. 18.1). Three types: rubidium (6.835 GHz Rb87, ±5×10⁻¹⁰…±5×10⁻¹², $2–8k), cesium beam (9.1926 GHz, defines the SI second; NIST-7 ±5×10⁻¹⁵; commercial ≥±5×10⁻¹², $30–80k), and the hydrogen maser (1.42 GHz, best performance but >$200k). Section 18.3 — the SI second is the duration of 9,192,631,770 periods of the Cs-133 hyperfine transition; TAI is the atomic scale kept by BIPM (≈250 standards from >40 labs). Before atomic time, astronomical scales were used: the mean solar second (1/86,400 of a mean solar day) underlies Universal Time — UT0, UT1 (±3 ms/day), UT2 (smoothed). The ephemeris second (1956–1971) was a fraction of the tropical year; ET was replaced by TT = TAI + 32.184 s (±10 µs).

Section 18.4 — UTC differs from TAI by an integer number of seconds; leap seconds (on 30 June or 31 December) keep UTC within ±0.9 s of UT1. A positive leap second produces a 61-second minute (23:59:59 → 23:59:60 → 00:00:00); DUT1 = UT1 − UTC is broadcast. Section 18.5 — time transfer needs an on-time 1 pps pulse and a time code. The dominant error is path delay (~3.3 µs/km). Five methods: (1) one-way, user-calibrated (Figure 18.1); (2) one-way, self-calibrating (advance the signal or compute τ_ab in software); (3) common-view (Figure 18.2); (4) two-way (Figure 18.3); (5) loop-back. IRIG-B time codes (1 s frame, BCD, dc-level or 1000 Hz AM) are the de-facto redistribution standard.

Section 18.6 — radio time signals. HF (WWV/WWVH on 2.5/5/10/15/20 MHz) gives worldwide coverage with cheap receivers, a voice announcement and a 100 Hz IRIG-H time code, best to ~1 s (<30 ms). LF (WWVB 60 kHz, LORAN-C 100 kHz) can often be received indoors; WWVB sends a BCD code by 10 dB carrier-power drops, LORAN-C gives on-time pulses at a TOC (no code, ±500 ns). GOES satellites relay a 50-bit/0.5 s time code (±100 µs with path correction). GPS: 24 satellites in six planes, L1 1575.42 MHz / L2 1227.6 MHz, C/A and P PRN codes, Selective Availability (SA) adds ~300 ns. One-way SPS: ±115 ns (50%), ±170 ns (1σ), ±340 ns (2σ) — Table 18.3; common-view GPS reaches ±5 ns. Tables 18.4 and 18.5 summarise signal performance and radio-clock makers.

Section 18.7 — computer time. Dial-up ACTS sends an ASCII time code with an on-time marker advanced 45 ms (loop-back calibration to ±5 ms); Table 18.6 decodes the ACTS code and Table 18.7 lists dial-up services. Network services use the Time (port 37), Daytime (port 13), NTP and SNTP (port 123) protocols — Table 18.8. Section 18.8 — future developments: cesium fountains (laser-cooled, tossed atoms) and trapped-ion standards (potentially ±1×10⁻¹⁸); satellite-based transfer will dominate, LORAN-C phased out, GPS improving if SA ends, GLONASS and INMARSAT emerging.
