Pressure and Sound Measurement

K. H.-L. Chau (Analog Devices) · R. Goehner · E. Drubetsky · H. M. Brady · W. H. Bayles Jr. (The Fredericks Company) · P. C. Pedersen (Worcester Polytechnic Institute)

This chapter presents the principles of pressure and sound (ultrasound) measurement. Section 26.1 describes pressure-sensing elements, detection methods (capacitive, piezoresistive) and silicon micromachined pressure sensors together with their limitations. Section 26.2 covers vacuum measurement — from direct-reading gages (mercury, Bourdon, diaphragm) to indirect-reading gages (thermal conductivity, ionization, molecular drag, mass spectrometry). Section 26.3 introduces basic ultrasound parameters, wave propagation, reflection/refraction at interfaces, transducer radiation fields, attenuation measurement, piezoelectric transducers, Doppler and the diffraction integrals.

26.1 Pressure Measurement Đo áp suất

Pressure is defined as the normal force per unit area exerted by a fluid (liquid or gas) on any surface. Absolute, gage and differential pressures are often written psia, psig and psid; in other unit systems this distinction is usually not made. A second class of units expresses pressure through the height of a static liquid column, e.g. the millimetre of mercury at 0 °C (mm Hg, also the torr) or the inch of water at 4 °C.

The pressure p developed at the base of a liquid column by its weight is:

(26.1)p = ρ g h

where ρ is the liquid density, g is the acceleration of gravity and h is the column height. A conversion table for the most popular pressure units is given in Table 26.1.

26.1.1 Sensing Principles Các nguyên lý cảm biến

Sensing Elements Các phần tử cảm biến

Because pressure is force per area, the most direct way to measure it is to isolate an area on an elastic element for the force to act on. The deformation of the sensing element produces displacements and strains that are sensed to give a calibrated pressure reading; this underlies essentially all commercial pressure sensors today. The elements group into diaphragms, capsules, bellows and tubes, as shown in Figure 26.1.

Flat and corrugated diaphragms deflect under pressure; a capsule joins two diaphragms; a bellows extends axially; Bourdon tubes (C-shaped, twisted, helical, spiral) convert pressure into free-end motion. Their stiffness and travel set the range and sensitivity.

Detection Methods Các phương pháp phát hiện

Capacitive Pressure Sensors Cảm biến áp suất điện dung

In a capacitive sensor a metal or silicon diaphragm is both the pressure-sensing element and one plate of a capacitor; pressure deflects the diaphragm, changing the plate spacing and hence the capacitance. Figure 26.2 shows the single-capacitor and differential designs. The differential design rejects common-mode effects such as temperature and reference pressure.

Figure 26.3 shows a practical differential capacitive sensing cell with isolating diaphragms and silicone oil; Figure 26.4 is a rugged industrial capacitive sensor built on that cell with readout electronics in the housing.

Piezoresistive Pressure Sensors Cảm biến áp điện trở

Piezoresistive (strain-gage) sensors are the most common type in use. The piezoresistive effect is a change in electrical resistance of a material under stress or strain. The gage factor G expresses the sensitivity — the fractional change in resistance ΔR/R per unit strain:

(26.2)G = (ΔR ⁄ R) ⁄ ε

with strain ε = ΔL/L. Two cases are distinguished: strain parallel to the current (longitudinal gage factor) and strain perpendicular to it (transverse gage factor). Figure 26.5 shows a typical silicon piezoresistive sensor: an array of diffused piezo-resistors on a diaphragm.

Many commercial packages suit printed-circuit-board mounting or have isolating diaphragms in stainless-steel housings, as in Figure 26.6.

A typical signal-conditioning circuit uses a voltage-divider bridge (Figure 26.7) to convert the resistance change into a voltage. Because the silicon gage factor is always negative, temperature compensation can be arranged in the bridge.

Silicon Micromachined Pressure Sensors Cảm biến áp suất vi cơ bằng silicon

Micromachined silicon sensors use microelectronic fabrication to build the diaphragm and the resistors or capacitor plates on one chip, giving small size, low cost and integrated electronics. They are widely used in industrial and automotive pressure measurement.

Limitations Các hạn chế

Silicon piezoresistive sensors suffer two notable thermal effects: (1) the temperature coefficient of resistance (0.06 %/°C to 0.24 %/°C); and (2) the temperature coefficient of the gage factor (–0.06 %/°C to –0.24 %/°C), which lowers pressure sensitivity as temperature rises. Thermal compensation (circuit or algorithm) is needed to hold accuracy over the operating range.

26.2 Vacuum Measurement Đo chân không

Background and History of Vacuum Gages Lịch sử và nền tảng

Vacuum measurement covers pressures below atmospheric. Vacuum gages are broadly direct-reading (mechanical or mercury) or indirect-reading (usually electronic). Figure 26.8 classifies the gages in current use; Figure 26.9 shows their operating-pressure ranges on a log scale.

Direct Reading Gages Các áp kế đọc trực tiếp

Mercury manometers measure a column difference (Figure 26.10); the straight-tube manometer covers up to several thousand psi. The McLeod gage (Figure 26.11) compresses the gas before measurement and serves as a primary standard with a liquid-nitrogen trap, down to ~10⁻⁶ torr. The Bourdon gage (Figure 26.12) reads above atmospheric to thousands of psi. The diaphragm gage (Figure 26.13) uses an elastic diaphragm as one capacitor plate (capacitive) or with a strain gage.

Indirect Reading Gages Các áp kế đọc gián tiếp

Thermal-conductivity gages measure the heat lost from a hot wire to the gas; the heat-transfer coefficient depends on the Knudsen number. Figure 26.14 shows the regimes; Figure 26.15 the Pirani gage; Figure 26.16 its calibration curves (gas dependent). A linear relation between molecular density and ion current defines a sensitivity factor S:

(26.3)I_i = S · I_e · P

with I_i the ion current (A), I_e the electron current (A), P the pressure and S the sensitivity (reciprocal pressure). The thermocouple gage (Figures 26.17, 26.18) and convection gage (Figures 26.19, 26.20) also exploit heat loss, the latter extending the range via convection.

Ionization gages ionize the gas with electrons; the collected ion current is proportional to pressure. The triode gage (Figures 26.21, 26.22) connects filament, grid and collector. The hot-cathode Bayard–Alpert gage (Figures 26.23, 26.24) uses a thin collector for very low ranges (~10⁻¹¹ torr). The extractor (Figure 26.25) and Penning (Figure 26.26) gages and the magnetic drag/magnetron types (Figures 26.27, 26.28) extend into the UHV region.

In the molecular-flow region the pressure is given approximately by the spinning-rotor (molecular drag) gage relation:

(26.4)P = (ρ a² ⁄ c) (ω′ ⁄ ω)

Partial-pressure measurement uses a quadrupole mass spectrometer (Figures 26.29, 26.30), giving a spectrum of intensity versus mass number.

26.3 Ultrasound Measurement Đo siêu âm

Applications of Ultrasound Các ứng dụng của siêu âm

Ultrasound (above 20 kHz) is used in NDE, flow metering, medical imaging (cardiac, abdominal), thickness/level gauging, cleaning, welding and lithotripsy. Its ability to penetrate liquids and solids and reflect at interfaces makes it especially useful for measurement.

Definition of Basic Ultrasound Parameters Định nghĩa các tham số siêu âm cơ bản

Acoustic waves are mechanical vibrations and need a medium (they cannot exist in vacuum). For a single-frequency (continuous-wave, CW) sound, the relation among frequency f, sound speed c₀ and wavelength λ is:

(26.5)λ = c₀ ⁄ f

The specific acoustic impedance z of a lossless medium:

(26.6)z = ρ₀ c₀

with ρ₀ the density (kg/m³). Acoustic impedance links particle velocity u to acoustic pressure p:

(26.7)z = p ⁄ u

At an interface between media of acoustic impedances z_i and z_t, the pressure and intensity reflection coefficients R_p and R_I are:

(26.8)R_p = (z_i − z_t) ⁄ (z_i + z_t); R_I = 4 z_i z_t ⁄ (z_i + z_t)²

The corresponding pressure and intensity transmission coefficients T_p and T_I:

(26.9)T_p = 2 z_t ⁄ (z_i + z_t); T_I = 4 z_i z_t ⁄ (z_i + z_t)²

Conceptual Description Mô tả khái niệm

Ultrasound imaging is formed by insonifying an object with a focused beam and recording the echoes reflected from its front and back surfaces versus arrival time (Figure 26.31). Echo strength and arrival time are used to build a B-mode image.

A pulse-echo ultrasound system (Figure 26.32) comprises a pulser, amplifier, transducer, receiver and processing/display block. A transmission system (Figure 26.33) uses separate transmit and receive transducers on opposite sides of the object.

Sound Velocity Vận tốc âm

For an ideal gas:

(26.10)c₀ = √(γ P₀ ⁄ ρ₀) = √(γ R T_K)

with γ = C_P/C_V; for air at 0 °C, c₀ ≈ 331.6 m/s. Its temperature dependence:

(26.11)c₀ = c_ref (1 + T ⁄ 273)

For liquids, using the adiabatic bulk modulus K_s:

(26.12)c₀ = √(K_s ⁄ ρ₀)

For pure water, the sound speed as a function of temperature and pressure (approximate, 1–100 atm):

(26.13)c(T, P₀) ≈ 1402 + 4.8 T − 0.055 T² + 2.3×10⁻⁴ T³ + 1.34×10⁻² P₀

For longitudinal waves in a solid (Lamé constants λ, μ):

(26.14)c_L = √((λ + 2μ) ⁄ ρ₀)

Wave Propagation in Homogeneous Media Lan truyền sóng trong môi trường đồng nhất

The general wave equation:

(26.15)∇² p = (1 ⁄ c₀²) ∂²p ⁄ ∂t²

The harmonic plane-wave solution:

(26.16)p(r,t) = A e^{j(ω t − k·r)} + B e^{j(ω t + k·r)}

with wavenumber:

(26.17)k = ω ⁄ c₀ = 2π ⁄ λ
(26.18)k = k_x x̂ + k_y ŷ + k_z ẑ
(26.19)k_z = √(k² − k_x² − k_y²)

Plane wave in the +k direction:

(26.20)p(r,t) = A e^{j(ω t − k·r)}

Spherical waves from a source at the origin:

(26.21)p(r,t) = (A ⁄ r) e^{j(ω t − k r)} + (B ⁄ r) e^{j(ω t + k r)}
(26.22)p(r,t) = (A ⁄ r) e^{j(ω t − k r)}

Acoustic Intensity and Sound Levels Cường độ âm và mức âm

The acoustic intensity I (mean power per unit area):

(26.23)I = P² ⁄ (2 ρ₀ c₀) = ½ ρ₀ c₀ U²
(26.24)SPL (dB) = 20 log₁₀(P ⁄ P_ref)
(26.25)IL (dB) = 10 log₁₀(I ⁄ I_ref)
(26.26)L_p = 20 log₁₀(P_rms ⁄ P_ref)

Reflection and Refraction at Boundaries Phản xạ và khúc xạ tại giao diện

The boundary conditions give Snell's law:

(26.27)sin θ_i ⁄ c₁ = sin θ_r ⁄ c₁ = sin θ_t ⁄ c₂
(26.28)θ_i = θ_r ; θ_t = arcsin((c₂ ⁄ c₁) sin θ_i)

The critical incident angle (only when c₁ < c₂):

(26.29)sin θ_c = c₁ ⁄ c₂

The Rayleigh reflection coefficient (at angle):

(26.30)R(θ) = (z₂ cos θ_i − z₁ cos θ_t) ⁄ (z₂ cos θ_i + z₁ cos θ_t)

Transmission through a single layer under normal incidence:

(26.31)R_layer = ((z₂² − z₁ z₃) ⁄ (z₂² + z₁ z₃)) · sin(k₂ L)
(26.32)T_layer = 1 − R_layer²

100 % energy transmission (R = 0) requires:

(26.33)z₂ = √(z₁ z₃) ; k₂ L = nπ + π ⁄ 2

Attenuation Measurement Đo suy hao

Attenuation (dB/cm) is measured by transmission or pulse-echo:

(26.34)Att = (10 ⁄ d) log₁₀(∫ v₁²(t) dt ⁄ ∫ v₂²(t) dt)
(26.35)Att = (10 ⁄ ΔT) log₁₀(∫ v_f²(t) dt ⁄ ∫ v_b²(t) dt)

CW Fields from Planar Piston Sources Trường CW từ nguồn piston phẳng

The field of a planar piston transducer splits into a near field and a far field. The near-field length:

(26.36)NF = a² ⁄ λ

The divergence angle approximately:

(26.37)θ = arcsin(0.61 λ ⁄ a)

The axial pressure (baffled piston):

(26.38)P_ax = 2 ρ₀ c₀ U₀ |sin(k a² ⁄ (4 r))|
(26.39)p(r,θ) = j ρ₀ c₀ U₀ (k a² ⁄ (2 r)) e^{j(k r − ω t)} · (2 J₁(ka sinθ) ⁄ (ka sinθ))

The directivity D:

(26.40)D = 4π I(θ) ⁄ P_rad
(26.41)D ≈ [2 J₁(ka) ⁄ (ka)]²

Selection Criteria for Ultrasound Frequencies Tiêu chí chọn tần số

Higher frequency gives better spatial resolution but greater attenuation and shorter range. Low frequencies suit long range or lossy media; high frequencies suit detailed shallow imaging.

Basic Parameters in Ultrasound Measurements Các tham số cơ bản trong đo siêu âm

Measured parameters include sound speed, attenuation, acoustic impedance, reflection coefficient, bandwidth, directivity and axial/lateral resolution — determined from the equations above together with transducer characteristics.

Theory and Applications Lý thuyết và ứng dụng

The Doppler effect: the Doppler frequency f_d for scatterers moving at velocity v, at angle θ to the beam:

(26.45)f_d = 2 v cos θ ⁄ c₀

CW Doppler measures frequency but not range; pulsed-wave (PW) Doppler measures both velocity and range and is subject to aliasing. A Doppler spectrum (via FFT) arises with a velocity distribution. The Fresnel diffraction integral (paraxial):

(26.46)p(x₀,y₀,z,t) = (j A₀ ⁄ (λ z)) e^{j(ω t − k z)} ∫∫_S u(x,y) e^{j (k⁄(2z))((x−x₀)²+(y−y₀)²)} dx dy

The Fraunhofer (far-field) approximation:

(26.47)p(x₀,y₀,z,t) = (j A₀ ⁄ (λ z)) e^{j(ω t − k z)} e^{j (k⁄(2z))(x₀²+y₀²)} ∫∫_S u(x,y) e^{−j (k⁄z)(x x₀ + y y₀)} dx dy

The Rayleigh integral (impulse technique):

(26.48)h(r,t) = (1 ⁄ (2π)) ∫∫_S (δ(t − r′ ⁄ c₀) ⁄ r′) dS
(26.49)φ(r,t) = h(r,t) ⊗ u(t)
(26.50)u(r,t) = ∇ φ(r,t)
(26.51)p(r,t) = − ρ₀ ∂φ(r,t) ⁄ ∂t

The received velocity from a high-impedance reflector:

(26.52)v(r,t) = A₀ cos[ψ(r)] · h(r, t − 2R ⁄ c₀)

Review of Applications Tổng quan ứng dụng

Ultrasound measures thickness, distance, fluid velocity (Doppler), elastic modulus, defects (NDE) and medical/tissue images. Acoustic microscopy (SAM) inspects microstructure non-destructively.
