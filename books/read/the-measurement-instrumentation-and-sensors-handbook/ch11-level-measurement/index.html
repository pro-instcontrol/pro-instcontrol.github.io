Level Measurement

Detlef Brumbi · Krohne Messtechnik GmbH

Chapter 11 · 15 figures, 3 tables, 23 equations

This chapter is a companion to the Vietnamese translation of Chapter 11. It carries all fifteen figures redrawn as vectors, the two tables of instruments and prices, and the argument the chapter makes about which physical effect each family of instruments actually exploits. The prose of the translation — the displacer algebra, the radar equation, the FMCW ramp, the capacitance of a concentric probe — is not repeated here.

Level is a distance, measured backwards

Brumbi opens with a definition that looks trivial and is not: level is the filling height, and almost every instrument on the market measures something else and converts. A radar on the roof measures d, the distance from the flange to the product, and the level is L = h − d with the tank height h held constant. Everything that goes wrong with level measurement in practice goes wrong in that subtraction: h is not constant when the roof sags, d is not the distance to the surface when the beam reflects off a agitator blade, and the surface is not a plane when the product foams.

The figure is worth reading as a taxonomy rather than a sketch. Four mounting positions, and each one implies a different error budget. (a) contacts the product, so it inherits its temperature, its corrosivity and its coating behaviour. (b) touches nothing, so it inherits only the vapour space — but it also inherits everything that floats in that space. (c) is intrusive, which is the only way to measure a dielectric property. (d) is a switch, and a switch is not a cheap level transmitter: it answers a different question, and §11.3 is entirely about devices that answer that different question.

Density first: the buoyancy family

§11.1 is the oldest technology in the chapter and still the one that survives in the nastiest service, because a displacer has no window, no antenna and no electronics in contact with the process. The algebra is three lines: weight F_G = ρ_A g A b, buoyancy F_B on the dipped length plus the dry remainder, and the resultant F_R = F_G − F_B, which collapses to F_R = g A L_d (ρ_A − ρ_L). The instrument measures F_R and solves for L_d.

Two consequences follow from that last expression and neither is obvious from the text alone. First, the body must be denser than the liquid, or the instrument floats and the range ends early — which is why displacers are long, thin and heavy rather than short and fat. Second, the sensitivity dF_R/dL is g A (ρ_A − ρ_L), so a displacer sized for a light hydrocarbon loses most of its span when the same tank is filled with water. The device is not mis-calibrated; it is answering a question whose scale factor changed.

Figure 11.3 is the clever variant. Instead of measuring a force with a strain gauge, it hangs a small ball on a wire, drives the wire at resonance and reads the frequency. For a stretched string f = (1/2l)√(F/ρ_W A_W), so F = 4 ρ_W A_W l² f² — a force-to-frequency conversion with no analogue electronics in the loop and a resolution set by the clock, not by the amplifier. The same wire can then be stepped up and down to find interfaces, which makes one instrument do level, interface and density.

Floats are the mirror image: the body is lighter than the liquid, so it rides the surface and the output is a position rather than a force. The two drawings in Figure 11.4 are the two ways that position is read out — mechanically, through a cable over a pulley to a counterweight, or magnetically, through the wall of a guide tube to a reed contact. The reed version is the one that survives: no packing gland, no cable to stretch, and stacking several reeds in the tube gives a coarse but genuinely continuous indication.

Hydrostatic measurement is the third member of the family and the one with the most instructive failure mode. p = p₀ + ρ_L g L is exact; the problem is that ρ_L is not a constant. §11.1 offers three answers, and the three panels of Figure 11.5 are those three answers. (a) vents the low side of a differential cell to the roof, which removes barometric pressure but not density. (b) puts a second sensor a fixed distance up the tank, so that ρ_L = (p₂ − p₁)/g l is measured rather than assumed, and the level follows from the ratio of two pressure differences — hydrostatic tank gaging, the standard method for custody transfer. (c) solves a different problem: when the bottom of the tank is inaccessible, a bubble tube purges inert gas down a pipe and reads the back pressure at the inlet, so the sensor sits on the roof and only the pipe touches the product.

The balance method closes §11.1 and is included mainly to be dismissed. Weighing the whole tank gives L = (F − F₀)/[(ρ_L − ρ_A) g A] exactly, and gives nonsense outdoors, where wind and the weight of snow and rain load the load cells. It is the right method for a small day tank indoors and the wrong one for a field storage tank, and the chapter says so in one sentence.

Time of flight: one equation, three carriers

§11.2 is the modern half of the chapter and it rests on t = 2d/v. Everything else is a choice of carrier — 340 m/s of sound, 3×10⁸ m/s of light — and every consequence follows from that ratio. Table 11.1 is the whole argument in three rows.

Ultrasonic340 m s⁻¹50 kHz7 mm1 ms
Radar300,000 km s⁻¹10 GHz3 cm1 ns
Laser300,000 km s⁻¹300 THz1 µm1 ns

Table 11.1 · Properties of the wave types used for time-of-flight level measurement.

Read the last column against the first. A 1 mm resolution needs a 6 µs round trip in air and a 7 ps round trip in a radar beam — six orders of magnitude apart. That single number explains why ultrasonic instruments count pulses with a microcontroller and radar instruments need either equivalent-time sampling or, more usually, a modulation scheme that moves the measurement into the frequency domain.

Figure 11.6 is the geometry and the timing that every later figure reuses. Note what the drawing makes explicit and the text only implies: the beam is a cone, not a line, so the echo comes back from an area rather than a point. That area is why a small radiation angle matters — a wide cone sees the tank wall, the agitator and the filling stream as well as the surface.

The three antennas in Figure 11.7 are three answers to the same requirement: put as much aperture as possible into a flange-sized hole. The horn is the cheap answer and the one that collects condensation. The dielectric rod is the answer for small tanks and corrosive vapours, because the radiating element is inert plastic rather than a metal cavity. The Cassegrain is the answer when the beam must be narrow enough to miss an agitator — a subreflector lets the effective aperture exceed the flange diameter, at the cost of a surface that must be kept clean.

Figure 11.8 is the packaging: microwave circuit, signal processing, microprocessor, display, supply and output, all above the flange in one housing. It is worth noting how much of that list is not microwave engineering. The RF chain is a handful of components; the rest is the same digital hardware that any transmitter carries, which is why radar level instruments became affordable in the 1990s rather than the 1970s.

The radar equation P_R = P_T G_T R G_R / D² is then simplified twice in the text, and both simplifications matter. First, R depends only on the permittivity of the product, R = [(√ε_r − 1)/(√ε_r + 1)]², so a product with ε_r ≈ 2 returns about 3 % of the incident power while a product with ε_r ≈ 80 returns about 64 %. That is the entire explanation for why radar works beautifully on water and badly on dry cement. Second, because the reflecting area fills the beam, D² scales as d², so P_R falls as 1/d² rather than 1/d⁴ — the reason a tank radar reaches 40 m with a fraction of a milliwatt.

Figure 11.9 is the guided-wave case, and it is the clearest drawing in the chapter. A pulse is launched down two wires; every change in permittivity reflects part of it. The surface gives the first echo, the interface between two liquids the second, the end of the line the third. Because the end of the line is a known distance away, it serves as the reference that calibrates the velocity — which is how TDR keeps its accuracy when the vapour above the product changes composition.

Figures 11.10 and 11.11 are the FMCW pair, and they are the heart of the chapter. The trick is to stop trying to time a 7 ps round trip and instead sweep the transmitter linearly in frequency. A delayed echo then arrives at a frequency that is offset from the current transmit frequency by Δf = (F/T)·t, a few kilohertz — easily counted. The distance follows directly: d = f c T / (2F). Figure 11.11 shows why the block diagram is more than a mixer: the VCO has to be linear, so its instantaneous frequency is measured against a DRO and the microprocessor closes a loop on the sweep. Everything expensive in an FMCW radar is in that linearity, not in the antenna.

Figure 11.12 is the odd one out and the most elegant idea in the chapter. If the reflection from the surface is weak, let the wave go through the product and reflect off the bottom instead. The echo comes back late, because the wave travels at c/√ε_r in the liquid, so the bottom appears to be further away than it is: (h_v − d)/(h − d) = √ε_r, and therefore L = (h_v − h)/(√ε_r − 1). The measurement is now a direct level measurement rather than a distance measurement, and it still works when the surface echo is unusable. Krohne sell this as “tank bottom tracing”.

Physical properties: the switch business

§11.3 changes the question. Instead of measuring a height continuously, it asks whether the product is present at one point, and it answers by detecting a property that differs sharply between gas and liquid: permittivity, conductivity, radiation attenuation, thermal conductivity, viscosity. Most of these devices are switches, and a switch is a legitimate instrument — an overfill protection loop needs a switch, not a transmitter.

The capacitive probe is the one worth doing the algebra for. For a concentric geometry the capacitance is a sum of two cylindrical condensers in series — the insulation and the product — which is Equation 11.19. The two limiting cases are the useful ones: a conductive product shorts out the second term and leaves C proportional to L through the insulation alone (11.20), while a bare electrode in a conductive liquid is really a conductivity cell, which is why 11.21 has ε₂ rather than ε₁ in it.

Radiometric gaging is the only method in the chapter that works through a blind wall, and Figure 11.14 shows the two configurations that matter: a point source with a rod detector, which gives a continuous profile, and a source array with a point detector, which gives a cheaper average. The exponential I = I₀ exp(−αd) is linearised in practice by calibrating in place, and the 2^(−t/T_H) term is not optional — a Cs-137 source loses 2,3 % of its activity a year, and an instrument that ignores that will drift out of tolerance without anything failing.

The vibrating fork closes the section and is the most-sold level switch in the world. A piezo bender drives the fork at resonance; when the paddles are immersed, the viscosity of the product damps the vibration and the electronics see the change in drive power or in resonant frequency. It is a point switch, it is insensitive to foam, and it is the reason §11.3 exists at all: a great many level measurements in a plant are really the question “is there anything here?”, and a fork answers it for the price of a pressure gauge.

Two readings. Accuracy costs money roughly as a logarithm: 10 mm is $500, 1 mm is $3,000, 0,1 mm is $10,000 — and the last step buys a laser, which fails in dust. Contactless costs a premium over contact: radar at $3,000 against a displacer at $2,000 for the same 1 mm. And the three switch technologies all sit at $500, which is the real message of the table: if the question is yes/no, the answer is cheap, and the expensive part of the specification is deciding that the question really is yes/no.
