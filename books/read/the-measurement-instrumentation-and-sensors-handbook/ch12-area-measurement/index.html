Area Measurement

Charles B. Coulbourn · Wolfgang P. Buerner · Los Angeles Scientific Instrumentation Co.

Chapter 12 · 10 figures, 2 tables, 8 equations

This chapter is a companion to the Vietnamese translation of Chapter 12. It carries all ten figures redrawn as vectors — the polar and linear planimeters, the measuring-wheel geometry, the parallelogram argument, the four-sided trace, the grid overlay, and the three digitizer families — plus the two manufacturer tables. The prose of the translation is not repeated here.

Planimeters are mechanical integrators. The tracer arm carries a pointer that follows the boundary of the area; one end of the arm is constrained to a fixed track — a circular arc for the polar type (Figure 12.1a) and a straight rail for the linear type (12.1b). The measuring wheel records only the component of motion perpendicular to its axis, so its accumulated count is the integral of sin φ along the path (Equation 12.1), and the enclosed area is the tracer-arm length times that count rescaled by the wheel circumference (12.2).

Figure 12.2 is the heart of the principle: the wheel rotates by an amount proportional to distance travelled times sin φ, where φ is the angle between the wheel axis and the direction of travel. Because the wheel only rolls a fraction of the path, the count is a projection, not the arc length — and that projection, multiplied by the arm length, is exactly the enclosed area. Figure 12.3 shows the same thing as a parallelogram: base = arm length, height = wheel reading converted to distance.

The four-sided trace in Figure 12.4 is the proof. Splitting the figure into four partial areas (12.3)–(12.6) and regrouping the integrals gives (12.7): the integrals for the constrained end cancel because that end runs a closed arc (net area zero), and what remains is exactly the area traced by the free, pointer end. The argument generalises to any number of sides and any shape.

The digitizer route replaces the integrator with coordinate capture. Given the ordered (x, y) boundary points, the area is the shoelace sum (12.8). The subtlety the chapter stresses is the closing term: the last term ties the first and last points together, which matters for an embedded processor with little memory — either store the first point and compute the closing term on a key press, or force the last digitised point to coincide with the first so the term is zero.

Figure 12.5 is the humble grid overlay — place a transparent grid, count squares at least half inside the boundary, multiply by the square area. Accuracy depends on grid size, grid precision and counting care. It is the sanity check every other method is implicitly compared against.

Three digitizer families are described. The tablet (Figure 12.10) senses the pointer against a wire grid embedded in the work surface, either by magnetostriction (a strain wave whose propagation time gives the distance) or by a 57.6 kHz signal whose amplitude profile peaks at the pointer. The sonic digitizer uses a spark-gap pointer and two or more microphones; the slant ranges to known microphone positions give x and y, with ambiguities resolved by extra microphones or by keeping the ambiguous points outside the work area. The arm digitizer (Figure 12.9) is a two-link arm with a rotary encoder at each joint, each providing more than 36,000 counts per revolution in quadrature; it reports (x, y) relative to an origin and rotation the user defines.

Section 12.2 is a buyer's guide. The manual polar planimeter (Figure 12.6) is the cheapest and simplest, preferred for occasional use; arm length is adjustable only for convenience, never for calibration, and the wheel is zeroed with a knurled wheel or a plunger. The electronic planimeters (Figure 12.7) add an optical encoder and a digital readout, and convert the reading to square feet, square metres, acres or hectares. The battery-operated models (Figure 12.8) package the processor on the pole arm and average multiple readings, but cannot measure length. Section 12.3 is the decision rule, and it is the most useful part of the chapter. Below an equivalent circle of about 2 cm diameter the digitizer wins, because the planimeter's resolution element becomes a large fraction of the area. Between 2 cm and 55 cm either instrument is excellent. Above 55 cm the planimeter still works if you subdivide the area into pieces; the digitizer reaches further but hits the same wall eventually, and then the same subdivision trick applies. The future the authors foresee — microprocessors for slope and tilt correction, statistics, centroids and moments — is exactly what every modern tablet and arm digitizer now does.

The defining-terms list is worth keeping: a planimeter is a mechanical integrator; the pole arm length does not affect the reading while the tracer-arm length is inversely proportional to it; a polar planimeter has its constrained end follow a circle, a linear one a straight line; the resolution element is the smallest discernible area, and for area measurement it has a value of 1.
