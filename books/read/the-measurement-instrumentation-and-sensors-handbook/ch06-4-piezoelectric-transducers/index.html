Piezoelectric Transducers and Sensors

Ahmad Safari, Victor F. Janas, Amit Bandyopadhyay & Andrei Kholkine · Rutgers University

Section 6.4 · 8 figures · about 5,294 words

Section 6.4 is the chapter’s materials-science entry. The other sections describe devices; this one describes why the material inside the device behaves the way it does, and the answer is crystal symmetry. Of the 32 crystallographic point groups, 11 have a centre of symmetry and cannot be piezoelectric; of the remaining 21, twenty show the effect. That single fact explains almost everything downstream — why quartz is weak but stable, why PZT at the morphotropic boundary is strong but temperature-limited, and why a poled ceramic is not the same material as an unpoled one.

Two effects, one tensor Hai hiệu ứng, một tenxơ

The same third-rank tensor dmij describes both directions. Written as a matrix dij it carries units of C·N⁻¹ or, equivalently, m·V⁻¹ — the equivalence is the point: a material that produces charge per newton also produces strain per volt, and the two numbers are the same number. The voltage coefficient gij = dij/(εiiε0) then says something practical: a low-permittivity material gives a large voltage for the same charge, which is exactly why polymer sensors beat ceramics on voltage output despite much weaker d coefficients.

Two entries deserve a second look. PZT at the morphotropic phase boundary (Zr/Ti = 52/48 at room temperature) sits between a tetragonal ferroelectric and an orthorhombic antiferroelectric, and the boundary is where the piezoelectric coefficients peak — which is why essentially all commercial piezoceramics are PZT near that composition. And the relaxor single crystals (PZN–PT, PMN–PT) are in a different league altogether: d₃₃ around 25 × 10⁻¹⁰ C·N⁻¹, k above 0.9, and strains of 1.7 %, an order of magnitude beyond PZT.

Poling, and why it is not optional Phân cực — và vì sao không thể bỏ qua

A ceramic fresh out of the kiln is a mass of randomly oriented polar grains and has no net polarization — it is not piezoelectric. Poling, a strong dc field applied just below the Curie temperature, rotates the domains toward the field. Symmetry prevents full alignment, so the ceramic keeps only a fraction of the single-crystal response, but it keeps it permanently until the material is driven above its Curie point or depoled by a reversed field. This is the same logic as magnetizing a permanent magnet, and the same fragility applies.

Processing decides the properties Chế tạo quyết định tính chất

The flow chart is not decoration. Lead loss above 800 °C is why PZT must be sintered in a sealed crucible with a PbO atmosphere; the cubic-to-tetragonal transition on cooling is why pure PbTiO₃ cracks itself apart and needs Ca, Sr, Ba, Sn or W doping; and the binder burnout at 500–600 °C is what stops residual organics from bloating the ceramic during sintering. Each of these is a place where a process choice becomes an electromechanical property.

Measuring d₃₃ Đo d₃₃

The resonance method is fast and needs only a rod of known dimensions: drive it, find fs and fp, and equation 6.63 gives k₃₃, from which 6.64 gives d₃₃. Its limitation is built in — you can only measure at the frequencies the sample’s own geometry selects. Below resonance, the direct method applies a known force and measures the charge, which is where the equivalent circuits of Figures 6.43 and 6.44 come in.

The voltage amplifier of 6.43 has a fatal flaw for piezoelectric work: its output depends on C, and C includes the cable capacitance, which is neither small nor known. Adding a large standard capacitor fixes the uncertainty but destroys the sensitivity. The charge amplifier of 6.44 removes the problem by holding the inverting input at virtual ground, so the cable capacitance is bootstrapped out and V = −Q/Cf, depending only on d₃₃ and a capacitor you chose yourself.

Why Rf is not optional. The ideal charge amplifier integrates the amplifier’s own input bias current along with the signal, and after a few seconds the output sits against a rail. The shunt resistor gives that current a dc path, at the cost of turning the response into the high-pass form of equation 6.66. The time constant is now set by RfCf alone — independent of the transducer and the cable — which is the practical advantage over a voltage amplifier even though the response is no longer flat to dc.

The two canonical sensors Hai kết cấu tiêu biểu

The bimorph of 6.45(a) solves a mechanical problem rather than an electrical one. Piezoceramics have high Young’s modulus, so a bulk block under a reasonable force produces almost no strain and therefore almost no charge. Making the material long and thin and mounting it as a cantilever trades force for displacement: a small tip movement now bends the beam enough to strain the ceramic. Two beams poled in opposite directions, cemented with a common centre electrode, double the charge, because the upper beam stretches while the lower compresses and the two series-connected outputs add.

The polymer diaphragm of 6.45(b) solves the opposite problem. PVDF has a much smaller d₃₁ than PZT, but a much smaller ε as well, and g = d/ε therefore comes out larger — so for the same pressure the polymer film delivers more volts. That, plus mechanical flexibility and the ability to be made a few micrometres thick, is why polymer film dominates microphone and hydrophone diaphragms even though ceramic dominates actuators. The catch is stated in the text: the advantage only survives if the amplifier has a high input impedance and sits close to the transducer, because a long cable will divide the signal away before it is measured.
