Density Measurement

Halit Eren · Curtin University of Technology

Chapter 21 · 16 figures, 4 equations, 3 references

Density matters in instrumentation for two reasons: determining the mass and volume of a product, and establishing its quality. Density is the mass of a given volume of a substance under fixed conditions, and it varies with pressure and temperature — far more so for gases. Measurements are either static or dynamic (on-line); static methods are better developed, cheaper and more accurate, dynamic samplers are expensive and highly automated. No single technique is universal: specific gravity (SG) is the dimensionless ratio of a substance's density to that of a standard — water for liquids (Eq. 21.1), air for gases (Eq. 21.2) — usually quoted at NTP or STP.

Solid density is simple when the material is uniform: density = mass/volume. Where solids have mixed constituents and changing volumes, dynamic methods such as radioactive absorption and ultrasonics are used. Fluid density is far more complex, and the chapter covers eleven device families: pycnometric, buoyancy, hydrometer, hydrostatic weighing, balance-type, column-type, vibrating element, radioactive, refractometer/index-of-refraction, Coriolis and absorption type.

21.1 Solid Density Khối lượng riêng của chất rắn

Once the volume and mass of a uniform solid are known the density follows directly from the basic ratio, in kg m⁻³. When the material is inhomogeneous or its volume changes, dynamic techniques are required.

21.2 Fluid Density Khối lượng riêng của chất lưu

Pycnometers (Figure 21.1) are static fixed-volume vessels: the bottle is weighed empty, then full of distilled aerated water to establish its volume, then full of the process fluid. A capillary in the stopper fixes the exact volume. Twin capillaries allow vacuum filling. They must be light, strong and nonmagnetic, and high-resolution balances are needed. Advantages: accurate and usable for both density and SG. Disadvantages: operator skill matters, the sample must be taken off-line, and high-precision units are expensive.

Buoyancy densitometers use Archimedes' principle: a sinker of known mass and volume on a fine wire is fully immersed and a precision force balance measures the supporting force, with corrections for surface tension on the wire, the sinker's cubic expansion coefficient and process temperature. They are accurate enough to calibrate other liquid density transducers; the magnetic-suspension version encloses the sinker in a pressure vessel and eliminates surface-tension error.

Hydrometers (Figure 21.2) are the most common liquid densitometers and are covered by standards such as ISO 387. A sealed ballasted bulb with a long graduated stem converts the volume of a fixed mass into a linear distance; the scale reads in kg m⁻³ or in SG, °API, Brix, Brine. ISO 387 covers 600–2000 kg m⁻³. They are cheap, have good resolution over a small span and are traceable, but the span is small, glass is fragile, the sample is off-line, and good precision needs surface-tension and temperature corrections.

Hydrostatic weighing (Figure 21.3) weighs a pivoted U-tube on flexible end couplings, usually stainless bellows, with a force balance. It reads continuously and handles two-phase liquids such as slurries, sugar solutions and powders, but must be mounted horizontally on a solid base, is bulky, and cannot measure gases.

Balance-type densitometers come in four common forms. The balanced-flow vessel (Figure 21.4) weighs a fixed-volume vessel through which liquid flows continuously, using a spring balance or pneumatic force-balance transmitter. The chain balanced float (Figure 21.5) suspends a self-centering fixed-volume plummet entirely below the surface; the chain force depends on plummet position, a transformer core transmits position to a pickup coil, and a resistance thermometer bridge compensates temperature. The gas specific gravity balance weighs a tall gas column against the floating bottom of the vessel. The buoyancy gas balance (Figure 21.6) balances a displacer in air, then in gas at adjusted pressure; the pressure ratio gives SG relative to air — a laboratory method, not for continuous measurement.

Column densitometers use bubbler tubes. The reference column version (Figure 21.7) compares a known head of sample liquid against water with a differential-pressure transmitter; both columns must be at the same temperature. The two-tube version (Figure 21.8) fixes the mouths at different heights, so the differential pressure equals the weight of the liquid column between them and can be calibrated directly against density — accurate to 0.1%–1% SG, but only for liquids that neither crystallize nor settle. The suppression type (Figure 21.9) adds a constant pressure-drop chamber in series with the low-pressure side for better scaling and accuracy.

Vibrating element densitometers exploit the shift in natural frequency as fluid properties change: f = √(K/(M + kρ)) (Eq. 21.3), where K is system stiffness, M transducer mass, k a system constant and ρ fluid density. Elements are driven magnetically (noncontact copper coils, reliable from −200 °C to +200 °C) or piezoelectrically (low cost, high impedance, no magnetic sensors, but bonded with adhesive so mounting strain matters). The vibrating tube (Figure 21.10) suits viscous liquids and slurries, with length about 20 diameters; twin tubes (Figure 21.11) vibrate in antiphase, block the flow far less and are easy to clean. The vibrating cylinder is a thin-walled 3:1 cylinder in a hoop mode, 25–300 µm wall, giving zero pressure coefficient — ideal for liquefied gases and refined liquids, but costly and unsuitable for viscous fluids. The tuning fork (Figure 21.12) uses low-mass forks, either in a small chamber or directly in the liquid, and always needs calibration. Advantages: work for liquids and gases, real-time, inherently digital, robust, programmable versions that output live and reference density, relative density, SG and concentration. Disadvantages: no direct link to primary measurements, and problems with multiphase liquids.

Radioactive densitometers pass γ rays through the sample and count arrivals with ion or scintillation detectors: J = J₀e^(−nρd) (Eq. 21.4), with n the mass absorption coefficient. Mass absorption is largely independent of composition, accuracy reaches ±0.0001 g mL⁻¹, and many units self-diagnose and compensate source decay. A longer path gives a stronger signal, so the pipe is either enlarged (Figure 21.13) or elongated (Figure 21.14), with the shielded source and usually the detector clamped to the wall. Advantages: non-contacting, works on multiphase liquids, programmable, suited to mining and heavy process industries. Disadvantages: a radioactive source must be handled, a minimum path length is needed, time constants can be long, and it suits only solids and liquids.

Refractometers are optical instruments using refraction in liquid media, typically with an in-line sensing window (prism) wetted by the product; they excel in blending and mixing applications and can measure dissolved solids. They are not recommended for suspended solids, high turbidity, entrained air, heavy colour, poor transparency or very high flow rates. The index-of-refraction version (Figure 21.15) measures the lateral displacement of a laser beam crossing the cell at an angle; position-sensitive detectors read the displacement, and interferometers are used for maximum sensitivity. Applications include adulteration control of edible oils, wines and gasoline, plus pulp and paper, food and beverage, sugar, dairy and chemicals.

Coriolis densitometers (Figure 21.16) resemble vibrating tubes: one or two flow tubes in a sensor housing, anchored at inlet and outlet and driven at the free end, using F = ma. The resonant frequency depends on geometry, material and tube-assembly mass, and the fluid mass equals density times the constant tube volume, so frequency relates directly to density. Temperature sensors compensate the change in elastic modulus, and density is computed from a linear relationship with tube period and calibration constants. Absorption densitometers use X-ray, visible, UV or sonic attenuation and phase shift — for example UV/X-ray for local mercury deposit density in arc discharge lamps, and ultrasonic sensors for slurries such as lime, where the probe can be fully immersed in agitated slurry to avoid coating, reaching 0.01% accuracy.
