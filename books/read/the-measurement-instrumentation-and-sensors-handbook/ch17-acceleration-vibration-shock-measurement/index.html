Acceleration, Vibration, and Shock Measurement

Halit Eren · Curtin University of Technology

Chapter 17 · 23 figures, 4 references

This chapter is the English companion to the Vietnamese translation of Chapter 17, “Acceleration, Vibration, and Shock Measurement” by Halit Eren. All twenty-three figures are redrawn as vectors, and the governing equations (17.1–17.47) are reproduced. The translation prose is not repeated here.

Accelerometers measure a general absolute-motion parameter — acceleration — and are also the sensor of choice for vibration and shock. They come as active (self-generating, e.g. piezoelectric) or passive (changing capacitance, needing external power); only passive types measure true static (dc) acceleration. They are preferred over displacement/velocity sensors because of their wide frequency range (down to dc), because destructive forces track acceleration, because transients/shocks are easy to capture, and because velocity and displacement are obtained by integration. They classify as deflection or null-balance, dynamic or kinematic, mechanical or electrical. Figure 17.1 shows the generic deflection-type seismic accelerometer. Calibration is static (tilting table, centrifuge up to >50,000 g) or dynamic (electrodynamic shaker + laser interferometer; back-to-back working standard).

Section 17.1 develops the dynamics. Periodic motion is sinusoidal: x(t)=X_peak·sin(ωt) (Eq. 17.1), u(t)=ω X_peak·cos(ωt) with U_peak=ω X_peak (Eq. 17.2), and a(t)=−ω² X_peak·sin(ωt) with A_peak=ω² X_peak=ω U_peak (Eq. 17.3); velocity leads displacement by 90°, acceleration leads velocity by another 90°. Non-sinusoidal periodic motion expands by Fourier series (Eq. 17.4). Random, transient and nonstationary vibrations are handled statistically (DFT/FFT, spectra, RMS). The seismic mass–spring–damper obeys m ẍ + c ẋ + k x = f(t) (Eq. 17.5) and, including base motion, m z̈ + c ż + k z = m g cosθ − m ẍ₁ (Eq. 17.6). In Laplace form: X(s)/F(s)=1/(m s²+c s+k)=K/(s²+2ζωₙ s+ωₙ²) (Eqs. 17.7–17.8) with ωₙ=√(k/m), ζ=c/(2√(km)). Figures 17.2–17.5 show the log a/v/x relation, the single-DOF pulse response, the unit-step responses for various ζ, and the Bode gain/phase plots. Multi-degree-of-freedom systems show one resonance peak per DOF; mass loading on light test pieces must be corrected.

Section 17.2 — electromechanical force-balance (servo) accelerometers. The displacement of a seismic mass is sensed and a proportional magnetic force restores the null, giving better linearity and no hysteresis than a mechanical spring, plus electrical self-test. Coil-and-magnetic types follow Ampère’s law F=m a=i l B (Eqs. 17.9–17.10); Figure 17.6 shows the basic coil/magnet unit. A rotational servo (Figure 17.7) yields e₀/eₐ=(K_p K_c R/K_s)·Kₐ s²/(s²+2ζωₙₗ s+ωₙₗ²) (Eq. 17.11), which for high loop gain reduces to a≈e₀/(Kₘ R) (Eqs. 17.12–17.13) — accuracy shifts from mechanical parts to a few stable parameters. Induction types (cup-and-magnet, eddy-current torque) are essentially generators; a typical instrument offers 1 µg threshold, 500 Hz natural frequency, ζ=0.3–0.8, 0.1% cross-axis, ±10 V at 30 g.

Section 17.3 — piezoelectric accelerometers. Compression or shear crystals under a proof mass give q=dᵢⱼ F=dᵢⱼ m a (Eq. 17.17); PZT has ~150× quartz’s strain constant. The transfer function is third-order (Eq. 17.18) with very small damping (ζ<0.01); low-frequency response needs large time-constant τ via a high-impedance amplifier. Figures 17.8–17.9 show the compression element and typical PZT frequency response. They cover 0.004 pC/g (250 kHz) to 1000 pC/g (7 kHz), down to 3×3 mm and 0.5 g, but have 2–4% cross-axis sensitivity.

Section 17.4 — piezoresistive accelerometers use semiconductor strain gages (large gage factor) in a Wheatstone bridge, are true static devices, useful below 1 Hz (Figure 17.10). Section 17.5 — differential-capacitance accelerometers: C=kεS/h (Eq. 17.19), h=h₀+m a/K (Eq. 17.20), giving f=f₀+fₐ (Eqs. 17.21–17.25); a diaphragm version (Figure 17.12) uses squeeze-film air damping (ζ≈0.7 stable within 15%). Section 17.6 — strain-gage accelerometers: ΔR/R=ΔL/L+2ν(ΔL/L)+(dρ/ρ)/(dL/L) (Eq. 17.26); bonded or unbonded wire, or semiconductor on a cantilever. Section 17.7 — seismic accelerometers. Newton’s law gives m ẍ₂+c ẋ₂+k x₂=m g cosθ+m ẍ₁ (Eq. 17.27) and, for relative motion, m z̈+c ż+k z=m g cosθ−m ẍ₁ (Eq. 17.28); with x₁(t)=x₁₀ sin(ω₁ t) it becomes m z̈+c ż+k z=m g cosθ+m a₁ sin(ω₁ t) (Eq. 17.30) with z=(m g/k)cosθ+(m a₁/k)·sin(ω₁t−φ)/√((1−r²)²+(2ζr)²) (Eq. 17.31), r=ω₁/ωₙ, φ=tan⁻¹(2ζr/(1−r²)) (Eq. 17.32). At high ω₁/ωₙ the output is linear and k z=m ẍ₁ (Eq. 17.33). Figures 17.13–17.15 show the displacement curve, the potentiometer version, and the strain-gage cantilever.

Section 17.8 — inertial types. A vibrating-string accelerometer (Figure 17.16) has f₁=(1/2l)√(T₁/mₛ), f₂=(1/2l)√(T₂/mₛ) (Eq. 17.34) and f₁²−f₂²=m a/(4 l² mₛ) (Eqs. 17.35–17.36); the sum is held constant by servo so the difference is linear in a. Cantilever and suspended-mass (centrifugal balance) variants follow. All are absolute instruments — scale factors set by mass, length, time.

Section 17.9 — electrostatic force-feedback accelerometers. Coulomb’s law gives E=Q/(kεS) (Eq. 17.37) and F=kεE²S/2 (Eq. 17.38). With E=(V₁−V₂)/h, F=kεS(V₁−V₂)²/(2h²) (Eqs. 17.39–17.40); the acceleration is a=kε(V₁−V₂)²/(2m h²) (Eq. 17.41), a quadratic relation V₁−V₂=√D·√a (Eq. 17.42). A second electrode linearizes it to a=(kεS/h²m)·(4V₁V₂) (Eqs. 17.43–17.45). Advantages: mechanical simplicity, low power, no hysteresis, zero tempco, easy shielding.

Section 17.10 — microaccelerometers. Bulk micromachining (from single-crystal silicon) and surface micromachining (sacrificial-layer etching, Figure 17.17) build 3-D structures. Vibrating-beam / double-ended tuning fork (DETF) devices (Figure 17.18) keep two beams 180° out of phase for a high-Q resonator; the acceleration appears as an FM square wave. A typical military unit: ±1200 g, 1.11 Hz/g, 2500 Hz bandwidth, DETF 9952 Hz unloaded, 8544 Hz at −1200 g, 11221 Hz at +1200 g, 9 g, 6 mm × 4.3 mm, 9000 Hz square-wave output.

Section 17.11 — cross-axis sensitivity (Figure 17.19) is the response to acceleration perpendicular to the main axis, expressed as % of main-axis sensitivity; it varies with direction and must stay below 3–4% (2–3% for piezoelectric, <1% for most others). Sections 17.12 and 17.13 cover selection (frequency range ≈ ⅓ of resonance for <1 dB; mass ≤ 1/10 of test mass; dynamic range to 10,000 g, specials to 100,000 g; transient zero-shift and ringing limits) and signal conditioning. Piezoelectric types use a charge amplifier (Figure 17.20) with E₀=Sₐ a₀(1+jωCₐ/C_f)/((1+1/G)(1+jωR_f C_f)) (Eq. 17.46), rolling off at f_{−3dB}=1/(2πR_f C_f) (Eq. 17.47), independent of cable length. Piezoresistive types use a full-bridge with shunt calibration (Figure 17.21). Microaccelerometers integrate conditioning on-chip (Figures 17.22–17.23), often outputting a buffered FM square wave that needs no ADC.
