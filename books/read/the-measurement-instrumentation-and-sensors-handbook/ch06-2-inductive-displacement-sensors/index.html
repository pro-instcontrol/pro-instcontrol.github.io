Inductive Displacement Sensors

Halit Eren · Curtin University of Technology

Section 6.2 · 11 figures · about 3,869 words

Sixteen figures in sixteen pages, and one idea underneath all of them: a magnetic circuit whose reluctance depends on position. Everything in section 6.2 is that idea with a different pickup bolted on — one coil, two coils, a toothed wheel, a rotating armature, a probe looking at eddy currents in someone else’s metal.

Reluctance is the whole story

Flux is magnetomotive force divided by reluctance, and reluctance is length over permeability times area. Put an air gap in the path and the reluctance jumps by three orders of magnitude, because mu drops from a few thousand to one. That is the sensitivity mechanism: a gap that moves a fraction of a millimetre changes the flux, and therefore the inductance, by something you can measure.

(6.6)ℜ = l / (μ μ0 A)  →  L = n² / ℜ

Single coil, then differential

The single-coil sensor is simple and nonlinear: L falls off as 1/(1 + αd), which is fine for a force cell modulating an LC oscillator and poor for a position measurement. The fix is to put two identical coils either side of the armature and wire them in opposition, so the nonlinearities cancel to first order over a small range. That is the pattern the rest of the section repeats.

The LVDT, and why it won

Two secondaries in series opposition, a core that slides through both, and a null in the middle. At null the induced emfs are equal and cancel; move the core and one grows while the other shrinks, so the output amplitude tracks displacement and the phase flips by 180° as the core crosses the centre. That phase flip is what lets a phase-sensitive demodulator recover the sign of the displacement, which is the thing a single coil cannot give you.

The transfer function is second order once you include the secondary loading, and the numerator puts the phase at +90° at low frequency and −90° at high frequency. The supply frequency is chosen so the phase passes through zero at the null position — which is why an LVDT is specified with its carrier frequency, not just its range.

The two problems the datasheet hides

Sharp makes two admissions worth repeating. First, the two secondaries are never quite identical — different inductance, resistance and capacitance produce a quadrature voltage at balance that no amount of careful alignment removes, only precision winding does. Second, the null is never exactly zero: harmonics in the supply and stray capacitance leave a residual, which is why centre-tapped excitation and a trim pot exist.

Eddy currents: measuring someone else’s metal

The probe never touches the target. Its flux induces eddy currents in the target surface, those currents push back on the active coil, and the bridge reads the push. Three skin depths of target thickness is the rule; the target must be at least as wide as the probe, or four times the probe diameter if it is curved, before it behaves like an infinite plane.

Shielding

An inductive sensor is a closed conductor sitting in a world full of stray fields, so it will pick up currents from every transformer and motor nearby. Two defences: high-permeability shells (insulated from each other so they do not form a shorted turn), and conductive sheets whose eddy currents generate a cancelling mmf. The second works well at high frequency; neither works without proper grounding.
