Torque and Power Measurement

Ivan J. Garshelis · Magnova, Inc.

Chapter 24 · 7 figures, 7 equations, 30 references

Torque, speed and power are the mechanical variables that define the functional performance of rotating machinery. Measuring them accurately is essential both for establishing a machine’s efficiency and for fixing operating regimes that are safe and conducive to long, reliable service. On-line measurement enables real-time control, helps hold product quality consistent and gives early warning of impending problems. Torque and power measurements are used in testing new machine designs and in developing new components, and torque measurement is also the established basis for controlling and verifying the tightness of threaded fasteners. This chapter covers the underlying concepts and the methods and apparatus in current use; speed, or more precisely angular velocity, is treated elsewhere in this handbook [1].

24.1 Fundamental Concepts Những khái niệm cơ bản

24.1.1 Angular Displacement, Velocity and Acceleration Dịch chuyển, vận tốc và gia tốc góc

Every point of a rotating rigid body moves in parallel or coincident planes while keeping a fixed distance from a line called the axis, and in a perfectly rigid body the points also keep fixed distances from one another. Rotation is perceived as the change of angular position of a reference point — its angular displacement Δθ over a time interval Δt — and the motion of that point, hence of the whole body, is characterised by its sense (clockwise or counterclockwise) and by its angular velocity ω = Δθ/Δt. A change Δω of that velocity in Δt is an angular acceleration α = Δω/Δt. With angles in radians and time in seconds, ω is in rad s−1 and α in rad s−2; angular velocity is often called rotational speed and quoted in revolutions per minute (rpm) or per second (rps).

24.1.2 Force, Torque and Equilibrium Lực, mômen xoắn và cân bằng

Rotation obeys Newton’s laws like any other motion. A force acts directly only on the component of motion along its line of action, so forces or force components lying in any plane that contains the axis produce no tendency to rotate about it. Rotation can be started, altered or stopped only by a tangential force Ft acting at a finite radial distance l from the axis; the effect grows with both Ft and l, so their product — the moment — is the activating quantity, and a moment about the rotational axis is a torque. Figure 24.1(a) shows a force F at an angle β to the tangent at a point P a distance l from the axis; the torque is the tangential component times the moment arm, T = (F cos β)l = Ftl (Equation 24.1), which with newtons and metres gives the unit N·m.

The resultant of any number of torques applied at different places along a body is their algebraic sum, torques tending to rotate it clockwise and counterclockwise taking opposite signs. Forces, and therefore torques, arise from contact with other solids, from motional interaction with fluids, or from gravitational (including inertial), electric or magnetic fields, and the source of each torque experiences an equal and opposite reaction torque. A non-zero resultant torque produces a proportional angular acceleration, T = Iα (Equation 24.2), where I in kg·m2 is the polar moment of inertia of the body about the axis; the relation holds whatever the state of motion. When α = 0 the torque is zero and the body is in equilibrium, which requires either no applied torque or more than one.

24.1.3 Stress, Rigidity and Strain Ứng suất, độ cứng và biến dạng

Any portion of a body in equilibrium is itself in equilibrium, so torques applied to it from outside must be balanced by equal and opposite internal torques from the adjoining material, transmitted by the collective action of stresses over the common cross-section. In a solid round section the shear stress τ rises linearly from zero on the axis to a maximum at the surface, and for a shaft of diameter d carrying torque T the surface value is τm = 16T/πd3 (Equation 24.3). Real materials are not perfectly rigid: their modulus of rigidity G fixes the finite ratio of τ to shear strain γ, so the maximum strain is also at the surface, γm = τm/G = 16T/πd3G (Equation 24.4). Shear strain appears as an angular displacement between axially separated cross-sections (Figure 24.1(b)); over a length L the shaft twists through φ = 32LT/πd4G (Equation 24.5).

24.1.4 Work, Energy and Power Công, năng lượng và công suất

If the body rotates through an angle θ while a torque T acts, the mechanical work is W = Tθ (Equation 24.6). Work is done on the body when the torque acts in the same sense as the displacement, and by the body otherwise; work on the body accelerates it and appears as kinetic energy Iω2/2, work by it decelerates it. A body that is not accelerating passes on at one place what is done on it at another. Work and energy are measured in joules; since a radian is dimensionless, 1 J = 1 N·m·rad ≡ 1 N·m, and to avoid confusion with torque it is better to express mechanical work in m·N or, preferably, in J. Power is the rate of doing work: a torque T acting through Δθ in Δt delivers TΔθ/Δt, and replacing Δθ/Δt by ω gives the simple P = Tω (Equation 24.7). The unit is the watt, 1 W = 1 J s−1 = 1 m·N s−1, with the historical horsepower at 1 Hp = 746 W. Rotating bodies transmit power between the places where external torques are applied.

24.2 Arrangements of Apparatus Bố trí thiết bị đo

Equations 24.1 to 24.7 are the physical basis of torque and power measurement, and Figure 24.2 shows the generalised arrangement. A driving torque generated inside a device at B is resisted by an opposing torque developed by another device at F; the drive — an electric motor, a gasoline engine, a steam turbine, muscular effort — is coupled through members C, a transmitting region D and further couplings E to the driven device, be it a generator, a pump, a machine tool or a set of mated threaded fasteners, where the resisting torque is met. The torque at B or F is what is to be measured, either indirectly through a correlated quantity such as a current or a fluid pressure, or more directly by measuring the reaction torque at A or G or the transmitted torque through D. Because torque and rotation are cause and effect, most interest in transmitted torque concerns rotating bodies.

In so far as the frames of both machines and their mountings to earth are perfectly rigid, the reaction at A equals the torque at B at every instant, and the reaction at G equals the torque at F; under equilibrium these equalities are independent of the compliance of any member. Also under equilibrium, and apart from usually minor parasitic torques from bearing friction and air drag on fast-moving surfaces, the driving torque at B equals the resisting torque at F. Reaction torque at A or G is usually obtained from forces measured at distances fixed by the apparatus, using Equation 24.1. Transmitted torque is obtained by measuring τm, γm or φ on a suitable member in region D and applying Equation 24.3, 24.4 or 24.5, or analogous expressions for sections other than solid round ones [2]; calibration against a known torque then makes any detail of the member in D unnecessary. When α is non-zero and measurable, T follows from Equation 24.2 — a method needing only non-invasive observation, and therefore especially useful for transitory torques such as those of individual firing events in a multicylinder engine [3].

Equations 24.6 and 24.7 apply only while the body rotates, since without motion no work is done and no power is transferred. Equation 24.6 gives the average torque from calorimetric measurement of the heat generated over a totalised number of revolutions, and Equation 24.7 is the routine basis of power measurement, with T from Equations 24.1, 24.3, 24.4 or 24.5 and ω measured by any suitable means [4]. F, T and φ are sometimes measured mechanically: the torque wrench used to tighten fasteners indicates torque by a needle over a calibrated scale driven by the elastic deflection of a spring member, in the simplest case the bending of the handle [5]. More generally, sensors or transducers convert the wanted quantity into a linearly proportional electrical signal — force sensors are also called load cells. Determining P usually requires multiplying the signals of separate T and ω sensors, though a transducer whose single signal amplitude is proportional to the power transmitted along a shaft has also been described [6].

24.3 Torque Transducer Technologies Các công nghệ đầu đo mômen xoắn

Several physical interactions convert F, τ, γ or φ into proportional electrical signals, and each requires some axial portion of the shaft to be dedicated to sensing. Figure 24.3 shows the typical sensing regions of four technologies in present use.

24.3.1 Surface Strain Biến dạng bề mặt

Figure 24.3(a) converts surface strain into a signal proportional to transmitted torque. Surface strain became the key basis for force and torque measurement after E. E. Simmons, Jr. and Arthur C. Ruge invented the bonded wire strain gage in 1938 [7]. A modern gage is simply a long electrical conductor, usually formed in a serpentine pattern in a very thin foil or film and bonded to a thin insulating carrier, which is cemented to the surface of the load-carrying member; strain is sensed as a change of resistance. Those changes are too small to measure directly, so two to four gages are arranged in a Wheatstone bridge, and independence from axial and bending loads and from temperature is obtained with a four-gage bridge of two diametrically opposite matched pairs, each aligned with a principal strain direction — which in round shafts lies at 45° to the axis. Limiting strains, from Equation 24.4 with τm at the shear proportional limit, rarely exceed a few parts in 103, so practice is to increase the compliance of the sensing region — smaller diameter, hollow or specially shaped sections — so that the limiting strain is reached at the highest torque to be measured, which maximises sensitivity.

24.3.2 Twist Angle Góc xoắn

If the shaft is slender enough (L > 5d) the twist at limiting τm can exceed 1°, enough to be resolved accurately for practical measurement. Figure 24.3(b) shows the common arrangement in which torque is found from the difference in tooth-space phasing between two identical toothed wheels on opposite ends of a compliant torsion bar. The phase displacement of the periodic signals from the two pickups is proportional to the peripheral displacement of the salient features, hence to the twist of the bar and so to the torque; the features are chosen to be sensible by non-contacting magnetic, optical or capacitive means. With more elaborate pickups the relative angular position appears as the amplitude of a single signal, which allows torque measurement even on a stationary shaft [13–15]; in other constructions a shaft-mounted variable-displacement transformer or similar device gives speed-independent output proportional to φ.

24.3.3 Stress Ứng suất

Besides elastic strain, the stresses that transmit torque show up as changes in the magnetic properties of ferromagnetic shafts. This magnetoelastic interaction [8] gives an inherently non-contacting basis for torque measurement, and two kinds of transducer are in use: Type 1 derives its output from torque-induced changes in the permeance of a magnetic circuit, Type 2 creates a magnetic field in response to torque. Type 1 transducers use branch, cross or solenoidal constructions [9]. Branch and cross designs detect torque as an imbalance of permeability along orthogonal 45° helical paths — the principal stress directions — on the shaft surface or on an added material; solenoidal designs detect differences in axial permeability between two adjacent surface regions pre-endowed with symmetrical easy axes. Branch and cross sensors miniaturise readily [10] but local variations of surface magnetic properties limit their accuracy; the solenoidal design of Figure 24.3(c) averages those variations out. Type 2 transducers carry a ring of magnetoelastically active material rigidly attached to the shaft, magnetised during manufacture with each axial half polarised circumferentially in the opposite direction, as the solid arrows of Figure 24.3(d) show [11]. Torque tilts the magnetisations into helical directions (dashed arrows), developing poles at the central domain wall and, of opposite polarity, at the ring end faces; the torque follows from one or more field sensors — Hall, magnetoresistive or flux gate — placed to sense the intensity and polarity of the field near the ring.

24.4 Construction, Operation and Application Kết cấu, vận hành và ứng dụng

Although a sensing region can be created on the shaft itself, it is more usual to install a pre-assembled modular transducer in the driveline, available with capacities from 0.001 N·m to 200,000 N·m; principle descriptions and installation instructions are found in manufacturers’ catalogues [12–20]. Trade names often identify the type: Torquemeters [13] for a family of non-contacting strain gage models, Torkducer® [18] for Type 1 magnetoelastic transducers, Torqstar™ [12] for Type 2, Torquetronic [16] for wrap-around twist angle sensors and TorXimitor™ [20] for optoelectronic non-contacting strain gage transducers. Many share generic features that transcend both the sensing technology and the range, and Figure 24.4 illustrates them.

24.4.1 Mechanical Considerations Những xem xét cơ khí

Maximum speeds vary widely, limited by size, principle, bearing type, lubrication and dynamic balance. Grease-, oil- or oil-mist-lubricated ball bearings are typical, and the parasitic torques of lubricants and seals limit accuracy at the low end of the range (minute-capacity units have no bearings at all [15]). Forced lubrication allows operation to 80,000 rpm [16]. High-speed operation demands careful thought about centrifugal stresses acting on the sensed quantity and about critical, vibration-inducing speed ranges; torsional oscillations from resonance between shaft elasticity and the inertia of coupled masses can corrupt the measurement, damage the transducer through excursions above its overload rating, and even be dangerous.

Housings either float on the shaft bearings or are rigidly mounted. Floating housings are restrained from rotating by soft means — a cable, a spring, a compliant bracket, or an eccentric feature resting against a fixed surface — and in such installations the axes of driving and driven shafts must be carefully aligned, torsionally rigid flexible couplings at each end absorbing small angular and radial misalignment; two flexible couplings at one end allow the other end to be coupled directly. Rigidly mounted housings carry feet or lugs like those of a motor frame, sometimes through adapter plates on a floating model, and are preferred where alignment is difficult or impractical, as when machines are changed often; they require dual flexible couplings at both ends. Modular transducers for zero or limited rotation need no bearings, and in such reaction-type units the housing must be attached to the shaft on one side of the sensing region only, so that all the applied torque is sensed. Whether rotating or stationary, the shaft ends generally carry coupling details — flats, keyways, splines, tapers, flanges, square drives.

24.4.2 Electrical Considerations Những xem xét điện

Transducers need excitation, and the raw output of the sensing device needs conditioning to a level and format suited to a meter or to data acquisition. Both are supplied by circuits matched to the sensing technology: strain gage bridges are typically powered at 10–20 V dc or ac and give 1.5–3.0 mV per volt of excitation at rated load, so amplifiers of gain 100 or more are needed, and ac excitation also calls for oscillators and demodulators. Components must be mounted on the torqued member, and getting power in and signals out of a rotating shaft needs special means. The most direct is conductive — silver-graphite brushes on silver slip rings, with a lift-off mechanism to extend life when not measuring. Non-contacting methods include inductive coupling between stationary and rotating windings [12–15], illumination of shaft-mounted photovoltaic cells [20], or batteries strapped to the shaft [21], limited by centrifugal force to low speeds; signals leave through rotary transformers, frequency-modulated infrared LEDs [19, 20] or FM telemetry [21]. Where rotation is limited to a few turns, as in steering gear, valve actuators or oscillating mechanisms, hard wiring of both power and signal is often suitable, and flexible cabling minimises incidental torque and gives long life. Non-contacting technologies avoid all of this.

24.4.3 Costs and Options Giá cả và các tùy chọn

Prices reflect the wide range of capacities, performance ratings, types, styles, options and accessories, and rise with capacity within a type; reaction types cost about half of similarly rated rotating units. A foot-mounted 565 N·m strain gage transducer with slip rings or rotary transformers and an integral speed sensor, specified to ±0.1% nonlinearity and hysteresis, cost about $4000 in 1997, with about $2000 more for compatible excitation, conditioning and display instrumentation. A comparable magnetoelastic transducer at ±0.5% costs about $1300, high capacity units for extreme speed with appropriate lubrication can exceed $50,000, and Type 2 magnetoelastic transducers mass-produced for automotive power steering cost about $10.

24.5 Apparatus for Power Measurement Thiết bị đo công suất

Rotating machinery spans power levels from fractions of a watt to tens of megawatts — a range of more than 108 — and power-measuring apparatus spans a corresponding range of types and sizes. Mechanical power flows from a driver to a load and can be found directly from Equation 24.7 by measuring ω together with the output torque of the driver or the input torque of the load, whichever is the device under test. When the DUT is a driver, measurement is usually needed over its full range of speed and torque, so the test apparatus must act as a controllable load able to absorb the delivered power; when the DUT is a pump, fan, gearbox or other load, the apparatus must include a driver able to supply the DUT’s full rated range. Power can also be found indirectly by converting it to or from another form of energy and measuring the calorimetric or electrical quantities, but given the ready availability of accurate torque and speed measurement, indirect methods are worth considering only where special circumstances make direct ones difficult.

Dynamometer is the special name for power-measuring apparatus that includes absorbing and/or driving means and in which torque is determined from reaction forces on a stationary part, the stator. An effective dynamometer is easily assembled by mounting the DUT so that the reaction torque on its frame can be measured; Figure 24.5 shows a device built for that purpose, and commercial models (Torque Table® [12]) rated to support 222 N to 4900 N are available with torque capacities from 1.3 N·m to 226 N·m. Torque tubes [4] and other mounting arrangements are also used; apart from possible rotational or elastic resonances such systems have no speed limit. More generally, and especially for large machinery, dynamometers incorporate a specialised driving or absorbing machine, classified by function as absorbing or driving (sometimes motoring), a universal dynamometer being able to serve as either.

24.5.1 Absorption Dynamometers Dynamometer hấp thụ

Absorption dynamometers, often called brakes because they work by creating a controllable drag torque, convert mechanical work into heat. A drag torque, unlike an active torque, can only restrain rotation, never initiate it, and the temperature rise is controlled by carrying the heat away, usually to a moving fluid, typically air or water. Drag is produced by inherently dissipative processes: friction between rubbing surfaces, shear or turbulence in a viscous liquid, the flow of electric current, or magnetic hysteresis. Gaspard Riche de Prony (1755–1839) invented in 1821 [22] a highly useful friction brake for testing the steam engines then becoming prevalent, and brakes of that kind are still used for instruction because they embody the principles and operating considerations of all absorption dynamometers.

Figure 24.6 shows a prony brake. The power that the engine shaft would deliver to its load is converted instead into heat by friction between the blocks and the flywheel rim, the clamping bolts setting the drag torque and water flow or evaporation removing heat from the inside of the rim. Neither the friction force nor even the flywheel radius need be known — a fact Prony recognised — because the drag torque, which tends to rotate the clamped apparatus, is held by the equal and opposite reaction F·r at the end of a torque arm of fixed radius r, where F is read by a scale or load cell; the power is P = Frω = Fr2πN/60 with N in rpm. Fluctuating friction makes rubbing a poor way to generate drag, yet prony brakes are so easily built that ad hoc versions using bare ropes or wooden cleats serve in laboratories and wherever undemanding or infrequent measurements are wanted. Refined versions form standalone dynamometers with self-contained cooling tanks up to 746 kW (1000 Hp), to 3600 rpm and 5400 N·m [23], stationary or mobile, used for testing large electric motors and the engines and transmissions of agricultural vehicles. They can impose full drag torque down to zero speed.

William Froude (1810–1879) [24] invented a water brake (1877) that does not rely on rubbing friction: drag is developed between rotor and stator by the momentum the rotor imparts to the water in the casing, forcing it to circulate between cup-like pockets cast into facing surfaces of both. The rotor runs in bearings that also fix its axial position, labyrinth seals prevent leakage with minimal drag and wear, and the stator casing sits in the frame on trunnion bearings in cradle fashion, its restraining torque measured by reaction forces much as in the prony brake. Drag is set by a valve controlling outlet back pressure [25], inlet flow [26], or both, the last allowing very rapid changes [27], and the absorbed energy leaves with the continuous water flow. Other cradle-mounted water brakes look similar but differ internally and rely on other principles, smooth rotors developing viscous drag by shear and turbulence; all hydraulic dynamometers nevertheless function deliberately as inefficient centrifugal pumps. Whatever the internal design and valve setting, maximum drag is low at low speed — zero at standstill — and rises rapidly, typically as the square of speed, while the irreducible presence of some water plus windage sets a speed-dependent lower limit on controllable torque; cavitation erosion and vibration set the upper limits of speed and power. Capacities run from 300 kW to 25,000 kW, with portable units as small as 75 kW [26]; the largest ever built [27], absorbing about 75,000 kW (100,000 Hp), tested nuclear submarine propulsion systems. Maximum speed matches that of the prime mover being tested and therefore falls as capacity rises, high-speed gas turbine and aerospace test equipment reaching 30,000 rpm [25].

In 1855 Jean B. L. Foucault (1819–1868) [22] demonstrated the conversion of mechanical work into heat by rotating a copper disk between the poles of an electromagnet, and this eddy current means of generating drag has been widely exploited in dynamometers since about 1935. Figure 24.7 shows the essentials: a toothed or spoked steel rotor turns in a spatially uniform field from dc-excited stator coils, inducing locally circulating currents in conductive copper portions of the stator, and the electromagnetic forces between the magnetised rotor and the field of those eddy currents create the drag. Torque, and hence input power, is controlled by the stator excitation current, with electrical input below 1% of rated capacity: the machine is effectively a short-circuited generator, since the resistive loss of the eddy currents is dissipated within it.

The heated stator must be cooled, sometimes by blower air in smaller machines [23] but more often by continuous water flow [25, 27, 28]. In dry gap brakes, the type of Figure 24.7, water is confined to passages in the stator; larger machines are often of the water-in-gap type, where water also circulates around the rotor [28], acting as in a water brake and adding a non-electromagnetic component that sets a lower limit to controllable torque, while in dry gap types windage sets that limit. Since drag arises from rotor motion it is zero at standstill for any excitation, rising rapidly and approximately linearly with speed before approaching a current-limited saturation, and the restraining torque is again measured by a reaction force at a fixed known distance from the axis. Standard eddy current brakes run from below 1 kW [23, 27] to over 2000 kW [27, 28], at up to 12,000 rpm in the smaller units and 3600 rpm in the largest, with special units of 3000 Hp (2238 kW) at 25,000 rpm having been built [28]. Hysteresis brakes [29] develop drag through the attraction and repulsion between poles established in a reticulated stator by the field coil and those created in a drag cup rotor by the stator field gradients; rotation cyclically reverses the local magnetisation of the special steel rotor, and the energy of those reversals, proportional to the area of the material’s hysteresis loop, is converted to heat in the cup and removed by blower or compressed air. As with eddy current brakes the drag is set by the excitation current, but unlike them rated drag is available down to zero speed (eddy current effects typically add only 1% per 1000 rpm), and their smooth rotating surfaces give low parasitic torque and a dynamic range as high as 200:1. Continuous ratings reach 6 kW (12 kW with two tandem brakes and two blowers), intermittent ratings 7 kW for 5 minutes or less, some low-capacity units are convection cooled, the smallest rated at 7 W continuous (35 W for 5 minutes), and maximum speeds run from 30,000 rpm to 10,000 rpm. Torque is measured by a strain gage bridge on a moment arm supporting the stator.

24.5.2 Driving and Universal Dynamometers Dynamometer dẫn động và vạn năng

Electric generators, ac and dc, offer another controllable drag and are readily adapted to dynamometer service by cradle-mounting their stators; such machines can also motor, delivering a controllable active torque, and when they can be selected to drive or absorb they serve as a universal dynamometer. In the absorbing mode a dc machine dissipates the generated power in a convection-cooled resistor bank, blower air cooling the machine being usually adequate since most of the mechanical input is dissipated externally — though all of it is still accounted for by the product of reaction torque and speed. In the motoring mode torque and speed are set by the field and armature currents. Modern ac machines use regenerative converters to return braking power to the line and high-power solid-state inverters for speed control, their internal construction being that of a plain three-phase induction motor without brushes, slip rings or commutator, so that the absence of rotor windings allows higher speeds than dc machines. Universal dynamometers are four-quadrant machines — able to produce torque with or against their rotation — which allows the effective drag to be reduced to zero at any speed. They are available over a relatively narrow range, 56 to 450 kW with 110 to 1900 N·m and 4500 to 13,500 rpm, reflecting their principal use in automotive engine development; special dynamometers for transmissions and other driveline components insert the DUT between a diesel or electric prime mover and a hydraulic or eddy current brake [30].

24.5.3 Measurement Accuracy Độ chính xác của phép đo

Power measurement accuracy is generally limited by the torque measurement, ±0.25% to ±1%, since speed can be measured to almost any desired accuracy. Torque errors arise from extraneous torques applied through hose and cable connections, from windage of external parts and from miscalibration of the load cell, and undetected friction in the trunnion bearings of cradled machines compromises accuracy. Ideally well-lubricated antifriction bearings contribute nothing to the restraining torque, but in practice the unchanging contact region of the rolling elements on their races makes them prone to brinelling from vibration, from the unsupported weight of attached devices, or even inadvertently during alignment; the problem is alleviated by periodically rotating the races, chiefly the outer ones, and in some bearing-in-bearing constructions the central races are continuously rotated slowly by a motor, while others support the stator on hydrostatic oil-lift bearings [28].

24.5.4 Costs Giá cả

The wide range of torque, speed and power levels, together with the varying sophistication of the instrumentation, is reflected in a very wide spread of prices. Suspension systems of the kind in Figure 24.5, for which the user supplies the rotating machine, cost $4000 to $6000 and rise with capacity [12]. A 100 Hp (74.6 kW) portable water brake with a strain gage load cell and a digital readout of torque, speed and power costs $4500, or $8950 with more sophisticated data acquisition [26]. Stationary and some transportable hydraulic dynamometers cost from $113/kW in the smaller sizes [25] down to $35/kW for the very largest [27], with transportation, installation and instrumentation adding significantly. Eddy current dynamometers cost from as little as $57/kW to nearly $700/kW depending on capacity, control system and instrumentation [24, 25, 28]; hysteresis brakes with integral speed sensors cost $3300 to $14,000 according to capacity [29], with compatible controllers from manual to fully programmable for PC test control and IEEE-488 data acquisition at $500 to $4200. The flexibility and high performance of ac universal dynamometers show in their comparatively high prices of $670 to $2200/kW [25, 28].
