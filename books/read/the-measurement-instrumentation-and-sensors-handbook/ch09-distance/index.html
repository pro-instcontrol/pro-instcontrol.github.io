Books · Measurement, Instrumentation and Sensors Handbook · Chapter 9

Distance

W. John Ballantyne · Spar Aerospace Ltd.

Chapter 9 · 18 source pages · 8 figures, 1 table

Ballantyne's chapter is really an argument disguised as a survey. The taxonomy in 9.1 — contact or not, active or passive, time-of-flight or triangulation or field — is not a filing system for products. It is a set of axes along which a design decision has to be made, and each axis trades one kind of capability for another. Read that way, the chapter is far more useful than its 1999 product table suggests.

The scale figure is the first clue about what the chapter is really claiming. Thirty orders of magnitude of measurable distance, and this chapter covers six of them — centimetres to kilometres. That is the range a robot arm or a surveying instrument actually needs, and it is also the range where the three principles are all competitive. Below a millimetre, contact and interferometry dominate; above a kilometre, only time-of-flight survives.

The active/passive distinction is the one most often misread. Active systems are not better; they are more controllable. The author's point about the correspondence problem is the real content: a passive stereo system has to solve, for every frame, which point in the left image is the same physical point as which point in the right image. An active system that time-multiplexes its targets — one LED lit at a time, as OPTOTRAK does — makes that problem disappear entirely. That is why almost every commercial motion tracker uses active targets, and it is a design lesson that generalises well beyond ranging.

The triangulation formula deserves a careful look, because the printed version and the drawn geometry do not agree. Read Figure 9-2 the way its own caption reads it — α₁ and α₂ measured at the two ends of the baseline, each against the baseline itself — and the third angle of the triangle is π − α₁ − α₂. The sine rule then gives the side from the left station to the target as R = b sin α₂ / sin(α₁ + α₂), and the perpendicular distance from the baseline to the target as h = b sin α₁ sin α₂ / sin(α₁ + α₂). The printed numerator, b sin α₁ sin α₂, is exactly the numerator of h; the printed denominator, sin(α₂ − α₁), should be sin(α₁ + α₂). The slip is easy to demonstrate rather than merely assert: the printed denominator vanishes whenever α₁ = α₂, that is, for a target on the perpendicular bisector of the baseline, where the true range is plainly finite and equal to b/(2 tan α₁). So this is not a small-angle approximation quietly standing in for an exact result — it is a sign error that manufactures a singularity at the one geometry every surveyor eventually sets up. Keep the corrected form in mind when you read the rest of the chapter, because the accuracy argument in 9.2 inherits the geometry.

The time-of-flight versus triangulation comparison is the chapter's central quantitative claim, and it is worth restating plainly: TOF error is set by the shortest interval the electronics can resolve, so it is flat with distance; triangulation error is set by geometry, so it grows as R². There is therefore always a crossover range, and it is a design parameter, not a law of nature — you move it by changing the baseline or the clock.

The radar range equation section looks like boilerplate until you notice what it is doing. Substituting the spot area (9.3) and the transmitter gain (9.4) into the general form (9.2) collapses the 1/R⁴ into 1/R². That collapse is the whole reason laser ranging works at all: for a target larger than the illuminated spot, doubling the range costs you only a factor of four, not sixteen. It also explains the design pressure the author notes — you cannot buy range with aperture indefinitely, because D² appears in the numerator but packaging volume goes as D³.

Active triangulation is where the chapter becomes concrete, and the two commercial systems it describes are still the two archetypes. Hymarc's synchronized scanning is a genuinely clever piece of optomechanics: instead of sweeping the beam and leaving the detector fixed, it sweeps both, using the two faces of one oscillating mirror. The effect is that the triangulation triangle stops changing shape as the beam scans, so the spot stays on the detector array instead of walking off it. Tilting the array relative to the lens plane then extends the depth of focus over the whole measuring volume — the Scheimpflug condition, applied to a scanner.

Perceptron's LASAR takes the other route: keep the geometry simple and put the intelligence in the modulation. Amplitude-modulate the laser at a reference frequency, compare the phase of the return against that reference, and the range falls out — with the same ambiguity problem the chapter flags for all coherent detection, since a phase measurement cannot distinguish R from R plus a whole modulation wavelength. The polygon mirror handles azimuth, the nodding mirror elevation, and the raster is built from the two rates.

The magnetic tracker comparison at the end is the most quotable part of the chapter, and it is a clean lesson in how a physical law becomes an engineering limit. A dipole field falls as 1/R³; optical triangulation falls as 1/R. Over a metre, that is a factor of a hundred in signal for the same starting point. No amount of electronics recovers it. That single exponent is why optical trackers have large working volumes and magnetic ones do not — and why magnetic trackers, despite needing no line of sight, remain niche.

What survives is the taxonomy and the crossover argument. Every time-of-flight sensor shipping today still trades resolution against clock speed; every triangulation sensor still trades range against baseline; every field-based sensor still fights the inverse-cube law. The parts changed. The arithmetic did not.
