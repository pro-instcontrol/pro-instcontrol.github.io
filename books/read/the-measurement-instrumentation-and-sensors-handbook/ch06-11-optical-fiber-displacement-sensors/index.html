Optical Fiber Displacement Sensors

Richard O. Claus, Vikram Bhatia & Anbo Wang · Virginia Tech

Section 6.11 · 0 figures · about 868 words

Four sensors are compared here, and they divide cleanly into two families. The Fabry–Perot pair modulates intensity; the grating pair encodes wavelength. Everything else in the section follows from that split — the demodulation cost, the cross-sensitivity, and the reason the authors end up recommending different sensors for different jobs rather than one winner.

The comparison is also a good example of how to read a sensor paper. The criteria are stated up front — strain resolution, dynamic range, cross-sensitivity, fabrication simplicity, demodulation complexity — and each sensor is then judged against all five. Very few of the numbers are surprising in isolation; what is useful is seeing them side by side.

Extrinsic versus intrinsic Ngoại so với nội

The EFPI puts its cavity in the air gap between two fibre ends. That single design decision buys three things at once: the cavity is not strained when the fibre is strained transversely, so the Poisson effect does not reach the measurement; the temperature coefficient is small because the cavity is mostly air; and the sensor can be built single-ended, which matters when you can only reach one side of a structure.

The IFPI puts its cavity inside the glass. Now the light travels through material that changes its refractive index when it is strained — the photoelastic effect — and that index change, not the length change, dominates the output. The consequence is that an IFPI responds to every strain component and to temperature, which is exactly what an embedded sensor must not do. It is a textbook case of a device that is elegant in principle and awkward in a real structure.

Equation 6.117 is worth pausing on. I = I₀cos²(4πd/λ) has a period of λ/2 in d, which is why the text can say that one fringe equals half a wavelength. And because the cosine is symmetric, the same intensity occurs on both slopes — which is why the sensor is operated on one slope only, around the Q-point, where the response is linear and the sign of the change is unambiguous.

Why wavelength encoding wins Vì sao mã hoá bước sóng thắng

An intensity sensor carries its information in a quantity that everything else also changes: connector loss, bend loss, source ageing, detector drift. A grating carries its information in wavelength, and wavelength is the one property of light that survives a long fibre link intact. That is the whole argument for fibre Bragg gratings, and it is why they became the default sensing element in structural monitoring despite being difficult to make.

The price is in the interrogation. A Bragg grating has a bandwidth of 0.1 to 1 nm and shifts by about 11 nm per per cent strain at 1550 nm, so you need a spectrometer or an interferometric scheme to read it. Equation 6.122, λ_B = 2Λn_eff, tells you why both strain and temperature work: they change Λ and they change n_eff, and the sensor cannot tell which did what. Hence the reference grating, and hence the temperature-compensated packages that dominate the product literature.

The 111 µε figure at 1300 nm is the number that decides applications. With a 0.1 nm spectrum analyser, a Bragg grating simply cannot resolve strains below about a hundred microstrain — not because the grating is bad, but because the readout is. This is a recurring theme in the chapter: the transducer is often better than the instrument attached to it.

The long-period alternative Giải pháp chu kỳ dài

A long-period grating couples the core mode to cladding modes travelling in the same direction. Because Δβ is small, the period is large — hundreds of micrometres — and the resonance bands are broad. Broad bands are usually a defect in a filter and a gift in a sensor: a broad slope means you can put a fixed-wavelength laser on the side of the band and read the shift as an intensity change, with nothing more than a photodiode.

That is the trade the section is really describing. You give up the sharp, easily multiplexed Bragg peak and you get a demodulator that costs a few dollars. Table 6.28 shows why the trade can pay: fibre D shifts by 15.2 nm per per cent strain, half again as much as a Bragg grating, and the sign of the coefficient even differs between fibres — negative for the dispersion-shifted fibre, positive for the rest.

The catch is temperature. Table 6.29 gives 0.062 to 0.154 nm/°C against 0.014 nm/°C for a Bragg grating — an order of magnitude worse. A long-period grating is therefore a strain sensor that also measures temperature very well and cannot distinguish the two, which is why multiparameter schemes appear in the literature almost as soon as the gratings themselves.

What the comparison actually says Điều phần so sánh thực sự nói

The closing comparison is deliberately unheroic: interferometric sensors are sensitive and fast but nonlinear; intrinsic sensors drift with temperature; gratings multiplex easily. Each may be used in specific applications. That is the honest conclusion, and it is more useful than a ranking, because the choice between them is usually made by the structure being monitored rather than by the sensor.
