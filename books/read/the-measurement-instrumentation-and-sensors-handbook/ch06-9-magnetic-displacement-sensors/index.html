Magnetic Displacement Sensors

David S. Nyce · MTS Systems Corp.

Section 6.9 · 9 figures · about 5,286 words

Four sensing principles share this section, and they are more different than the heading suggests. Magnetostriction measures a time of flight along a wire; magnetoresistance and the Hall effect measure a field at a point; magnetic encoders read a code. What they have in common is only that the quantity carried between the moving part and the fixed part is a magnetic field — and that is the reason all four are non-contacting.

The distinction between displacement and position is worth holding on to. Displacement is a change; position is a coordinate. Most of the sensors in this section actually report position from a fixed datum and let you subtract consecutive readings to get displacement, which is why the author calls them position sensors rather than displacement sensors. Only the incremental encoder is genuinely a displacement device, and it is the only one that needs to be re-zeroed after a power loss.

Two curves you must know how to read Hai đường cong phải biết đọc

Figure 6.80 is the practical one: hysteresis as a specification. The two branches are the same sensor approached from below and from above, and their separation is what you will see when a machine reverses direction and the reading does not come back to where it was. It is quoted as a percentage of full scale because it is a property of the whole chain — sensing element, conditioning, and mechanics.

Figure 6.81 is the material one. The loop area is energy lost per cycle, which matters enormously in a transformer core and hardly at all in a position sensor; what matters here is the shape near the origin. Remanence B_r is how much flux stays when you remove the field — that is what makes a permanent magnet a permanent magnet. Coercivity H_c is how hard it is to push that flux back to zero, and it is the single number that separates a soft magnetic core (low H_c, easy to magnetise and demagnetise, used for waveguides and read-head cores) from a hard magnetic material (high H_c, used for the position magnet itself).

Magnetostriction: a sensor built on a time of flight Từ giảo: cảm biến dựa trên thời gian bay

The magnetostrictive sensor is the most interesting device in this section because nothing in it moves except a sound wave. A current pulse is sent down a wire; at the position magnet, the combination of that axial field and the circumferential field of the current produces a local twist — the Wiedemann effect — and the twist runs both ways along the wire. The time it takes to reach the pickup at the far end is the distance, divided by about 3000 m/s.

Three numbers explain the whole product category. First, 3000 m/s is roughly nine microseconds per metre round trip, so a one-metre sensor answers in under 20 µs and the electronics, not the wave, sets the update rate. Second, the resolution of 1 µm comes from timing, not from the wavelength: a 1 µm change shifts the arrival by about 0.3 ns, which is well inside what a fast comparator can resolve against a repeated pulse. Third, the temperature coefficient of 2–5 ppm/°C is a property of the wire — the speed of sound in the alloy — not of the electronics, which is why these sensors hold their calibration.

The damping element is the part everyone forgets and nobody should. Without it, the wave travelling away from the pickup reflects off the far end and comes back as a second arrival, which the timing circuit will happily measure as a second, phantom magnet. The dead zone is simply the length of wire buried in the damper, and it is why a magnetostrictive sensor cannot measure right up to its own end cap.

Magnetoresistance and the Hall effect Từ điện trở và hiệu ứng Hall

Magnetoresistance is a small effect — one per cent at room temperature — and the section is honest about it. That one per cent is why a single MR element makes a poor position sensor and why the useful configurations are either a bridge of four elements or a long string of elements with decoding. The interesting development is GMR: by stacking alternating magnetic and non-magnetic layers a few atoms thick, the resistance change rises by an order of magnitude or more, and the effect saturates at higher fields. That is what made the read head in a hard disk drive possible, and it is the same physics that gives you a practical magnetic position sensor.

The Hall effect is the opposite trade: a large, easily conditioned output from a device that measures the field at one point and nothing else. That is why Hall sensors dominate in cheap angular position sensing and in brushless motor commutation, and why they are poor at long linear travel — you need hundreds of them, multiplexed, with a decoding scheme that knows which one is talking. The formula V_H = K_H β I / z says the output scales with current and inversely with thickness, which is why a Hall plate is made as thin as the process allows.

The two-magnet arrangement in Figure 6.87 is a neat trick: put the sensor where the fields cancel and you get a bipolar output that crosses zero at a known position, with good linearity near the crossing and a well-defined sign either side. It is the magnetic equivalent of a Wheatstone bridge null — you are measuring a difference of two nearly equal quantities, so the common-mode part of the field cancels.

Magnetic encoders and the Gray code Bộ mã hoá từ và mã Gray

The Gray code appears twice in this chapter — in the optical section and here — and for the same reason. Any absolute encoder that reads several parallel tracks at once has a transition where all the bits change together, and at that instant the reading is whatever the mechanical tolerances allow it to be. Gray code removes the problem at the source by making consecutive words differ in exactly one bit. Table 6.25 is worth reading column by column: the natural binary column changes several bits at every step, the Gray column never changes more than one, and the BCD column shows why a decimal display needs a different code entirely.

The read head itself is a small transformer with a saturable core. A read pulse into the primary produces an output pulse only if the core is not already saturated by the recorded medium — so a magnetised area reads as 0 and an unmagnetised area as 1. It is a destructive-looking scheme that is in fact entirely non-destructive: the read pulse is far too weak to alter the recording.
