Tilt Measurement

Adam Chrzanowski · James M. Secord · University of New Brunswick

Chapter 15 · 5 figures, 1 appendix, 5 equations

This chapter is a companion to the Vietnamese translation of Chapter 15. All five figures are redrawn as vectors (15.1 subsidence from tilt, 15.2 hydrostatic equilibrium, 15.3 hydrostatic leveling, 15.4 suspended/inverted plumb lines, 15.5 air-current and spiral-wire errors), and the equations (15.1–15.5) plus the suppliers appendix are reproduced. The translation prose is not repeated here.

Tilt is the angular change of a line joining two points within a vertical plane; because angular tilt is directly tied to the linear change over the base length, it can be derived from a linear measurement if the base is known. Section 15.1 reviews tiltmeters/inclinometers by sensing method (liquid/bubble, vertical or horizontal pendulum; mechanical, accelerometer, vibrating-wire, or electrolytic transducer). Sensitivity splits into geophysical tiltmeters (10⁻⁸–10⁻⁹ rad) and engineering ones (0.1″–seconds of arc). Examples: the mercury Model 300 (10⁻⁹ rad over a 587 mm base), coincidence-viewing clinometers (~0.3″), the Electrolevel (0.25″), the Talyvel and Niveltronic pendulums, and the popular servo-accelerometer tiltmeters whose output voltage is proportional to sin(angle). Figure 15.1 shows subsidence from a profile of tilt readings; the subsidence of point 4 follows equation (15.1), δh₄ = α₁s₁ + α₂s₂ + α₃s₃, with fidelity limited by measurement density and the uniform-slope assumption.

Section 15.2 is geodetic leveling: precision (or digital) levels give height differences with ±0.1 mm/setup (~0.5″ of tilt) but errors accumulate with setups and are biased by vertical refraction. Tilt between two benchmarks is T = d(Δh)/s; the precision compounds as shown in the chapter. Section 15.3 is hydrostatic leveling: two connected vessels satisfy the hydrostatic relation (15.2), P₁ + ρ₁gh₁ = P₂ + ρ₂gh₂ = c, illustrated in Figures 15.2 and 15.3 where the measured level difference is Dh₁₂ = r₂ − r₁. Temperature must be kept constant (1.2°C between containers ⇒ 0.05 mm error), and two-fluid designs cancel the temperature effect. Instruments cited include the ELWAAG 001 (±0.03 mm/40 m), Nivomatic (±0.1 mm/24 m), and the DAG network (~±0.01 mm).

Section 15.4 covers suspended and inverted plumb lines (Figure 15.4): both derive inclination from a horizontal position difference over a vertical separation. Inverted plumb lines anchor deep in foundation rock to measure absolute displacement of the surface. Two under-rated error sources are air currents and the wire's spiral (annealed) shape. An air current of speed v over length h, at distance H from the anchor, deflects the wire by e = f₀hH/Q (15.3), where f₀ = 0.08 d v² Q (15.4); with H=50 m, h=5 m, d=1 mm, Q=20 kg, v=1 m/s, e=1 mm. The spiral radius r (15.5), r = (d/8)·√(πER/Q), means a rotated wire can error by up to 2r — for d=1 mm, R=15 cm, Q=196 N, r=0.3 mm. Section 15.5 stresses that reliable deformation analysis needs regular calibration and integrated, time-series statistical treatment of the observations.

The chapter closes with 33 references and an appendix listing suppliers of tilt-measuring instrumentation (Applied Geomechanics, Schaevitz, SINCO, Roctest, Leica, Solinst, and others) — reproduced in the Vietnamese version.
