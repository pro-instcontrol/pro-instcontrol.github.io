Synchro/Resolver Displacement Sensors

Robert M. Hyatt, Jr. & David Dayton · ILC Data Device Corp.

Section 6.10 · 0 figures · about 999 words

Every sensor in this section is a transformer with a movable part. That single sentence explains almost everything else about them: why they need an ac excitation, why the output is an amplitude-modulated carrier rather than a voltage, why they are so rugged, and why the electronics that follow them are where the real engineering happens.

The induction potentiometer is the simplest step up from an RVDT: put windings on the rotor as well and you get more volts per degree. The section is refreshingly honest that this is a trade — four named penalties for one named benefit — and the benefit is signal-to-noise, not accuracy in the abstract.

Two modes that are easy to confuse Hai chế độ dễ nhầm lẫn

A resolver can be wired two ways, and the difference matters more than the wiring diagram suggests. In the control-transmitter mode you excite the rotor and read two stator windings; the outputs are sine and cosine of the shaft angle, and the amplitude carries the information. In the control-transformer mode you excite the two stator windings 90° apart and read the rotor; the amplitude is now constant and the phase carries the information.

That is why the same physical device can be described as an amplitude sensor in one paragraph and a phase sensor in the next. Figure 6.92 and Figure 6.94 are the two modes; Figure 6.93 is the sine/cosine output that most modern converter chips expect. Almost every resolver-to-digital converter on the market works in control-transmitter mode, because a sine and a cosine are easier to manipulate than a phase difference.

Why resolvers outlived synchros Vì sao resolver còn, synchro mất

Synchros were the World War II solution: three windings at 120°, a table of amplitudes per position, and a lot of decoding. They worked, and they flew in radar and fire control. But three phases at 120° are awkward arithmetic compared with a sine and a cosine, and the converter industry noticed. The Scott “T” transformer exists precisely to turn a synchro into a resolver before anything else happens — it is the adapter that let a generation of synchro hardware survive into the resolver era.

The lesson generalises: the winning standard is rarely the most elegant sensor, it is the one with the cheapest arithmetic downstream.

The ratiometric trick Mẹo theo tỉ số

Equation 6.113 divides the sine output by the cosine output before taking the arctangent. This is not a mathematical convenience, it is the whole reason a resolver survives in a factory. Any noise injected equally into both windings — a ground loop, a temperature drift in the excitation, a common-mode pickup from a nearby drive — appears in numerator and denominator alike and cancels. What does not cancel is noise that reaches one winding and not the other, which is why the wiring and shielding of the two pairs matter so much.

The same division is why the specification sheet can claim a large degree of temperature compensation without any temperature sensor at all.

Reading the type II loop Đọc vòng lặp Type II

Figure 6.98 looks complicated and is in fact one idea repeated. Two multipliers form sin θ cos φ and cos θ sin φ; their difference is sin(θ − φ); everything after that exists only to drive φ towards θ. The demodulator turns the carrier into a dc error, the integrator turns the error into a rate command, the VCO turns the rate command into pulses, and the up/down counter turns pulses into φ.

Two integrators in series — the analog integrator and the counter — make this a type II loop, and the consequence is worth stating plainly: at constant velocity the loop has no position error and no velocity error. The only error is transient, during acceleration. That is what lets a converter with a 100 Hz bandwidth track a shaft spinning at ten revolutions per second, and it is why the lag calculation in equation 6.116 has acceleration in the numerator and nothing else.

The price of type II is settling time. A large step has to be slewed at the maximum tracking rate and then allowed to settle for roughly eleven time constants in 16-bit mode. The worked example — 49 ms of slew plus 45 ms of settling, about 94 ms — is the number that decides whether a given machine tool can use this converter or needs a faster one.

The numbers worth remembering Những con số đáng nhớ

Sixteen bits is 65,536 counts per revolution, which is 20 arc seconds of resolution. The converter's own accuracy is ±2.3 arc minutes — six times coarser than its resolution, and the distinction between the two is the one people get wrong. Resolution is the smallest step the output can take; accuracy is how far that step may be from the truth. Repeatability of ±1 LSB is the third number, and for point-to-point motion it is often the only one that matters.

The velocity output deserves a mention of its own. A dc voltage proportional to rate, 0.25% linearity and 0.75% reversal error, is good enough to replace a tachometer in many systems — which is why a resolver often does double duty for feedback and for commutation, and why the section can claim a lower parts count than an encoder plus Hall sensors.
