Time-of-Flight Ultrasonic Displacement Sensors

Teklic Ole Pedersen & Nils Karlsson · Linköpings Universitet

Section 6.7 · 5 figures · about 2,330 words

Air-coupled ultrasound is the cheapest way to measure a distance without touching anything, and it is also the one that punishes carelessness about the medium. The physics section is not decoration: the two numbers that decide whether an ultrasonic rangefinder works — the impedance mismatch at the membrane and the temperature dependence of the speed of sound — are both in the first three pages.

The pulse-echo relation d = ct/2 hides a factor of two that matters. The sound travels out and back, so every error in c is doubled in the answer, and c is not a constant in air. At 343 m/s, a 1 °C change in air temperature shifts the speed by about 0.6 m/s, or 0.17%. Over a 1 m path that is 1.7 mm of apparent displacement — larger than most of the effects a displacement sensor is bought to detect.

Impedance is the whole problem Trở kháng là toàn bộ vấn đề

A piezoelectric ceramic has a characteristic impedance of roughly 30 MRayl; air is 415 Rayl. Put those into Equation 6.89b and the transmission coefficient comes out near 10⁻⁵ — five orders of magnitude of intensity lost at the membrane face before the wave has travelled anywhere. Every design decision in an air-coupled transducer follows from that number: the matching layer, the low-impedance polymer films, the electrostatic membrane with its trapped air gap.

The near field L ≈ D²/4λ and the half angle sin β = 1.22λ/D pull in opposite directions. A 40 kHz transducer in air has λ ≈ 8.6 mm; a 20 mm membrane gives a near field of about 12 mm and a half angle of 30°. Widen the membrane to narrow the beam and the near field grows as the square; raise the frequency and absorption rises faster than the beam narrows. There is no free direction.

The piezoelectric transducer is a resonator: it wants to work near the mechanical resonance of the disc, which is what makes it efficient and also what makes its impulse response ring. The electrostatic transducer is not a resonator in the same sense — its membrane is a stretched film over a grooved backplate, and the grooves are there to keep the trapped air from stiffening the film. That is why electrostatic devices produce the short, clean pulses that pulse-echo ranging wants.

Four ways to measure a time Bốn cách đo một khoảng thời gian

Table 6.17 is the honest summary. Pulse-echo is simple and noisy; phase angle is quiet and ambiguous beyond one wavelength; frequency modulation resolves the ambiguity but can confuse long and short paths; correlation is the most robust and the most expensive. The choice is almost always made by the interference environment rather than by the accuracy specification — a clean lab and a welding cell are not the same measurement problem.

The correlation method is worth understanding because it reframes the problem. Instead of timing an edge, it estimates the impulse response of the whole channel, h(t, t₀), by dividing the cross-spectrum by the auto-spectrum. The delay is then the peak of that response — or better, the peak of a curve fitted to it, which can resolve a fraction of a sample interval. That is where the sub-millimetre numbers come from: not from a faster clock, but from fitting a known shape to a noisy correlation peak.
