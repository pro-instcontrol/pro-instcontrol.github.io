Electropneumatic and Electrohydraulic Instruments

M. Pachter · C. H. Houpis · Air Force Institute of Technology

Chapter 99 · 10 figures, 26 equations

In a control system the output of the controller — also called the equalizer or compensator — which acts on the command and feedback signals, is invariably sent to an actuator. The actuator is a power element critically positioned at the plant input and, to a large extent, it determines the performance of the overall control system. The importance of modelling the actuation element accurately, so that the desired control performance is achieved and held over the operating range, can therefore hardly be overstated.

Items such as the actuator's phase-lag characteristics, parameter variation and ageing, sensor noise and the operating scenario — in a flight-control context, the flight conditions — must be considered explicitly during design. A robust control system, one that accounts for all of these, requires a good understanding and an accurate model of the actuation element. This chapter sets out the actuator modelling process.

99.1 Introduction

Power-to-weight considerations have driven actuator design through four generations: pneumatic, hydraulic, electrohydraulic and, most recently in flight-control applications, electrohydrostatic. The point of view taken throughout this chapter is that the actuator element already constitutes a feedback control system in its own right. On that reading the actuator comprises an “amplifier” element (the servo valve), a power element (the ram), an actuator displacement sensor, and a controller about which more is said below.

It is equally important that the actuator subsystem be seen in the context of the complete control system — a flight-control system, say. A relatively simple hydraulic actuator is considered first; the focus then shifts to the modern electrohydrostatic actuator (EHA), which in flight control offers a high degree of combat survivability and easier maintainability because its components are collocated with the actuator. Since the EHA needs no long hydraulic lines, the maintenance time and equipment required are reduced, and the profile exposed to hostile fire shrinks substantially. Actuator failures from various causes and from ageing must also enter the design of the overall system. A robust control system built around an actuator demands an accurate actuator model if it is to meet its performance specification; the EHA and its flight-control application are used here as the vehicle for presenting the modelling process.

Actuator modeling requirements

When an actuator has to be replaced in a control system, for the reasons just cited, by an off-the-shelf unit, the robustness of the overall system must be unaffected by the replacement. The actuator design process therefore entails:

  1. Derivation of an accurate mathematical model for the actuator.
  2. Sensitivity analyses on the actuator control system, covering sensitivity to variations in load, component efficiencies, physical plant parameters and actuator sensor noise.
  3. Identification of a reasonable set of plant variations, based on the sensitivity analysis, which is required for designing a robust actuator control system that will meet the specification in the presence of the predicted variations.

99.2 Hydraulic actuator modeling

The modelling of a purely hydraulic actuator employed in an irreversible flight-control system is presented first. With the notation of Fig. 99.1 the following relationships hold:

(99.1a)x1 = δec·a
(99.1b)(xv − xf)/(b + c) = (xv − x1)/c

which yield

(99.2)xvb + xfc = x1(b + c)

From these equations Fig. 99.2a is obtained. Next, the relationships

(99.3)dxp/dt = Q/A,   Q = Kxv,   δe = xp/(d + e),   xf = e·δe

are used to yield the block diagram of a typical feedback control system (Fig. 99.2b), which represents the irreversible hydraulic actuator under consideration. That diagram simplifies to Fig. 99.2c, which yields the first-order transfer function of the actuator:

(99.4)G(s) = δe(s)/δec(s) = K/(τs + 1)

where the gain K = a(b + c)/(ce), the actuator time constant τ = Ab(d + e)/(cKe), and 0.025 < τ < 0.1 s. For a step command, a typical actuator time response is shown in Fig. 99.3.

More detailed dynamic modelling of the valve leads to higher-order hydraulic actuator models. The transfer function of the actuator currently used on the F-16 is of fourth order [1], and its frequency response is compared with the first-order approximation in Fig. 99.4. The full-blown high-order model should indeed be used in robust control design. As Fig. 99.4 shows, the simplified first-order model fits the high-order one well at low frequency; unfortunately a significant phase difference appears at the higher frequencies where the actuator will actually operate, for those frequencies lie inside the bandwidth of modern robust (“high-gain”) compensators.

The next step in actuator development is the electronic, fly-by-wire mechanisation of the feedback loop, shown in Fig. 99.5: the actuator employs a solenoid-controlled valve, a potentiometer that measures the control-surface deflection (actuator displacement), and a hydraulic cylinder as the power element, the ram.

99.3 EHA actuator modeling

The novel EHA actuator (see, e.g., Reference 2) offers a high degree of maintainability and survivability because all its elements are collocated and no central hydraulic pump with the attendant high-pressure plumbing is needed. The modelling of the EHA modules is outlined first.

Motor

Electric motors, with rotor moment of inertia Jm and electromechanical damping Bm (Fig. 99.6), are subject to variations in output torque and consequent fluctuations in rotor speed Ωm. Since the load torque counteracts the torque the motor generates, perturbation may also come from variations in load torque:

(99.5)τe(s) = τcmd(s) − τload(s)

where τload is the load torque due to the differential pressure of the fluid in the pump. This gives a first-order transfer function

(99.6)Ωm(s)/τe(s) = 1/(Jms + bm)

Pump and fluid

Electric motors have a limited torque-to-mass ratio, because the magnetic flux density that can be generated is finite and limited [3]. High-pressure hydraulic systems, at 2000 to 5000 psi, can generate large forces and so yield more compact arrangements and higher torque-to-mass ratios than electric motors; in general they are also stiffer against the load. The EHA therefore uses a dc motor to pump high-pressure fluid into the piston chamber. The dc motor inside the EHA converts electric power into mechanical power; the pump converts that mechanical power into hydraulic power; and the hydraulic power, acting on the piston, is converted back into mechanical power capable of moving the load — large flight-control surfaces, for instance.

The flow rate Qm generated by the pump is proportional to motor speed:

(99.7)Qm(s) = (Dm/2π)·Ωm(s)

where Dm is the pump displacement constant. The flow rate depends primarily on two things: the change in chamber volume and the change in pressure due to fluid compressibility [4]. Chamber volume changes as the piston moves through the chamber at speed dxp/dt, and the flow due to that change is ±Adxp/dt. Secondary fluid effects — compressibility, internal leakage and external leakage — are modelled by a first-order equation:

(99.8)δQ(s) = Qm(s) − AsXp = [Kss + Cl]P(s)

which gives the transfer function

(99.9)P(s)/δQ(s) = 1/(Kss + Cl)

Piston and flight control surface

Fundamentals

The pressure developed by the pump and fluid acts on the piston surface (Fig. 99.7), causing the ram to extend or retract. That force generates a torque through a hinge which deflects the control surface. The torque has to overcome two load components: control-surface inertia and aerodynamic load. Surface inertia follows from the surface having a certain size and mass. The aerodynamic load occurs only in flight, when air pressure over the surface applies aerodynamic forces to it, and it is fixed by three factors: the surface area of the control surface, the aerodynamic loading — which varies with altitude and airspeed — and the relative angle of the surface to the wind. That angle depends on the surface deflection and on the aircraft angle of attack.

Dynamics

The magnitude of FA acting on the piston is P̄A, where P̄ is the differential pressure developed by the pump and fluid and A the piston area. The force created by pump and fluid is therefore

(99.10)FA = P̄A

The piston dynamics, with piston mass Mp and damping Bp, follow a second-order model:

(99.11)FA − FP = [Mps² + Bps]Xp

The torque acting on the control surface because of this force imbalance is described by

(99.12)τR = (Kh/Rh)(Xp − XL)

where Kh is the hinge stiffness constant and Rh the hinge length. The stabilator inertia acts against the torque the actuator generates, so that

(99.13)τR − τL = [JLs² + BLs]θL

where τL is the torque created by aerodynamic load and stabilator inertia, and JL and BL represent the mass properties of the control surface.

Stiff hinge joint model

Equations 99.11 to 99.13 represent a rather complex model of load dynamics. The complexity drops if the linkage between actuator and control surface is considered rigid — a valid assumption, since the natural frequency of the hinge in a well-designed actuation system is much greater than the actuator bandwidth of concern. The piston and load dynamics are then still expressed as

(99.14)FA − FP = [Mps² + Bps]Xp

and

(99.15)τR − τL = [JLs² + BLs]θL

Dividing Eq. 99.15 by Rh yields

(99.16)τR/Rh − τL/Rh = [(JLs² + BLs)/Rh]θL

Assuming rigidity of the hinge assembly, FR ≈ FP. Adding Eqs. 99.14 and 99.16 then gives

(99.17)FA − τL/Rh = [Mps² + Bps]Xp + [(JLs² + BLs)/Rh]θL

where τL is defined as

(99.18)τL = (Loadaero)θL

Since rigidity implies Xp = XL and θL = Xp/Rh, Eq. 99.17 reduces further to

(99.19)Fe = FA − τL/Rh = {[(Mp + JL/Rh²)s² + (Bp + BL/Rh²)s]}Xp

or, in transfer-function form,

(99.20)Xp(s)/Fe(s) = 1/{s[(Mp + JL/Rh²)s + (Bp + BL/Rh²)]}

Equation 99.20 is a simplified model of the load dynamics. A complete simplified design model of the bare EHA, without the controller, is shown in Fig. 99.8 using the individual component models.

Hinge moments

The aerodynamic loads described above are the torque opposing piston motion. Load generated by the air flowing above and below the control surface applies a torque to the hinge assembly, which in turn adds back-pressure to the piston. The hinge moment is modelled as

(99.21)τL = q̄StRh[Chα(α(s)/θL(s)) + Chδ]θL

where St is the surface area of the control surface, q̄ the dynamic pressure, and Chα and Chδ the hinge-moment coefficients with respect to angle of attack and to surface deflection δ. The dynamic pressure q̄ is a function of airspeed U0 and air density ρ [2]:

(99.22)q̄ = ½ρU0²

Air density in a standard atmosphere drops exponentially with altitude, and q̄ rises as the Mach number rises and altitude falls. For modern fighters with all-movable tails, where Chα ≈ Chδ, the hinge moment is modelled by

(99.23)τL = q̄StRhChδ[α(s)/θL(s) + 1]θL

Aircraft short period approximation

If the aircraft forward speed is taken as constant (speed perturbations u ≈ 0), the X-force equation can be neglected, since it does not contribute significantly to the short-period oscillation [5]. The short-period approximation of the longitudinal channel — the part relevant to actuator dynamics — is then extracted and written as

(99.24)[α̇  q̇]T = [[zα  zq], [mα  mq]]·[α  q]T + [zδ  Mδ]T[δe]

This yields a second-order minimum-phase transfer function of the form

(99.25)α(s)/δ(s) = −K(s + a)/[(s + b)(s + c)]

For the frequency range of interest the short-period approximation closely resembles the full state model. In Fig. 99.9 the solid line is the full-model frequency response and the dots the short-period response: phase and attenuation match closely at high frequency. As the figure also shows, the approximation is not valid below 0.5 rad/s, where the slow longitudinal mode — the phugoid — takes effect.

Quantitative Feedback Theory (QFT)

Robust QFT compensators [3,6] are ideal for obtaining the desired response from an actuator. Robust, in control theory, means the system under control — here the actuator — stays stable throughout its operating envelope, rejects disturbances and degrades the performance specification as little as possible. A QFT-designed feedback system using output feedback ensures that the output tracks the input despite parametric uncertainty or disturbance. The output of a unity-gain feedback system with uncertain plant models can vary with plant condition; the output of a robust QFT system varies far less. A unity-gain system, however, cannot control the tracking response, which is why a prefilter is included.

Plant — specifically actuator — uncertainty may come from manufacturing tolerances. Component A, say, is specified as 10 ± 0.1 mm long; over a large production run its length may lie anywhere between 9.9 and 10.1 mm, and that tolerance range can be wide enough to produce noticeable variations in the plant model. Uncertainty may also come from the environment, specifically the operating conditions: aircraft model parameters, for instance, vary widely with altitude and airspeed. Finally, the performance of components decays over time, adding one more element of uncertainty.

The QFT design paradigm accounts for plant variation inside the design procedure. It is a linear technique for designing a linear robust controller for linear or nonlinear systems [3,6] — here the actuator control system. So long as every QFT design requirement is met, the output responses are guaranteed to conform to the specification boundaries. The first step is to establish the upper and lower tracking boundaries; the maximum allowable disturbance fixes the disturbance boundary, and the phase margin, gain margin or maximum peak value fixes the stability boundary. Together these boundaries set the performance limit of the system. The next step is to analyse the system and identify the parameters that cause noticeable plant variation. The system — often modelled as a single plant transfer function in other design methods — is now modelled more completely as a set of plant transfer functions. The variations form a closed region on the Nichols magnitude-and-phase chart called the template; larger templates mean a higher degree of plant uncertainty, and templates at different frequencies differ in size and shape.

A most important aspect of modelling is the thorough analysis of the bare actuator plant, in order to find the sources of variation. For an aircraft application the three largest sources are fluctuations in aerodynamic load on the control surfaces, motor torque, and hydraulic pump fluid pressure.

99.4 Actuator compensator design

QFT compensators, designed in the frequency domain, are ideally suited to the actuator control system. The compensator processes its inputs — the commanded actuator displacement and the measured current actuator position — and generates a commanded signal to, say, the solenoid valve of a hydraulic actuator or a current for the dc motor of the EHA. To proceed with the design, the block diagram of Fig. 99.7 is transformed into the standard inner and outer QFT loop structure of Fig. 99.10 [2,3,6]. For this design problem the external disturbance d2(t) can be ignored, d2(t) = 0, while d1(t) models a possible measurement bias. The inner loop controls the angular rate of the motor, the outer loop the surface deflection. Since tracking in the inner loop matters less — its function is to reduce the uncertainty level of the outer loop — the inner prefilter can be set to unity and the tracking requirement is relegated to the prefilter FΘ of the outer loop.

99.5 Conclusion

The modelling of a hierarchy of hydraulic actuators has been presented, with emphasis on modern EHAs and carefully developed dynamic models of their subcomponents. A systems approach to actuator modelling and design is taken, and the feedback structure is highlighted. Because of the complex feedback interactions at work, proper actuator modelling requires considering the actuator in the full context of the control system in which it is employed; hence the aerospace application. At the same time the actuator is viewed not as a component of a control system but as a control system in its own right. It follows that the simple mechanical feedback linkages which once fulfilled the role of the controlling element can be replaced by a more flexible, high-performance, robust, full-blown electronic actuator compensator.

As for electropneumatic instruments and sensors, they are critical elements in systems where the flow of liquids or gases is controlled, as in the chemical industries. In aerospace, electropneumatic sensors measure airspeed, altitude, and the angle of attack and sideslip of the aircraft; these functions are collocated in the air data computer (ADC). Modern sensors use feedback action for accuracy enhancement, so the design of modern electropneumatic instruments and sensors is conceptually similar to the design of EHAs. Detailed information on actuators and sensors may be found in References 4 and 7.
