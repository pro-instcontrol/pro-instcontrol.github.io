Position, Location, Altitude Measurement

Dimitris E. Manolakis · Mark A. Stedham · Partha P. Banerjee · Seiji Nishifuji · Shogo Tanaka · Halit Eren · C. C. Fung · Jacob Fraden

Chapter 10 · 73 source pages · 43 figures, 4 tables, 8 co-authors

Chapter 10 is five monographs stapled together by an editor who noticed they were all about the same thing. An altimeter, an attitude reference, an inertial navigator, a satellite fix and a burglar alarm have almost nothing in common as hardware. They have everything in common as mathematics: each one measures a quantity that is only indirectly related to what you want, each one inverts that relation, and each one spends most of its engineering budget on the error analysis of the inversion. That is the thread worth following, and it is the thread the chapter itself never quite names.

One inversion, five costumes

Write the measurement as m = f(x) + n, where x is the state you want, m is what the instrument reports, and n is everything you cannot control. If f is linear and square, the inversion is a matrix inverse and the whole problem is conditioning. If f is nonlinear and over-determined — which is the normal case — the inversion is a Gauss–Newton iteration on the Jacobian, and the covariance of the answer is (JᵀWJ)⁻¹. Every one of the five sections lands on that same expression.

Section 10.1 does it with ranges: three or more slant ranges to known ground stations, linearised about a first guess, iterated until the residual stops shrinking. Section 10.2 does it with gravity: a triad of servo accelerometers reads the direction of the local vertical, and the attitude angles are recovered from the components. Section 10.3 does it by integration instead of algebra — twice, because acceleration integrates to velocity and velocity integrates to position. Section 10.4 does it with pseudoranges and a fourth unknown that is not a coordinate at all. Section 10.5 does it with a threshold, which is the degenerate case: f is a step function and the inversion is a single comparison.

The platform-versus-strapdown distinction in 10.3 is worth reading as a statement about where the inversion happens. A gimballed platform performs the coordinate transformation in metal: the gyros hold a frame, the accelerometers stay level, and the computer receives acceleration already resolved into navigation axes. A strapdown system performs the same transformation in software, at 100 Hz or more, and pays for it with dynamic range — the accelerometers now see the full gravity vector plus all the manoeuvre acceleration, and the gyros must resolve rates spanning five or six decades. What strapdown bought was not accuracy. It bought the removal of a precision mechanical assembly from the bill of materials, and that is why every modern system is strapdown.

The cubic term is the one that kills free-integration systems, and it is worth putting a number on it. Take a gyro drifting at 0.01° h⁻¹ — a good but unremarkable ring-laser grade — which is 4.85 × 10⁻⁸ rad s⁻¹. Left to integrate freely for one hour, the tilt reaches 1.7 × 10⁻⁴ rad, the implied horizontal acceleration error is 1.7 × 10⁻³ m s⁻², and the position error is ⅙ · 9.81 · 4.85 × 10⁻⁸ · (3600)³ ≈ 3.7 km. Nothing has failed. The arithmetic has simply been cubed.

Schuler's 1923 insight, which the chapter mentions in one sentence and then drops, is the fix. If the platform's level loop is tuned to a period of 2π√(R/g) = 84.4 minutes — the period of a pendulum whose length is the Earth's radius — then a constant gyro drift no longer integrates into a growing tilt. It produces a bounded tilt of ε/ωs and a position error that oscillates instead of diverging. The 84-minute Schuler oscillation is visible in every inertial navigation trace ever plotted, and it is the reason a 0.01° h⁻¹ gyro can support a transatlantic flight while the free-integration arithmetic above says it cannot.

The optical gyros that replaced the spinning mass trade the same currency in a different denomination. The Sagnac phase shift for a coil of N turns, area A, at rotation rate Ω is Δφ = 8πN AΩ/(λc). Put numbers in: a 10 cm diameter coil with 1000 turns, at 1550 nm, sensing the Earth's rotation rate of 7.29 × 10⁻⁵ rad s⁻¹, gives Δφ ≈ 3 × 10⁻⁵ rad — about 31 microradians, which is small but entirely measurable with a photodiode and a good phase detector. Scale the coil up and the shift scales with area, not with mass; that is why a fibre gyro can be made accurate by winding more fibre, while a spinning-mass gyro can only be made accurate by machining better bearings.

Geometry is destiny

Section 10.4 contains the other idea that generalises: the accuracy of a radiolocation fix is not a property of the transmitter, it is a property of where you are standing relative to the transmitters. The rms circle of Figure 10-29 looks like a statement about noise, but the interesting number is how that circle deforms as the geometry degenerates. Two ranges give two circles and their two intersections; as the two stations and the receiver approach collinearity, the intersections merge and the fix runs away along the line. Three ranges give three circles that should meet at a point and instead enclose a small triangle — the residual of the least-squares solution, and a direct measure of how much to trust the answer.

The hyperbolic systems make the same point in a form that is easier to see. A time-difference measurement defines a hyperbola, not a circle, and the family of hyperbolas for one station pair fans out from the baseline. Near the baseline the hyperbolas are crowded and a small position change produces a large time-difference change — high sensitivity, good accuracy. Far out along the baseline extension the hyperbolas spread apart and the same timing error becomes a large position error. The chapter's rule of thumb that the user should stay within about six baseline lengths is exactly this geometric dilution, stated as a number instead of a gradient. The same quantity appears in GPS under the name DOP, and it is the reason a fix taken with four satellites clustered in one part of the sky is worse than a fix taken with four satellites spread across it, even though the signal-to-noise ratio is identical.

One equation, two industries

The Doppler relation Δf ≈ v/λ₀ appears twice in this chapter, in contexts that share nothing but the physics. In 10.4 it is the basis of the Transit satellite fix: the satellite moves at 7.5 km s⁻¹, the received frequency sweeps through a few tens of kilohertz as it passes, and the shape of that sweep against time locates the receiver on a cone about the satellite's track. In 10.5 the same equation is the basis of a microwave burglar alarm: a person walking at 0.6 m s⁻¹ toward an X-band detector at 3 cm wavelength produces a 20 Hz beat note, which is exactly the frequency a cheap audio amplifier likes to work at.

The difference between the two applications is entirely in what you do with the sign and the geometry. Transit needs the full time history, because the curve's shape — its inflection point, its asymptotes — carries the range and the closest-approach distance. The motion detector needs only the existence of a nonzero beat, because its question is binary. That is why the detector costs a few dollars and the satellite fix costs a few thousand: same equation, different amount of information extracted from it.

GPS: the fourth unknown

The elegant move in the GPS design is not the satellites, the codes, or the frequencies. It is that the receiver's own clock error is promoted to an unknown. Four pseudoranges give four equations in four unknowns — x, y, z and the clock bias — and the solution is exact. A quartz oscillator good to a microsecond would otherwise contribute 300 m of range error; treating that error as a coordinate to be solved for converts a hardware problem into an arithmetic one. Add a fifth satellite and the system becomes over-determined, which is what allows receiver-autonomous integrity monitoring: the fifth residual does not have to be small, it only has to be consistent.

Dual frequency is the same trick applied to the atmosphere. Ionospheric delay goes as 1/f², so two carriers widely separated in frequency give two different delays for the same path, and the difference between them determines the delay without any model of the ionosphere at all. That is why L1 and L2 are where they are, and it is why the correction works even during a geomagnetic storm, when every model fails. The differential technique of Figure 10-36 extends the logic to everything the two receivers share: a reference station at a known point measures the total error — ionosphere, troposphere, ephemeris, clock — and broadcasts it as a scalar correction. What it cannot remove is the part of the error that is not shared, which is why DGPS accuracy degrades with distance from the reference station and why the sub-centimetre figures quoted in the chapter require carrier-phase processing rather than code ranging.

The two orbital altitudes are worth flagging as a specific kind of error, because it is the kind that survives editing. Both are off by roughly a factor of ten, and both would be caught by Kepler's third law in one line: a 12-hour period requires a semi-major axis of 26 560 km, which is an altitude of 20 200 km, full stop. A 2200 km orbit has a period of about 2 hours. The chapter's own Figure 10-35 shows six orbital planes with four satellites each, which is the real constellation and is inconsistent with its own text. When a figure and a sentence disagree, believe the figure.

Occupancy: detecting a change, or detecting a body

Section 10.5 opens with a distinction that most of the industry still gets wrong. An occupancy sensor reports a body; a motion sensor reports a change. Almost every cheap product on the market is the second and is sold as the first, which is why the lights go out while you are sitting still reading.

The reason is structural, not a manufacturing defect. A passive infrared detector is a pyroelectric device, and a pyroelectric device responds to the rate of change of its own temperature, not to its temperature. A stationary warm body produces a steady heat flux, the element's temperature stabilises, the polarisation stops changing, and the output returns to zero. The detector is working perfectly. It is answering a different question from the one the occupant is asking. To detect a stationary person you need a measurement of an absolute quantity that a body perturbs — dielectric loading, acoustic path length, weight on the floor, the CO₂ concentration in the room — and that is exactly what the seventeen-item list at the head of 10.5 is: a list of physical quantities a human body disturbs.

The dual-element PIR of Figure 10-43 is the best piece of engineering in the chapter, and it is worth spelling out why. A pyroelectric element is also piezoelectric, because the same non-centrosymmetric crystal structure that allows a spontaneous polarisation also allows a stress-induced one. So the element cannot distinguish "I was heated" from "I was pushed". Wind, footfall, a door slam and a warm body all produce the same charge. The fix is symmetry: two identical elements wired in series opposition, and an optical system that focuses the thermal image onto one element at a time. A common-mode disturbance — the room warming up, the whole housing being flexed — reaches both elements equally and cancels at the amplifier input. A moving body reaches one element and then the other, producing a bipolar pulse that no common-mode signal can imitate. The sensor is not made more sensitive; it is made deaf to everything that is not a moving target. That is the same strategy as a differential accelerometer, a bridge strain gauge, and a common-mode-rejecting front end on any instrument in this handbook, and it is the single most transferable idea in section 10.5.

The capacitive detector of Figure 10-38 shows the alternative and its cost. It measures an absolute quantity — the capacitance from a probe plate to earth, loaded by a nearby body — so it does detect a stationary person. But the quantity it measures is a few picofarads sitting on top of tens of picofarads of stray capacitance to everything else in the room, and the signal is a small fraction of that. Hence the driven shield the chapter mentions in one line: the only way to measure a small capacitance in a hostile environment is to make the environment not matter, by driving the guard electrode at the same potential as the probe so that no current flows through the stray path. It is the same principle as a guard ring on a PCB, and it is the difference between a sensor that works in a laboratory and one that works next to a filing cabinet.

The through-line

Read end to end, the chapter is a catalogue of ways to answer "where is it?" and "is anyone there?" with indirect measurements. The instruments differ by six orders of magnitude in cost and by nine in range. The method does not: build a model of what the sensor should read, invert it, characterise the residual, and then arrange the geometry so that the residual is small where you need the answer. The radar altimeter, the ring-laser gyro, the GPS pseudorange and the burglar alarm are the same calculation wearing different clothes.
