Proximity Sensing for Robotics

R. E. Saad · A. Bonen · K. C. Smith · B. Benhabib · University of Toronto

Chapter 8 · 17 source pages · 17 figures, 1 table

This chapter is a survey written in 1999, and its value today is not the catalogue of technologies — that has moved — but the argument it makes about where a proximity sensor sits in a robot's control loop. The authors are explicit: the sensor exists to bridge the uncertainty left by medium-range pose estimation. That single sentence sets every design constraint that follows, and it is worth unpacking before the hardware.

A robot that has already decided where an object is does not need a proximity sensor. A robot that has an estimate good to a centimetre and a few degrees needs something that will close the last fifty millimetres and the last thirty degrees, fast, without touching. So the specification is not “measure distance” but “measure distance and orientation over the uncertainty region, at a rate that does not slow the arm.” The chapter's 1–10 ms figure is a control-loop requirement, not a metrology one: at a typical closing speed the gripper moves a few tenths of a millimetre in a millisecond, so anything slower than about 10 ms leaves the estimate stale by the time it is used.

The pose definition is worth reading carefully, because it is not the usual Euler triple. x is measured along the sensor's own X axis, and the two angles are applied in a specific order — u about Y, then v about the resulting Z. That order matters: rotating about Z first and then about Y gives a different frame for the same physical surface. The chapter is defining a frame, not a set of independent angles, and any implementation that treats u and v as interchangeable will produce a systematic error that grows with tilt.

The four reflection phenomena are the physical core of the chapter, and they explain why optical proximity sensing is harder than it looks. Specular reflection from a planar microfacet returns a beam; diffuse return after penetration scatters; corner reflection diffracts. Which one dominates depends on the material, the wavelength and the surface finish — and the sensor has no way of knowing in advance. Every optical technique in the chapter is, in effect, a strategy for extracting geometry from a return whose amplitude is contaminated by an unknown mixture of those four.

Phase modulation is the most elegant answer to that problem, because it throws the amplitude away. Drive two sources in quadrature, sum the returns, and the phase of the sum depends on the path difference while the amplitude depends on reflectivity. Equation 8.8 makes the point exactly: the reflection factor C cancels out of the phase, so a machined aluminium block and a matt black plastic block at the same distance give the same φ. That is a genuinely material-independent measurement, and it is rare.

The price is that phase alone gives one number, and one number cannot give three pose parameters. Hence the six-LED head: different LEDs are driven in different combinations to produce different phase equations, and the system solves them in sequence — x first, then v, then u. The dependence is explicit in the text: to recover v from equation 8.9 you must already know x. This is a cascaded estimator, and its error propagates: an error in x becomes an error in v, which becomes an error in u. The chapter does not say so, but that cascade is why the reported accuracy degrades with tilt.

Amplitude modulation takes the opposite trade. It keeps the amplitude and normalises it away by differencing: DV = (V₁ − V₂)/(V₁ + V₂). If both receivers see the same source power P₀ and both convert light to voltage linearly, P₀ cancels in the ratio. The requirement of linearity is not a detail — it is the whole scheme. A receiver that compresses at high illumination will leave a residual P₀ dependence that looks exactly like a distance error.

The Y-guide is the chapter's best illustration of a general instrumentation principle: the response of a bifurcated fibre bundle is not monotonic, because the emitter cone and the receiver acceptance overlap in a way that peaks and then falls. The peak is not a nuisance to be designed out; it is a hard boundary. Below x_min the same brightness corresponds to two distances, and no amount of signal processing can tell them apart. Every Y-guide datasheet's “minimum range” is that ambiguity, drawn as a number.

The symmetric three-fibre constellation converts the same physics into an orientation measurement. Tilt the surface one way and one receiver gains while the other loses; the normalised difference is monotonic through zero and, crucially, insensitive to distance because both receivers sit at the same range. That insensitivity is the mirror image of the asymmetric pair's behaviour, where distance and tilt are entangled and must be separated by solving orientation first. The chapter states the asymmetry plainly, and it is the single most useful design rule in the section.

The one-emitter, eight-receiver head in Figure 8.11 is the mature version of the idea, and its numbers are worth pausing on: 6.25 µm and 0.02° over 0–6 mm, falling to a 50 mm and ±20° envelope. That is a dynamic range of four orders of magnitude in distance for a device built from plastic fibre and an LED. It is achieved not by better optics but by abandoning the closed-form DV scheme for a polynomial fit over all eight signals — a reminder that when the physics is messy, calibration beats algebra.

The geometrical family trades the amplitude problem for a geometry problem. Triangulation reads position on a detector array, so surface reflectivity only affects signal-to-noise, not the answer — which is why the chapter can claim the method is indifferent to irregularity and orientation. The cost is the baseline: h and y_o must be known precisely, and the resolution in x falls as the square of the distance, because a fixed spot displacement corresponds to a shrinking angle.

The Gaussian-lens family is the neatest of all. Put the source on the optical axis, image the spot, and x = fw/(w − f) follows from the thin-lens law alone — no baseline, no triangulation angle, and the source sits at the lens centre where it also maximises collected flux. The HexEye multiplies that unit seven times and gets two modes from one array: the active unit measures by the lens law, the passive ones by triangulation, and groups of three integrate into a local orientation. It is a genuinely elegant architecture, and it is the one place in the chapter where the sensor is designed as a system rather than a transducer.

The capacitive and ultrasonic sections are short, and both are honest about their limits. The parallel-plate capacitor is the clearest case in the book of a sensor whose sensitivity is best where its range is worst: C ∝ 1/d, so resolution is excellent at a tenth of a millimetre and useless at ten. The fringing variant escapes the conductivity requirement but inherits a material dependence that forces per-material calibration — which, for a robot handling unknown objects, is close to disqualifying.

The ultrasonic ring sensor is the one place where the chapter's own numbers tell a clean story. Six receivers at R = 30 mm give ±15°; eight at R = 20 mm give ±30°. Smaller ring, wider range — and the authors note, correctly, that smaller also means less accurate. The reason is simple: δ scales with R, so shrinking R shrinks the signal that carries the angle. Range and resolution are bought from the same purse.

What has not changed is the central difficulty. Every technique here still has to separate three unknowns — distance, tilt, reflectivity — from a signal that mixes them. Phase modulation removes reflectivity and cascades the rest. Amplitude modulation normalises reflectivity and cascades the rest. Triangulation and the lens law sidestep reflectivity entirely and pay in baseline precision. There is no free measurement; there is only a choice of which unknown to eliminate first, and the chapter is a careful account of that choice.
