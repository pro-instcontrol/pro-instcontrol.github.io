Bore Gaging Displacement Sensors

Viktor P. Astakhov · Concordia University

Section 6.6 · 7 figures · about 4,133 words

The section that follows is the odd one out in this chapter. Every other section measures a displacement with some physical effect; this one measures a hole, and the displacement is the small motion of a spring-loaded contact as it follows the wall. The engineering problem is not the transducer — it is the tolerance budget that decides how precisely the hole has to be made in the first place, and how much of that budget the gage itself is allowed to consume.

Viktor Astakhov opens with the tolerance argument, and it is worth keeping in view: a gage is only ever as good as the tolerance it has to police. The 10% rule — gage tolerance no larger than a tenth of the workpiece tolerance — is the whole design brief in one line.

The tolerance chain Chuỗi dung sai

ISO R 286 grades tolerances from IT 01 to IT 16, with the value in each grade rising with size according to a formula tied to the geometric mean diameter of the size band. Eighteen grades, 27 fundamental deviations below 500 mm and 14 above it, and every fit from loose clearance to heavy interference is built from those two choices. Nothing about the measurement is decided until those are fixed.

The nine classes of bore gage then sort themselves by when in the production cycle they act: master gages for reference, inspection gages for the inspector, manufacturer’s gages during production, post-process gages after the part is finished, and in-process gages inside the machining cycle. Post-process gaging with feedback is the interesting one — inspection data flows back into the part program of a CNC machine or the template of a tracer, and repeatable path errors are compensated rather than merely detected.

Fixed limit versus variable indicating Giới hạn cố định và chỉ thị biến thiên

A plug gage answers one question: is the bore between the limits? The go end must enter, the no-go end must not. Figure 6.53 shows the three AGD arrangements — two separate members, two members on one handle, and the progressive gage where the go portion leads the no-go portion on a single body. None of them produces a number.

Table 6.11 is the reason they can be trusted. Four gagemaker’s tolerance classes, XX through Z, cap the permissible error of the gage itself; class XX is roughly 0.5 µm on a 20 mm bore, class Z about five times that. Which class you buy is decided by how much of the part tolerance you are willing to spend on the gage.

A dial bore gage gives a number, and the number is a deviation from a master ring rather than an absolute diameter. That is the point of zeroing on the ring: the instrument never has to be accurate in absolute terms, only repeatable and linear over the narrow band around nominal. It is a comparative instrument wearing an absolute instrument’s clothes.

The three-point internal micrometer in Figure 6.56 solves a different problem. Two contacts can only define a chord; three contacts at 120° define a circle, so the instrument centres itself in the bore and stays aligned with the axis however deep the hole runs. That is why the same head works at the bottom of a blind hole, where a two-point gage would rock.

Where the air gage wins Đầu đo khí mạnh ở đâu

The air plug gage measures nothing directly. It measures the back pressure of a circuit whose restriction is the clearance between two jets and the bore wall. The non-linearity in Figure 6.57 is not a defect to be corrected — it is why the instrument is so sensitive: near zero clearance, a micrometre of extra diameter changes the pressure by far more than the same micrometre would change a dial reading. The price is a short working range and a hard dependence on supply pressure.

Five form errors follow directly from having more than one jet: taper, bell mouthing, barrelling, ovality and lobing. A single-jet plug sees only the diameter at its own plane and is blind to all five. Two jets at different axial positions separate taper from barrelling; two jets in the same plane separate ovality from lobing only if you rotate the plug, which is why three-point electronic heads exist.

Form, and the limits of a number Hình dạng, và giới hạn của một con số

A roundness machine abandons the diameter entirely. The part turns, the head stays still, and what is recorded is the radial deviation from a least-squares reference circle, magnified by a factor chosen to make the trace legible. The polar plot in Figure 6.59 is the result: a three-lobed shape that no single diameter measurement could have revealed, because every diameter across it is very nearly the same.

The fully automatic machines add the one thing manual staging cannot guarantee — that the part axis and the table axis coincide. The RA-600 centres and levels itself from a preliminary scan, then measures roundness, cylindricity, squareness and total runout without an operator touching a knob. The measurement is only as good as that alignment, which is why the machine spends its first seconds doing nothing but alignment.

GAGE R and R GAGE R and R

Repeatability is one operator, one gage, many trials. Reproducibility is many operators, one gage. The two together decide whether a measuring system is fit for a particular tolerance — and the answer is tolerance-dependent. A gage that passes at 50 mm may fail at 250 mm, because the same absolute error is a larger fraction of a tighter budget at the bigger size. Dyer’s published figures for its 830 and 230 series — linearity ±1 µm, repeatability better than ±0.25 µm — are worth remembering as the order of magnitude a good mechanical bore gage reaches before any electronics are involved.

The three sources of variation are worth separating because only one of them is the gage’s fault. The gage contributes linearity, repeatability, stability and calibration. The operator contributes repeatability and reproducibility. The production line contributes surface finish, setup error and temperature. Buying a better gage fixes the first group and does nothing for the other two.
