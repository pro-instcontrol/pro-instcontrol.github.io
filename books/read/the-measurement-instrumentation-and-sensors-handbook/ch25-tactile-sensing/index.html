Tactile Sensing

R. E. Saad · A. Bonen · K. C. Smith · B. Benhabib · University of Toronto

Chapter 25 · 12 figures, 19 equations, 44 references

Robots in industrial settings perform repetitive tasks such as machine loading, parts assembly, painting and welding. Only in rare instances can these autonomous manipulators modify their actions on the basis of sensory information. Although the vast majority of research in robot sensing has so far concentrated on computer vision, contact sensing is an equally important capability and has received some attention as well. Without tactile perception a robot cannot be expected to grasp objects effectively. Robotic tactile sensing is therefore the focus of this chapter.

25.1 Sensing Classification Phân loại cảm biến

Robotic sensing can be classified as either noncontact or contact [1]. Noncontact sensing involves interaction between the robot and its environment through some physical phenomenon — acoustic or electromagnetic waves, for instance — that acts without contact; the most important sensors of this type are vision and proximity sensors. Contact sensing, on the other hand, implies measurement of the general interaction that takes place when the robot’s end effector is brought into contact with an object, and it is further divided into force sensing and tactile sensing.

Force sensing is the measurement of the global mechanical effects of contact, whereas tactile sensing is the detection of a wide range of local parameters affected by contact. The most significant of those contact-based effects are contact stresses, slippage, heat transfer and hardness.

The properties of a grasped object that can be derived from tactile sensing fall into geometric and dynamometric classes [2]. The geometric properties include presence, location relative to the end effector, shape and dimensions, and surface condition [3–7]; the dynamometric parameters associated with grasping include force distribution, slippage, elasticity and hardness, and friction [8–12].

Tactile sensing requires sophisticated transducers, yet the availability of those transducers alone is not a sufficient condition for successful tactile sensing. The modalities through which the sensor interacts with the explored objects — contact forces as well as end-effector position and orientation — must also be controlled accurately [13–15]. This leads to active tactile sensing, which demands a high degree of complexity in the acquisition and processing of the tactile data [16].

25.2 Mechanical Effects of Contact Các hiệu ứng cơ học của tiếp xúc

Tactile sensing normally involves a rigid object indenting the compliant cover layer of a tactile sensor array [17], Figure 25.1. The indentation of a compliant layer by contact can be analysed from two conceptually different points of view [1]. The first is the measurement of the actual contact stresses — the force distribution — in the layer, which is usually what matters for controlling manipulation tasks. The second is the deflection profile of the layer, which is usually what matters for recognising geometrical object features. Different processing and control algorithms must be used depending on the approach adopted.

A definite relationship exists between the local shape of a contacting body and a set of subsurface strains or displacements, but it is quite complex. It therefore requires the Theory of Elasticity and Contact Mechanics to model sensor–object interaction [18], and Finite Element Analysis (FEA) as the practical tool for obtaining a more representative model of the sensor [19].

The study of tactile sensors generally comprises two steps: (1) the forward analysis, concerned with acquiring data from the sensor — the changes in stress or strain induced by an object indenting the compliant surface of the transducer; and (2) the inverse problem, normally concerned with recovering the force distribution or, in some cases, the shape of the indentor.

25.2.1 Simplified Theory for Tactile Sensing Lý thuyết giản lược cho cảm biến xúc giác

For simplicity the general two-dimensional tactile problem is reduced here to a one-dimensional one. Figure 25.2 shows a one-dimensional transducer consisting of a compliant, homogeneous, isotropic and linear layer subjected to a normal stress qv(x) created by the indentation of an object.

For modelling purposes the compliant layer is assumed to be an elastic half-space. This simplification yields closed-form equations for the analysis and avoids the more complex problem in which the effect of the boundary conditions at xmin and xmax must be taken into account. Modelling the sensor as an elastic half-space has been shown to be a reasonable approximation to the real case [18]. Under those conditions the normal strain at a depth y = d due to the normal stress qv(y) is given by [20]:

(25.1)εz(x) = ∫−∞∞ qv(x − x0) hz(x0, d) dx0

where εz is the strain at x and z = d due to the normal stress on the surface, and

(25.2)hz(x) = − 2d(1 + ν)[d2(1 − ν) − νx2] ⁄ [πrE(x2 + d2)2]

E and ν are respectively the modulus of elasticity and the Poisson coefficient of the compliant layer. Equation 25.2 assumes that the analysis is performed under planar strain conditions. A similar analysis can be performed for tangential contact stresses or strains.

The normal displacement at the surface, w, is given by:

(25.3)w(x) = ∫−∞∞ qv(x − x0) k(x0) dx0

where

(25.4)k(x) = [−2(1 − ν2) ⁄ (πE)] log|x ⁄ xa|

The singularity at x = 0 is expected because the stress itself is singular there. Note that k(x) is the deformation of the surface when a singular load of 1 N is applied at x = 0. The constant xa should be chosen so that the deformation is zero at x = xa. Here zero deformation should occur as x → ∞ — the sensor being modelled by an elastic half-space — that is, xa → ∞. The problem is associated with the two-dimensional deformation of an elastic half-space. To eliminate this difficulty the boundary conditions of the transducer must be taken into account, which means analysing a finite transducer and in general requires FEA.

Since strain (or stress) measurements are usually made by a discrete number of sensing elements, Equation 25.2 must be discretised (Figure 25.3), and the force distribution must correspondingly be reconstructed at discrete positions as shown in Figure 25.3. Let Δxq be the distance between the points at which the force distribution is to be reconstructed from strain (or stress) measurements made by sensing elements uniformly spaced at intervals Δxp at z = d. Assume, although it is not necessary, that Δxq = Δxp = Δx and that the forces are applied immediately above the sensing elements. One can then define the strain (stress) sample vector ζ, whose components are ζi = εx(xi), i = 1, 2, …, n, and the force distribution vector F, whose components are fi = qv(xj), j = 1, 2, …, n. The discrete form of Equation 25.1 is then:

(25.5)ζ = TF

where the elements of matrix T are Tij = kv(xi − xj), i = 1, 2, …, n and j = 1, 2, …, n [23]. A relation similar to Equation 25.5 can be obtained by discretising Equation 25.3. In the general case, where Δxq ≠ Δxp, T is not square; and in the general case the vector F comprises both vertical and tangential components.

Equations 25.1 and 25.3 represent the regular forward problem, while Equation 25.5 represents its discretised version. The inverse problem in most cases consists of recovering the applied force profile from measurements of strain, stress or deflection. (Surface displacement can also be used to recover the shape of the indentor.)

Reference [20] showed that the inverse problem is ill-posed because the operators h and k of Equations 25.1 and 25.3 respectively are ill-conditioned; the inverse problem is consequently susceptible to noise, and regularisation techniques must be used to solve it [20].

It has been proven that, to avoid aliasing when determining the continuous strain (stress) at a depth d with a discretised transducer, the elements must be separated by one tenth of the compliant layer’s thickness. Good results without much aliasing were nevertheless obtained with sensing elements separated by a distance equal to the sensor’s depth [18].

25.2.2 Requirements for Tactile Sensors Yêu cầu đối với cảm biến xúc giác

In 1980 Harmon conducted a survey to determine general specifications for tactile sensors [21]. Those specifications have since been used as guidelines by many tactile sensor designers:

  1. Spatial resolution of 1 to 2 mm
  2. Array sizes of 5 × 10 to 10 × 20 points
  3. Sensitivity of 0.5 × 10−2 to 1 × 10−2 N for each force-sensing element (tactel)
  4. Dynamic range of 1000:1
  5. Stable behaviour with no hysteresis
  6. Sampling rate of 100 Hz to 1 kHz
  7. Monotonic response, though not necessarily linear
  8. Compliant interface, rugged and inexpensive

Properties (5), (7) and (8) should apply to any practical sensor; the others are merely suggestions, particularly as regards the number of array elements and the spatial resolution. Developments since [21] have identified additional desirable qualities: reliability, modularity, speed and the availability of multisensor support [16].

25.3 Technologies for Tactile Sensing Các công nghệ cho cảm biến xúc giác

The technologies associated with tactile sensing are quite diverse; extensive surveys of the state of the art in robotic tactile transduction have been presented in [2, 3, 16, 17]. Some of them are discussed briefly below.

25.3.1 Resistive Điện trở

The transduction method that has received most attention in tactile sensor design concerns the change in resistance of a conductive material under applied pressure. A basic configuration of a resistive transducer is shown in Figure 25.4. Each resistor, whose value changes with the magnitude of the force, represents one resistive cell of the transducer, and a variety of materials has been used to manufacture the basic cell.

Conductive elastomers were among the first resistive materials used for tactile sensors. They are insulating natural or silicone-based rubbers made conductive by adding particles of conductive or semiconductive material such as silver or carbon. The changes in resistivity under pressure arise from two basically different physical mechanisms. In the first, the change is associated with deformation that alters the particle density within the elastomer; two typical designs of this kind are given in [22, 23]. In the second, the bulk resistance of the elastomer changes only slightly when it is compressed, but the design allows the contact area between the elastomer and an electrode to increase, and the contact resistance to change accordingly; a typical design is given in [24]. A newer tactile sensor with both three-axis force sensing and slippage sensing is reported in [25]; its pressure-sensing function uses arrays of pressure transducers that measure the change in contact resistance between a specially treated polyimide film and a resistive substrate.

Piezoresistive elements have also been used in several tactile sensors, a technology that is particularly attractive at present because micromachining allows the piezoresistive elements to be integrated with the signal-processing circuits on a single chip [26]. A 32 × 32-element silicon pressure sensor array incorporating CMOS processing circuits for the detection of a high-resolution pressure distribution was reported in [8]. The array consists of pressure cells organised as an x–y matrix with a cell spacing of 250 µm, and the CMOS processing circuits are formed around the array on the same chip. Fabrication used a 3 mm CMOS process combined with silicon micromachining; the diaphragm size is 50 µm × 50 µm and the overall chip size is 10 mm × 10 mm.

Figure 25.4 shows a circuit topology for scanning a 3 × 3 array of piezoresistive elements. The basic idea was originally proposed in [24] and has been adapted on several occasions by different researchers. Resistance changes are converted into output voltages; with the connections shown, the resistance R21 is determined from:

(25.6)V0 = (Rf ⁄ R21) Vcc

where V0 is the output voltage, Vcc the bias voltage and Rf the feedback resistance of the output amplifier stage.

One problem with the configuration of Figure 25.4 is the difficulty of detecting small changes in resistance against the internal resistance of the multiplexer and against changes in the supply voltage, both of which strongly influence the output. Other methods used to scan resistive transducer arrays are summarised in [3].

When piezoresistors and circuits are fabricated on the same silicon substrate, the array can be equipped with a complex switching circuit next to the sensing elements that allows better resolution in the measurements [9].

25.3.2 Capacitive Điện dung

Tactile sensors in this category measure capacitance, which varies under applied load. The capacitance of a parallel-plate capacitor depends on the separation of the plates and on their areas; a sensor using an elastomeric separator between the plates provides compliance so that the capacitance varies with the applied normal load, Figure 25.5(a).

Figure 25.5(b) shows the basic configuration of a capacitive tactile sensor. The intersections of rows and columns of conductor strips form capacitors, and each individual capacitance can be determined by measuring the corresponding output voltage at the selected row and column. To reduce cross-talk and electromagnetic interference the unselected rows and columns are grounded. Figure 25.5(c) shows an equivalent circuit for measuring the capacitance formed at the intersection of row i and column j, Cij. Rd is the input resistance of the detector and Cd represents the effects of stray capacitances — the detector-amplifier input capacitance, the stray capacitance to the unselected rows and columns, and the capacitance of the cable connecting transducer to detector. Because the stray capacitance due to the unselected rows and columns changes with the applied forces, the cable capacitance is designed to predominate [18].

The magnitude of the voltage at the input of the detector, |Vd|, is given by:

(25.7)|Vd| = CijRdω |Vs| ⁄ √(1 + [ωRd(Cij + Cd)]2)

Assuming that Cd » Cij and that ω is sufficiently large,

(25.8)|Vd| ≅ (Cij ⁄ Cd) |Vs|

When a load is applied the capacitor deforms as shown in Figure 25.5(a). For modelling purposes the plate capacitor is assumed to be under compression only. With no load applied, the capacitance of the element in the i-th row and j-th column, C0ij, is:

(25.9)C0ij = ε wl ⁄ h0

where ε is the permittivity of the dielectric, w and l the width and length of the plate capacitor, and h0 the plate separation with no load. The detector input voltage for this particular case is Vd0, so from Equation 25.8:

(25.10)Vd0 ≅ (C0ij ⁄ Cd) |Vs|

With a load applied the capacitor is under compression and the capacitance is:

(25.11)Cij = ε wl ⁄ (h0 − Δh)

The strain in this case is given by:

(25.12)ζz ≡ Δh ⁄ h0

where Δh is the displacement of the top metal plate and Δh « h0. The strain can be measured by:

(25.13)(|Vd| − |Vd0|) ⁄ |Vd| = (Cij − C0ij) ⁄ Cij = 1 − C0ij ⁄ Cij = 1 − (h0 − Δh) ⁄ h0 = Δh ⁄ h0 ≡ ζz

Consequently the strain at each tactel can be determined by measuring the magnitudes of Vd and Vd0 for each element.

Note that a tangential force would offset the plates tangentially and change the effective area of the capacitor plates. An ideal capacitive pressure sensor can quantify the basic aspects of touch by sensing normal forces and can detect slippage by measuring tangential forces; distinguishing between the two at the output of a single sensing element is, however, a difficult task and requires a more complex transducer than the one in Figure 25.5(a) [27].

Micromachined silicon-based capacitive devices are especially attractive for their potential high accuracy and low drift. A sensor with 1024 elements and a spatial resolution of 0.5 mm was reported in [28]; several possible structures for implementing high-density capacitive tactile transducers in silicon are reported in [29], and a cylindrical finger-shaped transducer in [18].

The advantages of capacitive transducers include wide dynamic range, linear response and robustness; their major disadvantages are susceptibility to noise, sensitivity to temperature, and the fact that capacitance decreases with physical size, which ultimately limits the spatial resolution. Research is progressing towards electronic processing circuits for measuring small capacitances with charge amplifiers [30] and towards new capacitive structures [29].

25.3.3 Piezoelectric Áp điện

A material is called piezoelectric if it produces electricity when subjected to a stress or deformation. The longitudinal piezoelectric effect occurs when the electricity is produced in the same direction as the stress, Figure 25.6: in Figure 25.6(a) a normal stress σ (= F/A) is applied along Direction 3 and charges are generated on the surfaces perpendicular to Direction 3. A transversal piezoelectric effect occurs when the electricity is produced perpendicular to the stress.

The voltage V generated across the electrodes by the stress σ is given by:

(25.14)V = d33 (h ⁄ ε) σ

where d33 = piezoelectric constant associated with the longitudinal piezoelectric effect
ε = permittivity
h = thickness of the piezoelectric material

Since piezoelectric materials are insulators, the transducer of Figure 25.6(a) can be regarded as a capacitor from an electrical point of view. Consequently,

(25.15)V = Q ⁄ C = (Q ⁄ εA) h

where Q = charge induced by the stress σ
C = capacitance of the parallel capacitor
A = area of each electrode

Comparing Equations 25.14 and 25.15 leads to:

(25.16)Q = d33 σA

The force applied to the piezoelectric material can therefore be determined by finding the charge Q, and charge amplifiers are usually used for that purpose. The basic configuration of a charge amplifier is shown in Figure 25.6(b): the charge generated in the transducer is transferred to the capacitor Cf and the output voltage is

(25.17)Vo = − Q ⁄ Cf

The circuit must periodically discharge the feedback capacitor Cf to avoid saturation of the amplifier by stray charges generated by the offset voltages and currents of the operational amplifier. This is achieved with a switch, as in Figure 25.6(b), or with a resistor in parallel with Cf.

The piezoelectric material most widely used in tactile transducers is PVF2, which shows the largest piezoelectric effect of any known material; its flexibility, small size, sensitivity and large electrical output offer many advantages for sensor applications in general and tactile sensors in particular. Examples of tactile sensors implemented with this technology can be found in [1, 31].

The major advantages of piezoelectric technology are its wide dynamic range and durability. Unfortunately the response of available materials does not extend down to dc, so steady loads cannot be measured directly; and PVF2 produces a charge output that is prone to electrical interference and is temperature dependent.

The possibility of measuring transient phenomena with piezoelectric material has recently encouraged some researchers to use the piezoelectric effect for detecting vibrations that indicate incipient slip, the occurrence of contact, local change in skin curvature, and for estimating the friction and hardness of an object [7, 10, 11]. If the piezoelectric transducer of Figure 25.6(a) is connected to an FET-input operational amplifier configured as a current-to-voltage converter, as in Figure 25.7, the output voltage is

(25.18)Vo = (dQ ⁄ dt) Rf = A Rf d33 (dσ ⁄ dt)

where Rf is the feedback resistor. The circuit accordingly provides the means to measure changes in the contact stress; a detailed explanation of the behaviour of this sensor can be found in [7].

25.3.4 Optical Quang

Recent developments in fibre optic technology and solid-state cameras have led to numerous novel tactile sensor designs [32, 33]. Some of them employ flexible membranes incorporating a reflecting surface, Figure 25.8. Light is introduced into the sensor through a fibre optic cable; a wide cone of light propagates out of the fibre, reflects back from the membrane and is collected by a second fibre. When an external force is applied to the elastomer it shortens the distance h between the reflective side of the membrane and the fibres, so the light gathered by the receiving fibre changes as a function of h, Figure 25.8(b). Recovering the distance unambiguously from the light intensity requires a monotonic function, which is obtained by designing the transducer to operate for h > hmin, where hmin is indicated in Figure 25.8(b); the region h > hmin is preferred to h < hmin for dynamic-range reasons.

Another optical effect that can be used is frustrated total internal reflection [5, 34]. An elastic rubber membrane covers, without touching, a glass plate — the waveguide — and light entering the side edge of the glass is totally reflected by the top and bottom surfaces and propagates along it, Figure 25.9.

The condition for total internal reflection is:

(25.19)n2 sin α ≤ n1

where n1 = index of refraction of the medium surrounding the waveguide (in this case air, n1 ≅ 1)
n2 = index of refraction of the waveguide
α = angle of incidence at the glass–air interface

Objects in contact with the elastic membrane deform it and induce contact between the bottom of the membrane and the top surface of the waveguide, disrupting the total internal reflection. The light in the waveguide is consequently scattered at the contact location. Light escaping through the bottom surface can be detected by an array of photodiodes, by a solid-state sensor, or transported away from the transducer by fibres [3]; the detected image is stored in a computer for further analysis. A rubber membrane with a flat surface yields a high-resolution binary (contact or noncontact) image [5]; if the rubber sheet is moulded with a textured surface (Figure 25.9) the output is proportional to the area of contact and the applied forces can therefore be detected [3]. Shear forces can also be detected with special designs [35]. Sensors based on frustrated total internal reflection can be moulded into a finger shape [5] and are capable of forming very high-resolution tactile images; such sensors are commercially available, and an improved miniaturised version of a similar sensor was proposed in [34].

Other optical transducers use “occluder” devices; one of the few commercially available tactile sensors uses this kind of transducer [36]. In one of the two available designs the transducer’s surface is made of a compliant material that has, on its underside, a grid of elongated pins. When force is applied to the compliant surface the pins undergo a mechanical motion normal to the surface, blocking the light path of a photoemitter–detector pair. The amount of movement determines how much light reaches the photoreceiver: the more force applied, the less light is collected, Figure 25.10. The major problems with this device are creep, hysteresis and temperature variation, and the scheme also requires individual calibration of each photoemitter–photodetector pair.

Fibres have also been used directly as transducers. Their use rests on two properties of fibre optic cables: (1) if a fibre is subjected to a significant amount of bending, the angle of incidence at the fibre wall can be reduced sufficiently for light to leave the core [37]; and (2) if two fibres pass close to one another and both have roughened surfaces, light can pass between them, the coupling being a function of their separation [3].

An example of an optical fibre tactile sensor whose sensing mechanism is based on the increase of light attenuation due to microbends in the fibres is shown in Figure 25.11 [37]. The transducer consists of a four-layer, two-dimensional fibre optic array built using two layers of optical fibres as a corrugation structure, through which microbends are induced in two orthogonal layers of active fibres. Each active fibre uses an LED as emitter and a PIN photodiode as detector. When an object is forced into contact with the transducer a light distribution is detected at each detector, related to the applied force and the shape of the object. Using complex algorithms and active sensing — moving the object relative to the transducer — the object’s position, orientation, size and contour can be retrieved [37]; the recovery of the applied force profiles was, however, not reported.

25.3.5 Photoelastic Quang đàn hồi

An emerging technology in optical tactile sensing is the photoelastic transducer. When a light ray propagates into an optically anisotropic medium it splits into two rays that are linearly polarised at right angles to each other and propagate at different velocities. This splitting results from a physical property of crystalline material called optical birefringence, or simply birefringence. The direction in which light propagates with the higher velocity is the fast axis; the one in which it propagates more slowly is the slow axis. Some optically isotropic materials — glass, celluloid, bakelite and transparent plastics in general — become birefringent when subjected to a stress field, and the effect lasts only while the loads are applied. The phenomenon is therefore called temporary or artificial birefringence, or more commonly the photoelastic phenomenon.

Figure 25.12(a) shows a photoelastic transducer proposed in [38]. It consists of a fully supported two-layer beam with a mirrored surface sandwiched in between. Normal line forces are applied to the top surface at discrete tactels separated by equal distances s along the beam. The upper compliant layer protects the mirror; the lower one is the photoelastic layer.

Circularly polarised monochromatic light, incident along the z-axis, illuminates the bottom surface. It propagates parallel to z, passes through the photoelastic layer and reflects back from the mirror. With no force applied the returning light is circularly polarised, because unstressed photoelastic material is isotropic. With force applied, stresses are induced in the photoelastic layer and make the material birefringent, introducing a phase difference between the components of the electric field associated with the light-wave propagation. The two directions of polarisation lie in the plane perpendicular to the direction of propagation — the x–y plane. The output light is consequently elliptically polarised, creating a phase-difference distribution p between the input and the output light at each point of the x–y plane, and that distribution carries the information of the force distribution applied to the transducer.

A polariscope is a practical way to observe the spatial variation of light intensity — the fringes — produced by the induced phase-difference distribution. Polariscopes can be linear or circular depending on the required polarisation, and reflective or transparent depending on whether the photoelastic transducer reflects or transmits the light.

A circular reflective polariscope, shown in Figure 25.12(b), is used to illuminate the transducer of Figure 25.12(a). The input light is linearly polarised and directed towards the photoelastic transducer by a beam splitter; before reaching the transducer it is circularly polarised by a quarter-wave plate. The output light is elliptically polarised when a force is applied, and is directed towards a detector through the quarter-wave plate, the beam splitter and an analyser. It is finally detected by a camera linked to a frame grabber connected to a PC for further data processing. The light illuminating the camera consists of a set of fringes from which the force distribution applied to the transducer must be recovered; a technique for that recovery is described in [38], and a model of the transducer using FEA is reported in [39].

One of the earlier applications of photoelasticity to tactile sensing dates back to the development of the Utah/MIT dexterous hand [40]. Those researchers proposed the photoelastic phenomenon as a transduction method for recovering the force profile applied to the fingers of the hand, limiting their application to a single-touch transducer although they claimed that an array of such devices could be implemented; building a large array of their devices would, however, be difficult. To overcome that difficulty another group proposed a different transducer [41]. Although an analytical model was developed for the sensor, a systematic method for recovering the two-dimensional force profile from the light intensity distribution was not reported, so the sensor was used mainly for forward analysis — observing the light intensity distribution for different touching objects brought into contact with the sensor. It could eventually be used for determining some simple geometric properties of a touching object.

A tactile sensor reported in [42] is capable of detecting slippage. The output light intensity — the fringe pattern — is captured by a camera interfaced to a PC. When an object moves across the surface of the transducer the intensity distribution changes, and direct analysis of the fringes is used to detect the movement of the grasped object; a special technique was reported to optimise the comparison for detecting differences between two fringe patterns arising from slippage of the object in contact with the sensor [42]. Such analysis does not require the recovery of the applied force profile.

Photoelasticity offers several attractive properties for tactile sensors: good linearity, compatibility with vision-based sensing technologies and the high spatial resolution associated with them, which could lead to the high-resolution tactile imagers needed for object recognition and fine manipulation. Photoelastic sensors are also compatible with fibre optic technology, which allows the electronic processing devices to be located remotely and avoids interference problems.

Other technologies for tactile sensing include acoustic, magnetic and microcavity vacuum sensors [43, 44].
