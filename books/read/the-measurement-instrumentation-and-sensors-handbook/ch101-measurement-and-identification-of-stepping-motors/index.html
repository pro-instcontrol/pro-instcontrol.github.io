Measurement and Identification of Brush, Brushless, and dc Stepping Motors

Stuart Schweid · Robert Lofthus (Xerox Corporation) · John McInroy (University of Wyoming)

Chapter 101 · 5 figures, 14 equations

Many systems — robots, for instance — exist precisely because they can move themselves or other objects. A great many more, xerographic printers among them, do not want the motion for its own sake: the motion is simply a condition of doing the main job. Every one of these systems needs one or more prime movers, the parts that directly or indirectly create all of the motion in the system.

101.1 Introduction

These applications differ widely in the kind of motion they need and in how precisely it must be produced. A child's toy works well enough with imprecise motion; printing demands very precise motion control. Depending on what is required, a prime mover can be chosen from a small set of candidates — dc brush motors, dc brushless motors and hybrid stepping motors — and the system around it can be controlled either as a function of time alone (open loop) or as a function of both time and system state (closed loop).

A closed-loop system needs some way of measuring the state of the system; for motion systems that usually means one or more sensors of position and/or velocity. For many control techniques — linear controllers in particular — it also helps to have a model of the plant, which can then be used with the usual design methods to improve the dynamic response substantially. This chapter describes the main modes of operation of those prime movers and then sets out, in detail, a method for measuring the open-loop response of a candidate motion system.

101.2 Hybrid Stepping Motors

Hybrid stepping motors give fairly accurate positioning in open loop, which is how they are most often used. They succeed there because they stay within a commanded step as long as they have enough torque to resist whatever external torque the system applies. Since the motor runs open loop, it must be chosen a priori to supply that torque: the peak torque the system needs follows from its inertia, its acceleration profile and the external torque upon it. Once the torque required at every speed is known, a motor is picked from its torque–speed curve, which gives the greatest torque that can be tolerated at each speed without losing a step. A motor whose curve stays above the requirement at all speeds will do.

A hybrid stepping motor has several coils — almost always two — which turn the rotor when they are commutated in a fixed sequence. In “full stepping” the current command to each winding is constant in magnitude and varies only in polarity; which direction counts as positive is entirely arbitrary. In that mode the motor needs only four separate energisations of the winding per revolution, although clever mechanical arrangements raise the number of steps per revolution far above that — typically 200, and as many as 800.

With two windings, A and B, the four possible energisations are A+B+, A+B−, A−B− and A−B+. Command them to the motor in that order and it turns.

Chopping Current Amplifiers

The stepper needs a current of a particular amplitude in its winding, but for economic reasons most systems provide a voltage supply (Vss) rather than a current source. A current chopper is a low-cost closed-loop switching amplifier that regulates the winding current, exploiting the significant inductance of the winding. The two windings obey:

(101.1)dIA/dt = (VA − IARA − Kbω sin θ) / LA
(101.1)dIB/dt = (VB − IBRB − Kbω cos θ) / LB

where I is the winding current (A), R the winding resistance (Ω), L the winding inductance (H), V the applied voltage (V), Kb the torque constant (Nm/A), ω the motor velocity (rad/s) and θ the motor position (electrical rad). Every ts — a very short interval, of order 10 µs — the chopper applies either Vss or −Vss to the winding, according to whether the desired current IM is above or below the measured one:

(101.2)if IA < IM then VA = Vss, else VA = −Vss
(101.2)if IB < IM then VB = Vss, else VB = −Vss

The usual requirement is that Vssts/L be small against IM, so that the chopping ripple is small against the wanted current; and because the mechanical system is low-pass by nature, it barely responds to the small-amplitude, high-frequency components that remain.

Microstepping

Microstepping lets the commanded reference position fall between the full-step positions. The commanded currents for a reference position θref are:

(101.3)IA = IM cos θref
(101.3)IB = IM sin θref

where θref is the desired electrical position (four full steps carry it through one electrical revolution). For constant-velocity work:

(101.4)θref = N ωd t

with N the number of electrical revolutions per mechanical revolution — a quarter of the steps per revolution — and ωd the desired velocity (rad/s).

Closed-Loop Control

Hybrid stepping motors can also be run closed loop, but they are nonlinear devices, so the linear design toolbox does not apply directly. Hamman and Balas [1] linearise about each step with a fourth-order model, which is useful for reshaping the undamped response to a commanded change of position. Schweid [2] proposes a scheme giving good transients at constant velocity while using the full fourth-order model. Where current choppers are fitted, a second-order model suffices, and the following control law can be applied [3–5]:

(101.5)IA = IM cos θref − IC sin θref
(101.5)IB = IM sin θref + IC sin θref

These currents linearise the motor model, so ordinary techniques such as PID control can be used [6]. IM is the open-loop component that microsteps the motor; IC is a feedback term that buys an improved dynamic response.

Translator's note. The printed equation 101.5 gives the second term of IB as sin θref, identical to the first. For the pair to be orthogonal — the condition that makes it linearise the model — that term has to be cos θref. The translation keeps the print and flags the point rather than silently correcting it.

Position Measurement

Closed-loop control needs a position measurement, and that measurement must be aligned with the motor's construction rather than offset from it, because the law of equation 101.5 multiplies the command current IC by the sine and cosine of the motor position. One way is an incremental encoder with an index pulse on the motor; a calibration then fixes the position of the index relative to the shaft, and that relationship serves for all later measurements.

Another scheme estimates position from the back emf of the two coils. Some motors are built with extra sensing coils that make those signals easy to measure. From equation 101.2:

(101.6)VemfA = Kbω sin θ = VA − IARA − LA dIA/dt
(101.6)VemfB = Kbω cos θ = VB − IBRB − LB dIB/dt

and the position follows from:

(101.7)θ = −atan(VemfA / VemfB)

In most motors those two signals cannot be measured directly, but at constant velocity an excellent estimate is still available by approximating them from low-pass-filtered coil voltages. A method developed at Xerox, patented as U.S. 5378975, is described here. Taking the Laplace transform of equation 101.6:

(101.8)VemfA(s) = VA(s) − (RA + sLA)IA(s)
(101.8)VemfB(s) = VB(s) − (RB + sLB)IB(s)

Filtering both sides with low-pass filters matched to the coil dynamics gives:

(101.9)VfiltA(s) = VA(s)/(1 + sLA/RA) − RAIA(s)
(101.9)VfiltB(s) = VB(s)/(1 + sLB/RB) − RBIB(s)

Both quantities are easy to obtain: low-pass the coil voltage with a simple RC network and subtract the IR term. The subtraction is easy because the current is a known input — it is commanded — and the resistance is easily measured.

Assume matched coils, L = LA = LB and R = RA = RB, and constant velocity θ = Nωdt; the back emf voltages are then sinusoidal, and their filtered versions are:

(101.10)VfiltA(s) = |H(Nωd)| Kb sin(Nωdt + φ)
(101.10)VfiltB(s) = |H(Nωd)| Kb cos(Nωdt + φ)

where |H(Nωd)| is the magnitude response of the filter at the input frequency and φ = −atan(NωdL/R) the phase shift the filtering introduces. Dividing the two and taking the inverse tangent:

(101.11)θ = atan(−VfiltA(t) / VfiltB(t)) + atan(NωdL/R)

The full derivation is in reference [7], which also adds Kalman filtering to improve the velocity estimate obtained from position — necessary because differentiating a position measurement is very sensitive to noise.

101.3 dc Brush and Brushless Motors

The dc servomotor is the oldest and probably the most widely used servo actuator. Its name comes from the fact that a constant (dc) voltage makes it move, and over a linear range its torque is proportional to winding current. Its chief disadvantage is the brush, which does the mechanical commutation; brushes are the first part to fail, which limits the reliability of the dc brush motor against other actuators.

The dc brushless motor behaves similarly — a dc voltage turns it — but commutates very differently. Where the brushed motor switches mechanically, the brushless motor uses Hall effect sensors to switch electronically, and so achieves far greater life and reliability.

Pulse Width–Modulated Power Amplifier

Many systems need a varying voltage at the motor terminals to produce the required position or velocity profile, yet have only a single supply Vss. Rather than an analog power amplifier — expensive and inefficient — a pulse width–modulated (PWM) amplifier is used. It can deliver only Vss, −Vss (or zero in some designs); intermediate voltages come from varying the relative percentage of time each is applied at a high fixed frequency, and the mean voltage is a linear function of that duty cycle. The motor also sees the higher harmonics of the switched waveform, at integer multiples of the square-wave frequency, but most motors have at least two poles — one electrical, one mechanical — far slower than the switching frequency, and these attenuate any effect they might have on motion. Figure 101.2 shows a PWM amplifier set to deliver 12 V from a 24 V rail.

Measuring the System Dynamics

Where motion requirements are moderate or stringent, dc brush and brushless motors run closed loop, which needs a measure of position or velocity. A tachometer is commonly used for rotational speed, outputting a voltage proportional to it; where position is needed, an incremental encoder produces a digital pulse at equal position intervals — a 1000 line/revolution encoder gives 1000 rising (and 1000 falling) edges per revolution.

Once the full system, feedback sensors included, is known, a feedback control algorithm can be developed. In the design of most compensation algorithms — linear compensators such as state feedback or PID especially — one of the most important tasks is to have a good model of the system dynamics.

There are several ways to get one. The commonest is to write the state equations from the physical equations, which requires every parameter to be known; some are hard to measure and are not supplied by the manufacturer — the torsional constant of a hard rubber roll, say. Even with every parameter known it is still worth having a way of measuring the response, because that either validates the model or tells the designer it is not an accurate representation of the system.

The method below collects input/output data which can then be fitted to a frequency response or a transfer function. Signal analyzers such as the HP3562A do that curve fitting accurately; and if the data are collected discretely, packages such as MATLAB estimate a model in parametric (state space, transfer function) or nonparametric (frequency response) form. The procedure is written for motor systems but applies to most linear systems.

Take a typical closed-loop system as in Fig. 101.3. Several obstacles make its open- or closed-loop transfer function hard to obtain: some outputs cannot be measured, and some inputs cannot be controlled or modified.

To acquire a frequency response, perturb the system with a known input and measure the response; that needs a controllable input, one that can be manipulated from outside. In Fig. 101.3 the only controllable inputs are the torque on the motor shaft and the reference command.

Changing the external torque means attaching a calibrated brake or another motor. That is cumbersome, and it alters the dynamics by adding inertia to the very system being measured. In many systems the reference is a digital square wave — the feedback element is an incremental encoder and produces a similar wave — so manipulating it directly needs a voltage-to-frequency converter, whose own dynamics can corrupt the data.

A measurable output must also be available. In many servo systems the only measurable outputs are armature voltage and position/velocity. The armature voltage may be a PWM signal needing filtering to an analog voltage; an incremental encoder output is a digital square wave needing a frequency-to-voltage converter. Either converter may bring dynamics that corrupt the data.

Consider instead the system of Fig. 101.4. The summing junction added to it can be placed at any point convenient for measurement, so it is no longer limited to the unaugmented inputs or outputs of the system.

With analog controllers the junction's inputs and outputs are analog voltages, easily controlled and measured; a simple op-amp adder implements it, adding no appreciable dynamics to the original system and therefore not skewing the data.

When the response is to be measured, the time-varying contributions of the other inputs — the velocity reference and the external torque Td — must be eliminated. The velocity reference is simply held constant. The external torque is usually beyond control and will inject noise, so to secure a large signal-to-noise ratio the alternative reference z(t) should be made as large as possible.

z(t) can be a swept sine, a random input or anything else. During the stimulation, time records of the other junction nodes, y(t) and x(t), are collected. The transfer function between Y and X is:

(101.12)Y(s)/X(s) = −Gc(s) Kamp(s) Gm(s)
(101.12)−Y(s)/X(s) = Gc(s) Kamp(s) Gm(s)

The second term is, by definition, the open-loop transfer function. As before, MATLAB or a signal analyzer fits it parametrically or nonparametrically from the record. Note that the system must be running closed loop while the data are collected, with Gc a proportional controller at a value that keeps the whole system stable — and, for good data, as large as that allows.

A digital or microprocessor-controlled system is handled similarly, with small modifications. In Fig. 101.5 a microprocessor is the controller, so the summing junction is just an add instruction inside it, and the problem becomes getting data in and out. Getting data in needs an ADC, represented by the block Af, which also carries any dynamics inherent in the converter — gain especially. The microprocessor output may be a digital PWM signal that needs low-pass filtering to give the analyzer an analog voltage; that filter is Pf, typically a simple RC network whose cutoff is far below the PWM frequency.

With ωref static and Td minimal, the transfer function between Za(s) and Xa(s) in Fig. 101.5 is:

(101.13)Za(s)/Xa(s) = (1 + Gc(s) SH(s) Kamp(s) Gm(s)) / (Af(s) SH(s) Pf(s)) ≡ O(s)

When Gc = 0, Za(s)/Xa(s) = 1/(Af(s)SH(s)Pf(s)) ≡ C(s), and (O(s) − C(s))/C(s) = Gc(s)SH(s)Kamp(s)Gm(s) — the open-loop transfer function.

A more direct route opens up if a software system-identification package such as MATLAB is used instead of a logic analyzer. The stimulus z can be stored as a lookup table in microprocessor memory or generated in real time by any random-number or other algorithm; y(k) and x(k) at the internal junction can be captured by a logic analyzer or emulator if they are written to an external bus every sample period. Neither a DAC nor an ADC is then needed, and no combination of two transfer functions is required to reach the open-loop one. The transfer function between Y(z) and X(z) is:

(101.14)Y(z)/X(z) = −Gc(z) SH(z) Kamp(z) Gm(z)
(101.14)−Y(z)/X(z) = Gc(z) SH(z) Kamp(z) Gm(z)

Again this is the open-loop transfer function, obtained with minimal extra software and a logic analyzer, and again the input/output set can be handed to any identification algorithm.
