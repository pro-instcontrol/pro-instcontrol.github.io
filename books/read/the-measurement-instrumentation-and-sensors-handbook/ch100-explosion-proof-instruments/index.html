Explosion-Proof Instruments

Sam S. Khalilieh, P.E. · Earth Tech

Chapter 100 · 7 figures, 3 tables

Wherever a hazardous atmosphere can exist, electricity has to be a primary concern of the engineer and of the system designer. Such atmospheres are not confined to the familiar surroundings of industrial, chemical and environmental facilities; they occur in many less obvious places — where dust is present, where gas can accumulate, where reactions form combustible gases. Minimising the risk in those places means designing purpose-built, hazard-reducing electrical systems. Most electrical equipment is built to standards aimed at reducing fires and casualties, and the majority of incidents that do occur can be traced to poor or defective installation, misuse of approved equipment, deterioration, or accidental application. Combined with an explosive atmosphere, any of those can produce extremely dangerous conditions. Designing an electrical system for a hazardous location calls for careful planning, research, engineering and some ingenuity in applying the right protection techniques, so that better applications and classifications — and lower hazard — result.

100.1 Introduction

The safety of people and equipment in a hazardous area should never be taken for granted. In 1913 a massive methane explosion in a coal mine in Glamorganshire, South Wales, killed 439 miners. After months of study, a group of experts and scientists concluded that the explosion had been caused by a small amount of electrical energy stored in the circuit; that small energy, together with an explosive gas–air mixture and the absence of proper protection, proved fatal.

Understanding the dangers of electrical equipment in hazardous areas starts with the basics. Chemically, oxidation, combustion and explosion are all exothermic reactions that differ only in the speed at which heat is released. For any of them to occur, three components must be present at once, in the right concentrations: (1) fuel, whether liquid, gas or solid; (2) enough oxygen, i.e. air; and (3) an ignition source, electrical or thermal.

Potentially hazardous ignition sources include (1) hot surfaces — motor windings, heat-trace cable, luminaires; (2) electric sparks and arcs, as circuits are made and broken or as short circuits occur; (3) mechanical sparks from friction and grinding; (4) electrostatic discharge, a separation process in which at least one chargeable substance is present; and (5) radiation, compression and shock waves.

Working with electrical equipment in hazardous locations requires familiarity with the following terms.

1. Flash point — the lowest temperature at normal atmospheric pressure at which a combustible or flammable material releases enough vapour to be ignited by an energy source. By flash point (FP), flammable liquids fall into four hazard classes:

  1. AI (FP < 21 °C),
  2. AII (21 °C < FP < 55 °C),
  3. AIII (55 °C < FP < 100 °C), and
  4. B (FP < 21 °C when dissolved in water at 15 °C).

2. Ignition temperature — the lowest temperature at normal operating pressure at which a dangerous mixture ignites independently of the heating element or the heated element.

3. Flammable limits — the upper explosive limit (UEL) is the highest vapour-to-air concentration ratio above which flame does not propagate when an ignition source is applied; such a mixture is “too rich” to burn. The lower explosive limit (LEL) is the lowest ratio below which flame does not propagate; such a mixture is “too lean” to explode. The LEL deserves particular attention because it gives the minimum quantity of gas needed to create a hazardous mixture. Flammable limits are normally quoted in percent by volume, % vol. A mixture midway between the UEL and the LEL explodes far more violently than one nearer either limit.

4. Maximum surface temperature — the highest temperature a piece of electrical equipment produces under normal or fault conditions. It must stay below the lowest ignition temperature of the potentially explosive atmosphere around it. Equipment for hazardous locations must be marked with its class, group and maximum surface temperature (or range), referred to a 40 °C (104 °F) ambient. Table 100.1 shows that apparatus of a given T class may be used wherever the gases present have an ignition temperature above that class. For extra safety the maximum surface temperature is best kept below 80% of the lowest ignition temperature of the surrounding gas. Do not confuse maximum working (operating) temperature with maximum surface temperature, which is measured under the worst conditions of the apparatus: equipment rated for a 70 °C ambient must show a temperature rise no greater than a 10 °C safety margin to be classed T6, or 5 °C for T3, T4 and T5 (Table 100.1).

5. Vapour density — the weight of a volume of pure vapour compared with the weight of an equal volume of dry air at the same normal pressure and temperature; numerically the ratio of the molecular weight of the gas to the average molecular weight of air (28.96). Methane (CH4), molecular weight 16 and vapour density 0.6, tends to rise; acetone (C3H6O), molecular weight 58 and vapour density 2, tends to settle near ground level.

In the United States the National Electrical Code (NEC) defines a hazardous area as “an area where a potential hazard may exist under normal or abnormal conditions because of the presence of flammable, combustible or ignitable materials” [1]. That general description is broken down into classes, divisions and groups so that the extent of the hazard can be assessed properly and safe electrical systems designed and specified.

Classification matters for economic as well as safety reasons. Proper application, good engineering and experience can confine the most volatile areas (Class I, Division 1) to reasonable distances around potential leaks and ignition sources. Inside Class I, Division 1 the equipment and installation cost becomes a burden, because the equipment is considerably more expensive and must pass stringent tests for correct, safe operation under normal and abnormal conditions. NFPA 497 A & B [2] and ANSI/API RP 500 [3] are excellent sources for fixing the boundaries of a hazardous area.

100.2 Fundamentals of Explosion Protection

Classifying a hazardous area is usually done by highly qualified people — chemical engineers, process engineers and safety officers — whose primary objective is to establish where a potentially hazardous atmosphere exists, under what conditions, and for how long. Careful study and design of the electrical installation is crucial to safe operation and to preventing the accidental ignition of flammable materials.

The NEC, adopted by many states, agencies and companies as the basis for inspection, sets out the requirements and procedures for electrical installations in hazardous areas. Articles 500–504 cover equipment and wiring at all voltages where fire or explosion hazards may exist because of flammable gases or vapours, flammable liquids, combustible dust, or ignitable fibres and flyings.

100.3 Enclosure Types and Requirements

Choosing the right enclosure matters for two reasons:

  1. Protecting personnel against accidental contact with enclosed electrical equipment.
  2. Protecting the internal equipment against outside harm.

Enclosures carry a type number indicating the degree of protection and the conditions for which they are suitable. Some applications need both, and the enclosure is then designated by a two-part number with the smaller first, e.g. 7/9. The types below, with their enclosed equipment, have been evaluated to Underwriters Laboratories UL 698, Industrial Control Equipment for Use in Hazardous Locations, and are marked with the class and group letters.

Type 7 — nonventilated, for indoor use, classified for Class I, Groups A, B, C and D as defined in Table 100.2; the letters A to D sometimes appear as a suffix. Under UL 698 a Type 7 enclosure must withstand the internal explosion pressure of specific gases and prevent that explosion from igniting a hazardous mixture outside the enclosure (explosion test). Sheet-steel Type 7 enclosures must additionally withstand twice the internal explosion pressure for 1 min without permanent deformation, and three times the pressure without rupture; cast-iron enclosures must withstand four times the pressure without rupture or deformation. The test may be waived where calculation shows a safety factor of five to one for cast metal, or four to one for fabricated steel. Enclosed heat-generating devices are designed so that external surfaces cannot reach temperatures able to ignite an explosive vapour–air mixture outside the enclosure (temperature test).

Type 8 — nonventilated, for indoor use, for Class I, Groups A, B, C and D as in Table 100.2, with the letters A to D as a suffix. Under UL 698 the oil-immersed equipment must operate at rated voltage and under the most severe current conditions in the presence of flammable gas–air mixtures without igniting them.

Type 9 — nonventilated, for indoor use, classified for Class II, Groups E, F and G as in Table 100.2, with E, F or G as a suffix. Under UL 698 the enclosure and its equipment are evaluated to the edition of UL 698 in force at the time of manufacture. That evaluation reviews the dimensional requirements for shaft openings and joints, the gasket material, and the temperature rise under a blanket of dust. The device runs at full rated load until equilibrium temperatures are reached, then cools to ambient over at least 30 h while continuously subjected to circulating dust of specified properties; no dust may enter the enclosure (dust penetration test). Type 9 enclosures must also pass the “temperature test with dust blanket”, like the temperature-rise test except that the circulating dust is not aimed directly at the device. Dust in contact with the enclosure must not ignite or discolour from heat, and the exterior surface temperature, based on 40 °C (104 °F), must not exceed the specified values under normal or abnormal conditions. Where gasketed enclosures are used the gaskets must be noncombustible, nondeteriorating and vermin-proof, and mechanically attached. Type 9 ventilated enclosures are as the nonventilated ones except that ventilation is forced air from outside the hazardous area, producing positive pressure inside the enclosure; the enclosure must still meet the temperature design test.

Type 10 — nonventilated, designed to meet the requirements of the U.S. Bureau of Mines for atmospheres containing mixtures of methane and air, with or without coal dust.

Note that enclosures for hazardous applications are designed for specific applications and must be installed and maintained as the manufacturer recommends: any misapplication or alteration may jeopardise the integrity of the enclosure and eventually cause catastrophic failure of the system. Every enclosure should be solidly grounded and properly labelled with a warning that reminds the operator to de-energise the incoming supply before servicing.

100.4 Protection Methodologies

Choosing a protection technique to suit each application looks complicated, because safety, reliability, cost and maintenance all have to be weighed. Over the years only a few methodologies have been used for hazardous areas. They differ in application and in principle, but they share one aim: to eliminate one or more of the components that combustion needs. The three most widely used are

  1. intrinsic safety,
  2. explosion-proof construction,
  3. purging and pressurization.

Intrinsic Safety

Intrinsic safety (IS) is, simply stated, about preventing explosions. It rests on limiting the thermal and electrical energy in the hazardous area to levels that cannot ignite a specific hazardous mixture at its most ignitable concentration. IS is concerned with the minimum ignition temperature and the minimum ignition energy needed to ignite a given group. An IS circuit supplies a low energy level (about 1 W) and is used only to power instruments with a low energy demand. It comprises an intrinsically safe apparatus (the field device), an associated apparatus, and the interconnecting wiring.

Design begins with the field device, because that determines which associated apparatus can be used so that the circuit works properly under normal conditions yet stays safe under fault. Field devices may be simple — resistance temperature devices (RTDs), thermocouples, mechanical switches, proximity switches, light emitting diodes — or nonsimple: transmitters, solenoid valves, relays. A field device counts as a “simple device” if its energy-storing or generating values do not exceed 1.2 V, 0.1 A, 25 mW (or 20 mJ) in an intrinsically safe system under normal or abnormal conditions.

A simple device may be connected to an intrinsically safe circuit without further certification or approval. That it cannot store or generate high energy does not mean it may be installed in a hazardous area unmodified: it must always be used with an associated apparatus to limit the energy in the hazardous area, since a fault outside that area can leak enough energy in. A nonsimple device — a relay or a transmitter — can generate and store energy above those values and needs evaluation and approval under the entity concept (described below) before it may be used with an IS circuit. Under that concept such devices carry the entity parameters Vmax (maximum voltage allowed), Imax (maximum current allowed), Ci (internal capacitance) and Li (internal inductance). Under fault the voltage and current must stay below the apparatus's Vmax and Imax to prevent excess heat or sparking, which can be disastrous in a hazardous area. Ci and Li express the device's ability to store energy as internal capacitance and inductance, and must be less than the Ca and La of the associated apparatus (Table 100.3).

An associated apparatus (Figure 100.1), also called a safety barrier, is the energy-limiting device that protects a field device in the hazardous area from excessive voltage or current. It is normally installed in a dust- and moisture-free enclosure (NEMA 4) in the nonhazardous area, as close as possible to the boundary so that the capacitance of the cable is minimised. If it must sit in the hazardous area, it goes in an explosion-proof enclosure (e.g. NEMA 7D).

Figure 100.1 shows the three major components of a Zener safety barrier (other kinds exist — isolation and repeater barriers among them):

  1. The resistor, which limits current to a specific value, the short-circuit current (Isc).
  2. The fuse, which interrupts the circuit should a diode fail — it blows if the diode conducts.
  3. The Zener diode, which limits voltage to a specific value, the open-circuit voltage (Voc).

Zener diodes are unusual in conducting under reverse bias. Applied in the reverse direction, a Zener passes a small leakage current, which stays small until the bias exceeds the Zener breakdown voltage. Beyond breakdown the inherently high resistance collapses to a very low value and the current rises abruptly; the diode becomes a conductor and diverts the excess voltage to ground. If the current continues above the fuse rating the fuse opens and the circuit is interrupted completely. Most barriers use at least two diodes in parallel so that one failure still leaves protection (redundant safety).

In 1988 ANSI/UL 913 [5] permitted barriers with replaceable fuses on these terms: “if it is accessible for replacement, the fuse on a fuse-protected shunt-diode barrier shall not be replaceable by one of higher rating.” The fuses sit in tamper-proof assemblies to prevent confusion or misapplication, and the diodes have power ratings that must not be exceeded. Zener diodes and fuses are governed by a specific set of parameters that let the fuse operate at one-third the power rating of the diode and avoid irreversible damage to it. The diode's power rating follows from

Zw = 1.5 × Voc × 2 × If

where Zw is the minimum power rating of the Zener diode, Voc the maximum Zener open-circuit voltage, and If the fuse current rating.

Selecting the best barrier for an application depends on the field device and requires analysis to ensure the IS circuit behaves correctly under normal and abnormal conditions. Three characteristics matter most: (1) internal resistance, (2) rated voltage and (3) circuit polarity. Whichever barrier is chosen, its internal resistance Ri limits the short-circuit current under fault, and the current through Ri creates a voltage drop across the barrier that must be accounted for (V = IR). The rated voltage of the barrier must equal, or reasonably exceed, the supply voltage — “reasonably”, because excessive supply voltage can make the diode conduct, driving a large current through the fuse and blowing it. A regulated supply greatly reduces the problems of excessive supply voltage. Completing the analysis means establishing the polarity: ac barriers can be connected to a positive or a negative supply, dc barriers are rated for one or the other.

Making Field Devices Intrinsically Safe

RTDs and thermocouples become intrinsically safe through isolated temperature converters (ITCs), which turn the low dc signal of the field device into a proportional 4–20 mA signal. These converters need no ground connection for safe, correct operation of the IS circuit. Because they store energy, transmitters are nonsimple devices and must be approved as intrinsically safe; where they carry third-party approval their entity parameters still have to be examined carefully. Transmitters (4–20 mA) convert physical measurements in the hazardous area — pressure, flow — into electrical signals for a controller in a safe area. Depending on conditions, a 4–20 mA signal can be made intrinsically safe with a repeater barrier, which duplicates the output signal to match the input. Repeaters may be grounded or ungrounded; ungrounded repeaters are known as transformer-isolated barriers, because the incoming voltage or signal is fully isolated from the outgoing one by a transformer. Digital inputs — mechanical and proximity switches, both simple devices — become intrinsically safe through a switch amplifier, which is simply a relay or an optocoupler (a high-speed relay using optical isolation between input and output) that transfers a discrete on/off signal from the hazardous to the safe area.

Grounded safety barriers are passive devices designed specifically to stop excess energy in a nonhazardous area from reaching a hazardous one. They suit most field devices, but they only work properly with a solid, low-impedance (<1 Ω) connection to ground, which prevents the ground loops and induced voltages that would otherwise hinder the system.

Ignition Curves

Every circuit has electrical characteristics that fall into three categories — resistance, inductance and capacitance — and to some degree every circuit has all three. One may be so small that its effect is negligible beside the others, which is why we speak of resistive, inductive and capacitive circuits. Since IS rests on the principle that a large current can cause an explosion in a hazardous area and the lack of one cannot, the ranges of current that are safe and those that are dangerous have to be identified.

What is a dangerous amount of electrical energy? The answer lies in the ignition curves, published in most IS standards, ANSI/UL 913 [5] among them. Three of the most referenced are shown in Figure 100.2. They give the energy needed to ignite various hazardous atmospheric mixtures at their most easily ignitable concentration — the concentration found by taking the volume percentage between the upper and lower explosive limits of that mixture. In all three, energy levels (voltage and current) below the curve of a group are insufficient to ignite that group.

Because the ignition temperature is directly related to the voltage and current consumed, both Voc and Isc of the barrier must be less than Vmax and Imax. Designing an IS system also means accounting for the cable resistance R (Ω/m), inductance L (µH/m) and capacitance C (pF/m), which are distributed along its length. Capacitance and inductance can be had from the cable manufacturer's literature; failing that, default values may be used from NFPA 493/78 (A-4-2): Cc = 200 pF/m (60 pF/ft) and Lc = 0.66 µH/m (0.2 µH/ft). Determining the maximum wiring distance that still ensures correct operation requires computing the capacitance and inductance.

One common approach uses “lumped parameters”, comparing and matching the voltages and currents of the intrinsically safe apparatus and of the associated apparatus according to Equations (100.1) and (100.2). Any deviation from either compromises the integrity of the system and introduces hazardous conditions. The reactive parts of the system must also be verified, to show that the Ca and La of the associated apparatus are not exceeded by the field device plus the field wiring, as in Equations (100.3) and (100.4). Simple and effective as it is, this method tends to exaggerate the capacitance and inductance of the wiring, which can be limiting in some applications.

Another method exploits the relation between cable resistance and inductance. It applies where the L/R ratio of the associated apparatus is higher than the calculated L/R ratio of the cable; under those conditions the smaller Da (maximum allowed distance) may be ignored and the cable extended to the larger Da. This is the more flexible method where cable length is the issue. Figure 100.3 and the example below illustrate both.

Certification and Approval

Approval and certification help, but careful planning, design and engineering are still necessary. IS standards, procedures and tests are recognised worldwide. Testing authorities include Underwriters Laboratories (UL) and Factory Mutual Research Corp. (FM) in the U.S., the Canadian Standards Association (CSA) in Canada, and the Physikalisch-Technische Bundesanstalt (PTB) in Europe. Intrinsically safe products suit all the Classes, Divisions and Groups of Table 100.2 — but the product must be rated and classified for each specific application. In the U.S. FM adopted two methods of testing and approving equipment for hazardous areas:

  1. Loop (system) approval: an intrinsically safe apparatus is evaluated together with a specific associated apparatus and approved for installation in that combination. Any change to the circuit requires re-evaluation and re-certification (Figure 100.4).
  2. Entity approval: the intrinsically safe apparatus and the associated apparatus are evaluated separately and each is given its own electrical entity parameters (Figure 100.5). Correct application matches the entity parameters as in Table 100.3. Comparing the entity values of an intrinsically safe apparatus with those of an associated apparatus is the crucial step in judging the safety of a circuit. Most safety barriers are entity approved for all hazardous locations.

Since most field devices can store energy, they must carry either loop approval or entity approval for an intrinsically safe system to be correctly built and to operate.

IS engineers commonly advocate intrinsically safe equipment for five reasons:

  1. Safety. No explosion can occur in an intrinsically safe system under any operating condition. IS equipment runs at lower power levels and prevents shocks, excess thermal energy and arcing, any of which may be a hazard in other systems and other circumstances.
  2. Reliability. Components and assemblies of IS circuits are tested for reliability before being labelled and certified, and most IS equipment is designed with special circuitry for surge suppression and for suppressing spikes and transients.
  3. Ease of handling and installation. IS systems tend to be small and need none of the expensive, bulky accessories — enclosures, seals, rigid metallic conduit — that raise the initial investment.
  4. Economy. In some jurisdictions, facilities with hazardous conditions must carry special liability insurance. With a proper IS installation the probability of explosion is 10−18 [8], or nearly nonexistent, and premiums tend to be lower.
  5. Maintenance. Equipment can be calibrated and maintained without disconnecting power, so downtime is shorter.

Wiring an intrinsically safe system resembles any other installation, but identification and separation must follow strict rules if the system is to work. All IS components — terminal blocks, conductors, apparatus — must be explicitly marked and labelled. The conventional colour for IS equipment is blue. In open wiring, IS conductors must be physically separated from non-IS conductors by at least 50 mm (2 in.) so that induced voltage cannot defeat the purpose of IS. Where IS conductors occupy a raceway, the raceway should be labelled “Intrinsically Safe Circuits”, and IS conductors should never be placed with non-IS ones. Where a cable tray is used, a grounded sheet-metal partition is an acceptable means of separation. Where IS and non-IS conductors share an enclosure, a 50 mm (2 in.) separation must be maintained, and a grounded metal partition must be in place to prevent contact between conductors that may come loose. Deterioration of the insulation of IS conductors belonging to different circuits in the same raceway or enclosure can be detrimental to the system. IS conductors must have an insulation grade able to withstand an ac test voltage of 550 V root-mean-square (rms), or twice the operating voltage of the IS circuit. Non-IS conductors in the same enclosure must withstand an ac test voltage of 2U + 1000 V, with a minimum of 1500 V rms, where U is the sum of the rms voltages of the IS conductors. A widely used and strongly recommended practice is separate compartments for IS and non-IS conductors.

Besides physical separation, sealing the conduits and raceways that carry IS conductors is essential to stop gases, vapours and dusts passing from hazardous to nonhazardous areas. Under the NEC, seal-offs need not be explosion-proof; where an associated apparatus is installed in an explosion-proof enclosure inside a hazardous area, they must be. Code does not require it, but fitting explosion-proof seal-offs to conduits carrying IS conductors is good engineering practice, as Figure 100.6 shows.

Explosion-Proof Fundamentals

Explosion-proof design is a mechanical concept: it leans heavily on the mechanical construction of the enclosure and on the narrow tolerances between its joints, threads and flanges to contain, cool and vent any internal explosion safely. By definition, explosion-proof enclosures must prevent the ignition of explosive gases or vapours that may surround them (Type 7 and Type 10 only). In Class I, Divisions 1 and 2, arcing devices — switches, contactors, motor starters — must be enclosed in an explosion-proof enclosure rated for that area. Contrary to popular belief, explosion-proof enclosures are not, and should not be, vapour-tight: put succinctly, an explosion inside must be prevented from starting a larger one outside. Unlike IS, explosion-proof enclosures address the maximum internal pressure (see NEMA Type 7). Figure 100.7 shows the rugged construction of a standard explosion-proof panelboard.

Besides durability and strength, explosion-proof enclosures must be “flame-tight”: joints and flanges are held within narrow tolerances so that the hot gases of an internal explosion are cooled as they escape. Any gas released into the hazardous atmosphere outside is then too cool to ignite it.

Explosion-proof enclosures tend to be bulky — which makes them easy to identify — and heavy, requiring conduit seals and careful handling. Unlike IS equipment, they operate at normal power levels, which some circuits and equipment demand. With the right equipment, installation and maintenance, they can distribute high voltage and power into hazardous areas safely and effectively.

Where ignitable amounts of dust are present, enclosures housing electrical equipment must be dust-ignition-proof: they must keep combustible dust out while preventing arcs, sparks or internally generated heat from igniting the dust around the exterior. They must also dissipate internal heat efficiently, since many dusts ignite at relatively low temperatures. Unlike Class I, Division 1 explosion-proof enclosures (Type 7), Class II, Division 1 dust-ignition-proof enclosures (Type 9) are designed to prevent an explosion; they therefore need not be as strong, or have walls as thick, because there will be no internal explosion.

Purging and Pressurization

This methodology allows electrical equipment to operate where hazardous conditions exist and no other method applies, because of imperative high-energy demands and sheer physical size — large motors and switchgear units, for instance, that are not commercially available for Class I, Groups A and B. It is also used where control panels housing instruments and electrical equipment must sit in a hazardous area. Purging and pressurization relies on clean air or inert gas (nitrogen, say) supplied continuously to the enclosure at enough flow to keep the equipment adequately cooled and to maintain enough internal pressure to keep combustible atmospheres out. The enclosures are not explosion-proof, but they must be relatively vapour-tight and strong enough to perform safely and satisfactorily. The system consists of:

  1. Clean air (or inert gas) supply. Careful study and analysis matter here, because the air supplied must be reasonably free of contaminants. Finding a safe location for the intake takes skill and ingenuity; consulting an HVAC specialist is recommended. Vapour density, location, wind pattern and the surrounding environment all deserve consideration. Where compressors and blowers supply the compressed air, the size and location of the compressor or blower must be chosen to meet the airflow requirement without compromising the main objective of safety and reliability.
  2. Purging. A pressurized enclosure that has been out of service for a time tends to collect a combustible mixture. Before energising, inert gas and positive pressure must supply enough initial clean air volume to minimise the concentration of any combustible mixture present. For typical applications a flow of four times the internal volume of the enclosure usually suffices; for unusual ones the volume must be calculated carefully to ensure the purge succeeds.
  3. Pressurization. This uses the pressure differential between outside and inside to keep flammable material out, by holding a higher pressure inside. For safe operation the protected enclosure must be kept at a positive pressure of at least 25 Pa (0.1 in. water) [2] above the surrounding atmosphere throughout the operation of the protected equipment.
  4. Signals and alarms. Warnings and alarms are essential when positive pressure is lost. Three types of pressurization and alarm may be used, depending on the nature of the enclosure controls and the degree of hazard outside. Door interlocks are also required, to prevent the protected enclosure being opened while the circuit is energised.

According to NFPA 496 there are three types of pressurization:

  1. Type X — reduces the classification inside the protected enclosure from Division 1 to nonclassified. This usually involves a source of ignition in a tight enclosure located in a potentially hazardous atmosphere. Type X requires a disconnecting means (a flow or pressure switch) that de-energises the protected enclosure completely and automatically as soon as the protective gas supply fails, whether pressure or flow is lost. The disconnecting means must be explosion-proof or intrinsically safe, since it usually sits in the hazardous area.
  2. Type Y — reduces the classification inside the enclosure from Division 1 to Division 2. The protected enclosure houses equipment rated for Division 2 and provides no source of ignition, so no immediate hazard is created. Type Y requires a visual or audible alarm on system failure. A word of caution: safeguards must ensure that any malfunction does not raise the external surface temperature of the enclosure above 80% of the ignition temperature of the combustible mixture present.
  3. Type Z — reduces the classification inside the enclosure from Division 2 to nonclassified. Type Z requires a visual or audible alarm if a failure to maintain positive pressure or flow inside the protected enclosure is detected.

Recognising and understanding the dangers of using electricity in hazardous areas is a crucial part of choosing the best protection. For most applications, where energy demands differ, the technique will likely combine several methodologies and specialised technologies. Analysis and investigation done properly look time-consuming and expensive, but for those willing to follow the established guidelines and sound engineering practice, the process helps secure the highest level of compliance while yielding substantial savings and preventing property damage and injury.

Defining Terms

Inductance: the ability of an electrical apparatus to store an electric charge (energy). An inductor releases that energy when the circuit is opened (broken).

Oxidation: the process in which negatively charged ions (anions) lose electrons at the anode during an electrochemical process; to become neutral, anions must lose electrons.

Resistance temperature device (RTD): a device that measures temperature from the change in resistance.

Zener diode: a nonlinear solid-state device that does not conduct in reverse bias until a critical voltage is reached, after which it conducts in reverse bias without damage. Zener diodes have an almost constant voltage characteristic in the reverse-bias (normal) region.
