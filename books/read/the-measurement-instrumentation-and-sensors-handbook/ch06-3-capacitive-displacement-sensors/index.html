Capacitive Sensors — Displacement

Halit Eren & Wei Ling Kong

Section 6.3 · 9 figures · about 4,596 words

Section 6.3 is the capacitive counterpart to the resistive and inductive families of 6.1 and 6.2. It covers three sensing geometries — variable gap, variable area, variable dielectric — plus the differential three-terminal arrangement that removes most of the gap sensor’s hyperbolic nonlinearity, and then a long tail of applications that reuse the same physics: pressure, acceleration, level, humidity, grain moisture.

What makes the section worth reading closely is that the same equation, C = εA/d, gives a linear sensor or a hopeless one depending entirely on which of the three quantities moves. Move the area and you get linearity for free. Move the gap and you get a hyperbola that has to be linearised electronically — and every one of the circuits in the last part of the section exists to do exactly that.

The differential arrangement deserves the emphasis the section gives it. By taking the difference of two gaps that move in opposition, the 1/(d − δd) and 1/(d + δd) terms expand into a ratio in which the leading nonlinearity cancels: δC/C = δd/d exactly to first order. The range is twice the nominal separation, the output is zero at the centre, and the sign of the displacement appears as a 180° phase reversal — which is why a phase-sensitive demodulator, not a rectifier, is the right detector.

The numbers worth remembering Những con số cần nhớ

  • Smart capacitive position sensor (Fig. 6.27): 1 µm accuracy over a 1 mm range, total measuring time better than 0.1 s, capacitance range 1 pF down to 50 fF. The guard ring has to keep the relative deviation below 100 ppm, which is what the two exponential criteria (6.31) and (6.32) enforce.
  • Capacitive accelerometers: 0–1000 Hz bandwidth, ±2 g to ±500 g dynamic range for a typical lightweight part.
  • Tantalum humidity sensor: 375 pF at 0 % RH, 1.7 pF per % RH, error under 2 % from nonlinearity and 1 % from hysteresis over 0–100 % RH.
  • Grain moisture: chamber roughly 150 mm deep by 100 mm diameter, operated 0–50 °C with tight temperature compensation, calibrated per material, useful over 0–40 % moisture.
  • Gasoline level: εr ≈ 2 for the hydrocarbons in gasoline against ≈ 1 for air, which is what makes a linear capacitance-versus-level reading possible in an aircraft tank.

Why the signal conditioning is the hard part Vì sao phần xử lý tín hiệu mới là chỗ khó

A capacitive sensor is mechanically trivial — two plates and a gap — and electrically awkward. The capacitances are picofarads or less, comparable with cable capacitance; the sensor is not a pure capacitance but carries a loss resistance through the dielectric; and at the low end the impedance is so high that stray pickup dominates. The section’s four techniques are four different answers to that:

  • The charge amplifier holds the inverting input at virtual ground, so cable capacitance is bootstrapped out and the output becomes proportional to Cx rather than to the voltage across it.
  • Amplitude demodulation recovers the mean gap from a modulated carrier, then a low-pass filter recovers the vibration about that mean.
  • Square-wave linearisation drives two differential capacitors with antiphase square waves and averages the difference, which converts the ratio (C₁ − C₂)/(C₁ + C₂) directly into a signed dc level.
  • Feedback linearisation servos the excitation amplitude until the capacitor current is constant, making the voltage amplitude linear in x.

Only the first of these four is a true lineariser of the gap sensor; the others linearise something else and are worth keeping straight. The charge amplifier makes e₀ proportional to Cx. The square-wave circuit makes e₀ proportional to the normalised difference (C₁ − C₂)/(C₁ + C₂), which is the quantity the differential geometry already made linear in δd. The feedback loop makes e₀ proportional to x by holding the current constant, so the nonlinearity is absorbed into the excitation amplitude instead of the output.

Translator’s note. The source’s claim that equation 6.49 makes the output “directly proportional to the plate separation x” holds only for the variable-area geometry, where Cx ∝ x. For the variable-gap sensor the same equation gives e₀ ∝ 1/x, still hyperbolic. The genuine contribution of the charge amplifier is linearity in Cx, not in x. The heading “Pulse Width Modulation” in the source also precedes a passage on amplitude modulation; the real PWM material is under “Square wave linearization”.

Where the physics is reused Vật lý ấy được dùng lại ở đâu

Pressure, acceleration, level and humidity are all the same capacitor with a different thing moving. In the pressure sensor the diaphragm is one plate and its centre deflection goes as (a² − r²)², so the mean deflection — and hence ∆C/C — is one third of the centre value. In the accelerometer the proof mass is the plate and the Coulomb force F = −½V²dC/dx is what pulls it; with a constant supply the force is independent of x when C is linear in x, which is why the electrode shape is chosen to make it so. In the level sensor the dielectric boundary moves; in the humidity sensor the dielectric absorbs water and changes its permittivity.

The humidity variants are a study in materials rather than circuits. Aluminium oxide is an ionic dielectric: sensitive, cheap, and vulnerable to ammonia, sulphur trioxide and chlorine. Porous silicon is inert and can be anodised cheaply, and its pore size can be tuned so contaminants are too large to enter. Tantalum with a cracked chromium overlayer gets the water in through the cracks. All three rely on the same trick — pores sized for a water molecule — and all three fail the same way when dust blocks the pores, which is why sintered metal or plastic hoods appear in the practical advice.
