Strain Measurement

Christopher S. Lynch · Georgia Institute of Technology

Chapter 22 · 14 figures, 2 tables, 40 equations, 29 references

Stress and strain describe loads on and deformations of solid materials. Homogeneous means the properties are the same everywhere; isotropic means they are independent of direction — an annealed steel bar is both, a human femur is neither. For a long bar under a tensile load, stress is force divided by cross-sectional area and strain is length change divided by original length; the bar elongates longitudinally (ε_L) and contracts transversely (ε_t). In the linear range the ratio of stress to strain is the Young's modulus E and the negative ratio of transverse to longitudinal strain is the Poisson's ratio ν. A tangential force per unit area is the shear stress τ, whose distortion is the shear strain γ; their ratio is the shear modulus G. Temperature change gives thermal strain, whose ratio to temperature change is the expansion coefficient α.

Principal directions undergo elongation but no shear; at least two always exist on any surface. Where they are unknown — near a hole or notch, in an anisotropic specimen, or in a complicated structure — extra gages are needed. Elastic and thermal properties combine into Hooke's law, Equations 22.1–22.6.

Sensor families are piezoresistive (foil, wire and semiconductor), piezoelectric (PVDF and quartz), fiber optic, birefringent film and Moiré grids; each needs its own signal conditioning. Selection depends on geometry, temperature, strain rate, frequency, magnitude, cost, complexity, accuracy, spatial and time resolution, transverse and temperature sensitivity, and conditioning complexity. The data in Table 22.1 are illustrative only and are by no means complete. The sensor descriptions name only the sensor type, not the many different sizes and shapes available. Longitudinal strain sensitivity is given as the sensor output per unit longitudinal strain along the sensor axis. If the signal conditioning is included, every sensitivity can be expressed as volts per unit strain [3, 4], but that value depends on the gain and quality of the conditioner. Temperature sensitivity is given as the output change caused by a temperature change. In many cases a higher strain resolution can be reached, but resolving smaller strains is harder and may need vibration and thermal isolation. With the Moiré technique the strain resolution is a function of the observed gauge length: the technique resolves 100 nm of displacement (1/4 fringe order), and dividing that by the observed length gives the strain resolution. Spatial resolution corresponds to the gage length for most sensor types. 22.2 Principles of Operation of Strain Sensors Nguyên lý hoạt động

Piezoresistive foil gages (Figure 22.5) are a polyimide substrate, a constantan foil grid, lead wires and often a polyimide encapsulation. The grid runs in one direction so that strain stretches the legs lengthwise; transverse strain merely separates them, so the gage is sensitive only along its length, with almost no shear sensitivity. The resistance change follows ΔR/R = Gε (Eq. 22.7), and with temperature and transverse terms, Eq. 22.8. Self-temperature compensation (STC) cancels thermal output by choosing a material whose thermal response is offset by the specimen's thermal expansion; manufacturers quote STC numbers matched to common specimen materials.

For one rectangular leg (Figure 22.6) R = ρL/A (Eq. 22.9). A first-order Taylor expansion (Eq. 22.10) gives Eq. 22.11, where the relative change comes from resistivity, length strain and area strain. Because the metal behaves as a fibre in a polymer matrix, the stress state is uniaxial, so area strain is the sum of width and thickness strain (Eq. 22.12) and equals −2νΔL/L₀ (Eq. 22.13), giving Eq. 22.14. The elastic gage factor for constantan comes out just over 2. Under plastic deformation resistivity change is negligible, Poisson's ratio becomes 0.5 and the factor is 2 — annealed foil exploits this to measure beyond 10% strain, at the cost of hysteresis on unloading.

Gages come with various backings (polyimide, glass-fibre reinforced phenolic), open-faced or encapsulated, with solder tabs or attached leads, and in rosettes. Bonding follows a step-by-step procedure: degrease; abrade with fine emery or 400-grit paper; draw (do not scribe) layout lines; clean with isopropyl alcohol using one-pass wipes; neutralise to pH 7–7.5; then apply the gage with cellophane tape, quick-set glue and catalyst, never touching it with bare fingers. The tape lifts the gage, positions it, is peeled back from one side, catalyst is applied, a drop of glue is placed at the tape/specimen joint, and the tape is wiped down at about 30° to drive the glue line forward; press for about a minute, peel the tape back and attach the leads.

The relative resistance change is measured with a Wheatstone bridge (Figure 22.7), which reads a small change against an initial zero rather than against a large resistance. One to four arms may be gages; paths ABC and ADC are dividers, so V_B and V_D follow Eqs. 22.15a and 22.15b, the output is Eq. 22.16 and balance is Eq. 22.17. With small changes the first-order output is Eq. 22.18. Thermal drift cancels directly if R₁ is bonded to the specimen and R₂ is held on it with heat-sink compound: R₁ sees strain plus temperature, R₂ only temperature, and the bridge subtracts them.

Sensitivity is proportional to bridge voltage, but the dissipated power P = I²R must be conducted away or the gage heats and its resistance drifts. Heat-sinking scales with the specimen's thermal conductivity; a reasonable allowable gradient is 40 °C per metre (about 1 °C per 25 mm). For roughly equal arms, P = V_in²/4R (Eq. 22.19), and equating power density to K∇T gives Eqs. 22.20 and 22.21. A 0.010 m × 0.010 m 120 Ω grid on thick aluminium (K = 204 W m⁻¹ °C⁻¹) allows 19 V; thin specimens allow less, and smaller gages force lower excitation. Pulsing the bridge for a short duration cuts the energy dissipated and raises sensitivity 10–100 times; commercial pulse supplies offer widths from 10 µs at 350 V.

Where principal directions are unknown, three gages at three angles form a rosette. The common 0–45–90° pattern gives directions x, x′ and y against principal directions 1 and 2 at angle θ; Mohr's circle (Figure 22.8) is drawn with elongational strain horizontal and shear strain vertical, centre C and radius R. Principal values are Eqs. 22.22–22.23, the centre Eq. 22.24, the gage outputs Eqs. 22.25–22.27, and division gives θ and R via Eqs. 22.28–22.29.

Semiconductor piezoresistive gages work the same way with a gage factor nearly an order of magnitude larger, but the resistance–temperature coupling is very large — a small temperature change can shift resistance an order of magnitude more than strain — so they must be temperature compensated. They are used mainly in transducers such as load cells, are fragile and need great care in application.

Piezoelectric gages are parallel-plate capacitors whose dielectric repolarizes under strain, producing charge on the electrodes. PVDF (Figure 22.9) is cheap but inaccurate and depoles at moderate temperature; it suits dynamic measurements such as frequency and logarithmic decrement, not quantitative strain. Quasistatic use drains charge through the instrument, decaying the signal with a time constant set by input impedance; quartz is very accurate but also loses charge, though electrometers and charge amplifiers give time constants from seconds to hours. Polarization is P = Qd/V (Eq. 22.30), i.e. charge per unit electrode area (Figure 22.10); the Taylor expansion (Eqs. 22.31–22.32) shows PVDF is dominated by the second term and proportional to remanent polarization, which decays with time, depends strongly on temperature and falls rapidly near 50 °C — accuracy within ±3% is possible only if the sensor is kept cool. PZT ceramics are brittle and depole above 200 microstrain, keep polarization to 150 °C or more, with iron-doped "hard" PZT best for stability; quartz is the most accurate, non-polar but strain-polarized, and limited to small strain.

Two conditioning circuits dominate (Figure 22.11). The electrometer drives a capacitor C at least 1000 times C_g through a cable resistance, equilibrating with time constant R_gC_g — about 50 ns risetime — and charge follows from Q = CV (Eq. 22.33). Because instruments drain charge with time constant RC, FET-input op amps extend it to many hours. The charge amplifier is an FET-input op amp with capacitive feedback; it does not amplify charge but produces a voltage proportional to it, again with a time constant of hours, enabling near-static use. High-impedance conditioners and low-cost capacitive terminators for high-frequency work are both available, and piezoelectric sensors are active — they need no external energy source.

Fiber optic gages are miniature interferometers, most commercially as Fabry–Perot cavities (Figure 22.12): laser, single-mode fiber, coupler, sensing cavity and photodetector. Light partly reflects at the fiber end and partly crosses the cavity to reflect from the far end; the recombined beams interfere according to twice the cavity length. Straining the specimen stretches the cavity, cycling constructive and destructive interference — for a 1.3 µm source each peak is a 650 nm gap change, and a 3 mm gage resolves 1 µstrain.

Birefringent film sensing gives a full-field measurement. Two crossed Polaroid sheets with a PMMA bar between them show colour fringes under bending: birefringent materials carry light at different speeds in different directions, so a polarized beam emerges with a phase difference that rotates its polarization, and the analyzer transmits or blocks accordingly. Fast and slow directions are the principal strain directions and the amount of birefringence tracks strain magnitude, giving fringes of constant principal-strain difference, Eq. 22.34; with a silvered backing the light traverses the film twice, giving Eq. 22.35. Quarter-wave plates (Figure 22.13) give a full view of the fringe field, and commercial systems are sold as polariscopes.

Moiré gives a full field but measures displacement, from which strain must be differentiated. Two equally spaced gratings superposed either block or pass light; stretching one changes its pitch so some regions pass light and some do not (Figure 22.14). Fringes of order N correspond to a displacement of one grating pitch, δ = gN. With reference gratings at 0° and 90°, displacements follow Eqs. 22.36–22.37 and strains Eqs. 22.38–22.40. Geometric moiré is usually not sensitive enough for strain distributions, so moiré interferometry is used instead, and out-of-plane measurement uses a single grating interfering with its reflection or shadow.
