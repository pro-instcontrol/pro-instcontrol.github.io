Velocity Measurement

Charles P. Pinney · Pinney Technologies, Inc. · William E. Baker · University of New Mexico

Chapter 16 · 16 figures, 20 references

This chapter is a companion to the Vietnamese translation of Chapter 16 on velocity measurement. All sixteen figures are redrawn as vectors, and the governing equations (16.1–16.4, the Coriolis force, and the differenced-accelerometer relation) are reproduced. The translation prose is not repeated here.

Linear velocity is the time rate of change of position — a vector whose magnitude is speed. Angular velocity is the analogous rate for rotation; the reference frame is set by the measurement method. The chapter opens with the kinematic definitions: average speed over t₁–t₂ is v_avg = (y₂−y₁)/(t₂−t₁) (Eq. 16.1), and in the limit the instantaneous velocity is V_y = dy/dt (Eq. 16.2), the slope of the position–time curve. Because acceleration is the rate of change of velocity, speed follows from integration: V_y(t) = V_y(tᵢ) + ∫ a_y(t) dt (Eq. 16.3). Figure 16.1 shows the position–time graph and the secant whose slope is v_avg.

Reference-based methods measure both the moving object and the reference frame. Examples: two spaced pickups (muzzle velocity), stroboscopic photography, high-speed video, and differentiating a displacement record (noisy). A linear velocity transducer is essentially a linear generator: a coil cutting the field of a permanent magnet induces e_i ∝ B·L·V (Eq. 16.4), shown in Figure 16.2. A dc tach-generator on a rack-and-pinion converts linear to angular speed; the same principle drives an automobile speedometer.

The Doppler shift — an apparent frequency change when source and observer move relative to each other — works for sound, light, or microwaves. Radar and laser-Doppler vibrometers exploit it; VISAR uses interferometry on any reflecting surface for very high speeds. Figures 16.3 (Michelson interferometer) and 16.4 (VISAR fiber-optic setup) illustrate the optical methods. Note the chapter text mislabels the shifts (calling approach a “red shift”); the correct convention is blue shift on approach, red shift on recession — consistent with its own train-whistle example.

Seismic devices need no link to a reference frame. A spring-suspended seismic mass in a housing gives, via a secondary transducer, an output proportional to base acceleration over a band — an accelerometer. Integrating acceleration (Eq. 16.3) yields velocity, avoiding the noise of differentiation but introducing sensitivity to the unknown initial speed, bias/zero-shift, and bandwidth limits. A velocity-proportional output (geophone, vibrometer) results when the motion transducer itself is a linear velocity transducer; the usable low-frequency limit for 5% accuracy is 4.6× the natural frequency. Figure 16.5 shows the seismic instrument.

Angular velocity is usually measured as a pulse train or sine wave whose frequency is proportional to rotational speed. Relative methods (with respect to the body’s base) include dc/ac tach-generators (Figures 16.6–16.8), rotating-magnet sensors (Figure 16.9), optical slotted-disk sensors (Figure 16.10), Hall-effect gear-tooth sensors (Figures 16.11–16.12), and Wiegand-effect sensors (Figure 16.13). Absolute methods use gyroscopes: spinning mass, fluidic, the piezoelectric Coriolis tuning fork (Figure 16.14, force F = 2m·Ω×V_r), the magnetohydrodynamic sensor (Figure 16.15), fiber-optic, and differenced-and-integrated accelerometers (Figure 16.16, ω = (1/l)∫(a₁−a₂)dt). The chapter closes with Poincaré’s observation that absolute velocity cannot be determined.

The chapter lists 20 references (Dally/Riley/McConnell, Doebelin, Barker & Hollenbach on laser interferometry, Valyn on VISAR, Allegro on Hall sensors, Deimel and Scarborough on gyroscopes, Dao on fluidic rate sensors, Kulikovskiy & Lyubimov on MHD, and Feynman’s Lectures) — reproduced in the Vietnamese version above.
