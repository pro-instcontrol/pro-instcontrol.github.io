Acoustic Measurement

Per Rasmussen (G.R.A.S. Sound and Vibration)

Chapter 27 · 21 figures, 19 equations, 5 tables

Sound is normally defined as vibration of a solid, liquid or gaseous medium in the frequency range of the human ear, i.e. between about 20 Hz and 20 kHz. Here the definition is further limited and only vibrations in liquid and gaseous media are considered. In contrast to solid media, a liquid or gaseous medium cannot transmit shear forces, so sound waves are always longitudinal waves, in which the particles move in the direction of propagation of the wave. Wave propagation in gaseous and liquid media can be described by three variables: the pressure p, the particle velocity u and the density ρ. The relation between these is described by the wave equation [1], and this can be derived from three basic equations: the Euler equation (essentially Newton's second law applied to a fluid), the continuity equation and the state equation.

Although the wave equation in principle can be used to describe and calculate all sound waves in all situations, in practice it will often be impossible to perform the necessary calculations. In some special cases it is possible to get analytical results directly from the wave equation, and these cases are therefore of special interest. The cases most often encountered in acoustics are the free field, the diffuse (or reverberant) field, and the closed coupler.

The free field is, in principle, an infinite, empty (except for the medium and the source) space with no reflections, where waves radiate freely in all directions. In practice the free field is implemented in anechoic chambers, where all walls have been made nearly 100 % absorptive. The diffuse field is obtained in a reverberation room where all walls are in principle 100 % reflective; at the same time the walls are made non-parallel, and the result is a sound field with waves in all directions. The closed coupler is a small chamber with dimensions small compared to the wavelength. A special case is the standing wave tube — a tube of diameter smaller than the wavelength with a sound source in one end; with a suitable loudspeaker the propagation can be assumed one-dimensional, which simplifies the mathematics enough for the field to be calculated.

In practice almost the only parameter measured directly in acoustics is the sound pressure, and all other parameters — sound power, particle velocity, reverberation time, directivity — are derived from pressure measurements. These are performed with measurement microphones in gaseous media and hydrophones in liquid media. Measurement microphones are all of the condenser type to ensure precision, long-term stability and sensitivity. Hydrophones are usually made with a rubber coating over a sensitive element of piezoelectric material.

The traditional 20 Hz to 20 kHz range for acoustic measurements is selected because this is the range audible to the human ear. Sound waves exist outside this range as infrasound (below 20 Hz) and ultrasound (above 20 kHz). As the basic equations and measurement principles are the same for both, many of the principles from the 20 Hz to 20 kHz range extend to these ranges.

27.1 The Wave Equation Phương trình sóng

Sound wave propagation cannot take place in a vacuum but is always associated with some kind of medium. For simplicity, assume this medium is air, although the same equations are valid for all gaseous and fluid media. In this medium the concept of an air particle can be introduced: a small volume of air in which acoustical parameters such as pressure and density can be considered constant. On the other hand the volume must be large enough to include a very large number of molecules, so that it can be considered a continuous medium and not a collection of molecules. The Euler equation for such a particle is:

(27.1)−grad p = ρ₀ (∂v ⁄ ∂t)

where p is the pressure, ρ₀ the density and v the particle velocity. This can be considered as Newton's second law (F = ma) applied to a fluid: the gradient of the pressure equals the force F acting on the particle, the density equals the mass m, and the time-differentiated particle velocity ∂v/∂t equals the acceleration.

The second equation needed is the continuity equation. It simply states that if you have a small volume of air and bring in some extra air, the density (or the mass) will increase. Mathematically:

(27.2)div v = −(1 ⁄ ρ₀) ∂ρ ⁄ ∂t

where c is the sound velocity, which depends on the composition of the air and the temperature. For normal air at 0 °C the velocity is 314 m s⁻¹, while at 20 °C it is 340 m s⁻¹.

The third equation is the state equation, relating pressure changes to changes in density — if a small volume of air is compressed, the density increases:

(27.3)∂ρ ⁄ ∂t = (1 ⁄ c²) × ∂p ⁄ ∂t

Now three equations relate the three variables. Eliminating the particle velocity and the density gives one differential equation for the sound pressure:

(27.4)∇²p = (1 ⁄ c²) ∂²p ⁄ ∂t²

This is the wave equation for acoustic waves in gaseous and fluid media. In principle it allows the sound pressure anywhere in a sound field to be calculated, given suitable boundary conditions. In practice, however, solutions can only be found in a few simple cases.

27.2 Plane Sound Waves Sóng âm phẳng

In free space, at great distance from the source, sound waves are approximately plane waves, so the wave equation depends on only one coordinate. If the wave fronts propagate in the x-direction, the solution reduces to:

(27.5)p = A cos[(ωt − k)(x + φ₀)]

where ω is the frequency. Similarly the particle velocity in the free field is:

(27.6)v = (A ⁄ ρc) cos[(ωt − k)(x + φ₀)] = p ⁄ (ρc)

This means that in a plane wave the particle velocity equals the pressure divided by the constant ρc, and pressure and particle velocity are in phase. The constant ρc is the acoustic impedance; for air at 20 °C the density is 1.29 kg m⁻³ and the sound velocity 340 m s⁻¹, giving an acoustic impedance of 438.6 kg m⁻² s⁻¹.

The plane wave transmits energy in the direction of propagation. The power transmitted per unit area is the intensity in the direction of propagation (older textbooks mistakenly used “the intensity of the sound” for the magnitude of the sound pressure). In general the intensity is the product of sound pressure and sound velocity, so for the plane wave the intensity I follows from Equations 27.5 and 27.6:

(27.7)I = v p = p² ⁄ (ρc)

Thus in the plane wave the transmitted intensity can be calculated from the sound pressure, and since sound power is intensity per unit area, the sound power is the intensity multiplied by the area.

27.3 Spherical Waves Sóng cầu

Another simple solution describes radiation from a point source into free space. The point source is an infinitely small sphere whose surface pulsates radially. In practice, for small sources (dimensions small compared to the wavelength) the point source is a good approximation to the real physical source, which makes the spherical solution of special interest.

The wave equation is transformed into spherical coordinates r, θ, ψ. As the point source radiates equally in all directions, the solution depends only on the distance r from its centre:

(27.8)p = (P₀ ⁄ r) cos(ωt − kr)

The sound pressure is seen to be inversely proportional to the distance from the source. The particle velocity splits into a near-field contribution vn and a far-field contribution vf:

(27.9)vf = (P₀ ⁄ ρc)(1 ⁄ r) cos(ωt − kr)
(27.10)vn = (P₀ ⁄ ωρr²) sin(ωt − kr)

The far-field contribution in 27.9 is in phase with the pressure in 27.8, and the particle velocity is inversely proportional to the distance r. The near-field contribution is inversely proportional to the square of the distance and therefore dies away rapidly as the distance increases.

As with the plane wave, the intensity in the spherical wave is the product of pressure and particle velocity. For the near-field contribution:

(27.11)I = vn p = (P₀ ⁄ ωρr²) sin(ωt − kr) · (P₀ ⁄ r) cos(ωt − kr) = 0

That is, the near-field part of the particle velocity does not contribute to the radiated power, since it is 90° out of phase with the pressure. The far-field contribution gives:

(27.12)I = vf p = (P₀² ⁄ r²ρc) cos²(ωt − kr)

The intensity decreases with the square of the distance; combining 27.8 and 27.12:

(27.13)I = (P₀² ⁄ r²ρc) cos²(ωt − kr) = p² ⁄ (ρc)

identical to 27.7 for the plane wave. Thus, as for the plane progressive wave, the intensity in the spherical wave can be calculated from the pressure.

27.4 Acoustic Measurements Phép đo âm học

As the wave equation shows, the full acoustic field can in principle be described from pressure measurements alone, so every other acoustic parameter can be derived from pressure, and in practice pressure is often the only parameter measured. There have been a few attempts to make transducers for particle velocity based on, for example, transmission of ultrasonic waves, but the absolutely dominating transducer for acoustic measurement is the condenser microphone, Figure 27.1. These have proved superior in temperature stability, long-term stability and insensitivity to rough handling. While measurement microphones are designed and produced to ensure well-defined, accurate measurements, a wide range of other microphones serve other purposes — for incorporation in telephones, where price is decisive, or for studio recording, where subjective evaluation matters more than objective performance.

27.4.1 Condenser Microphones Micro condenser

The condenser microphone consists basically of five elements: protection grid, casing, diaphragm, backplate and insulator; see Figure 27.2. Diaphragm and backplate form the parallel plates of an air capacitor. This capacitor is polarized with a charge from an external voltage supply (externally polarized type) or by an electric charge injected directly into an insulating material on the backplate (prepolarized type). When the sound pressure in the field fluctuates, the distance between diaphragm and backplate changes and so does the capacitance. As the charge is kept constant, the change in capacitance generates an output voltage at the microphone terminal.

The acoustical performance of a microphone is determined by its physical dimensions: diaphragm area, diaphragm-to-backplate spacing, the stiffness and mass of the suspended diaphragm, and the internal volume of the casing. These set the frequency range, the sensitivity and the dynamic range. The sensitivity is the output voltage for a given sound pressure excitation; in itself it is of little interest except for calibration purposes. However, the sensitivity (together with the electric impedance of the cartridge) also determines the lowest sound pressure level that can be measured. For example, a microphone with 2.5 mV Pa⁻¹ reaches about 40 dB (re. 20 µPa), while one with 50 mV Pa⁻¹ reaches about 15 dB (re. 20 µPa).

Size is the first parameter determining sensitivity: in general the larger the diaphragm diameter, the more sensitive the microphone. There are, however, limits to how sensitive a microphone can be made by simply enlarging it. The polarization voltage between diaphragm and backplate attracts the diaphragm and deflects it toward the backplate; as the microphone grows, the deflection grows until the diaphragm touches the backplate. Increasing the spacing or decreasing the polarization voltage avoids this, but both reduce sensitivity. Hence the optimum size of a practical measurement microphone for use up to 20 kHz is very close to ½″ (12.6 mm).

Conversely, decreasing the size increases the useful frequency range, which is partly set by the size. At high frequencies, when the wavelength becomes much smaller than the diaphragm diameter, the diaphragm stops behaving like a rigid piston and “breaks up” (not a destructive phenomenon): different parts move with different magnitude and phase and the frequency response changes. To avoid this, the upper limiting frequency is placed so that the sensitivity drops off before breakup begins. For a typical 0.5 in. microphone this gives an upper limit between 20 kHz and 40 kHz depending on diaphragm tension. A stiffer diaphragm raises the resonance but reduces sensitivity, because the deflection for a given sound pressure level is smaller.

The frequency response is determined by diaphragm tension, diaphragm mass and the acoustical damping in the airgap between diaphragm and backplate. The system is represented by the mechanical analogy of a mass–spring–damper system, Figure 27.3. The mass represents the diaphragm mass and the spring its tension, so a stiffer diaphragm is a stiffer spring. The damping element represents the acoustical damping between diaphragm and backplate, which can be adjusted by, for example, drilling holes in the backplate: air then escapes the airgap more easily when the diaphragm deflects, reducing damping.

The frequency response of this simple mechanical model is given in Figure 27.4 together with the influence of the different parameters. At low frequencies (below resonance) the response is set by diaphragm tension, and as described, lowering the tension raises the sensitivity. The resonant frequency is set by tension and diaphragm mass: more tension, higher resonance; more mass, lower resonance. The response around resonance is set by the acoustical damping, where more damping lowers the response.

Although materials and assembly techniques have changed in recent years, the basic microphone types remain unchanged: free-field, pressure and random-incidence microphones, built with different frequency characteristics to match different requirements.

The pressure microphone measures the actual sound pressure as it exists on the diaphragm. A typical application is measurement in a closed coupler or, as in Figure 27.5, at a boundary — the microphone forms part of the wall and measures the pressure on the wall itself. Its frequency response should be flat over as wide a range as possible, bearing in mind that sensitivity falls as the range is extended. The acoustical damping in the airgap is adjusted so that the response is flat up to and a little beyond the resonant frequency.

The free-field microphone is designed to measure the sound pressure as it existed before the microphone was introduced into the field. At higher frequencies the presence of the microphone itself changes the pressure: in general the pressure around the cartridge increases because of reflections and diffraction. The free-field microphone is designed so that its frequency characteristics compensate for this increase, so the output is proportional to the pressure as it existed before insertion. It should always be pointed toward the source (0° incidence), as in Figure 27.6.

In that situation the diaphragm in the field causes a pressure increase in front of it, Figure 27.7(a), depending on wavelength and microphone diameter. For a typical ½″ microphone the maximum increase occurs at 26.9 kHz, where the wavelength (λ = 342 m s⁻¹ / 26.9 kHz ≈ 12.7 mm ≈ 0.5 in.) coincides with the diameter. The microphone is then designed so that its sensitivity decreases by the same amount as the pressure rises, by increasing the internal acoustical damping to obtain a response as in Figure 27.7(b). The result, Figure 27.7(c), is an output proportional to the pressure as it existed before insertion. The curve in Figure 27.7(a) is also called the “free-field correction curve”, since it must be added to the cartridge response to obtain the free-field characteristic.

In principle the free-field microphone must be pointed at the source and the waves must travel in essentially one direction. In some cases — measuring in a reverberation room or other highly reflecting surroundings — the waves have no well-defined direction but arrive from all directions at once. Waves from the front cause a pressure increase as described, while waves from the back are reduced to some extent by the shadowing effect of the cartridge. The combined influence therefore depends on the directional distribution of the sound. For measurement microphones a standard distribution has been defined on statistical grounds, resulting in the standardized random-incidence microphone.

Measurement microphones can be externally polarized or prepolarized. Externally polarized types are by far the most stable and accurate and should be preferred for precision work. Prepolarized microphones are preferred in some cases because they need no external polarization source — typically on small hand-held devices such as sound level meters, where a polarization supply would add excessively to cost, weight and battery consumption. Still, prepolarized microphones are in general much less stable to environmental changes than externally polarized ones.

27.5 Sound Pressure Level Measurements Đo mức áp suất âm

The human ear basically hears sound pressure, but its sensitivity varies with frequency: it is most sensitive between 1 kHz and 5 kHz and falls at higher and lower frequencies. This led to several frequency weighting functions that attempt to replicate the sensitivity of the ear. The ear's response to time-varying signals and impulses led to instruments with well-defined time weighting functions. The resulting instrument is the sound level meter, as defined for example by IEC International Standard 651, “Sound Level Meters” [2]. The standard defines four classes for different accuracies (Table 27.1). Type 0 is the most accurate, intended for precision laboratory measurements; Type 1 is most widely used for general-purpose measurements, see Figure 27.8. Type 2 is used where low price matters, while Type 3 is not used in practice because the wide tolerances make results too unreliable. The output of the sound level meter is in principle assumed to approximate the impression perceived by the human ear.

Functionally the sound level meter divides into four parts: microphone and preamplifier, A-weighting filter, rms detector and display (Figure 27.9). The microphone must ensure correct measurement of the sound pressure within the frequency range of the given class; the standard also sets requirements on directivity. The frequency response of the instrument, including the weighting filter, is specified for waves arriving along the reference direction. For waves from other directions the standard allows wider tolerances at higher frequencies, acknowledging the inevitable reflections and diffraction there.

The preamplifier converts the high-impedance microphone output to a low-impedance signal but has in itself no voltage gain, or even negative gain. The signal then passes through an A-weighting filter — a standardized filter that in principle resembles the sensitivity of the human ear, so that a measurement using it correlates with the subjective response of an average listener. The filter, with the characteristic of Figure 27.10, attenuates low and high frequencies and slightly amplifies the 1 kHz to 5 kHz region. Other weighting curves — B, C and D — may correlate better with subjective response in special cases, such as very high or very low levels, or aircraft noise.

The signal then passes through an exponential rms detector with a time constant of either 125 ms (“fast”) or 1 s (“slow”). These time constants simulate the behaviour of the ear under time-varying signals. Especially when the stimulus duration becomes short (around 200 ms), the sound is judged subjectively lower than the same sound heard continuously; the same effect is obtained with “fast” averaging. This, however, gives a higher statistical uncertainty on the level estimate than “slow”, so “slow” should be chosen for a continuous signal. Other standards describe special meters such as integrating or impulse sound level meters for special purposes.

27.6 Frequency Analyzers Máy phân tích tần số

While the sound level meter gives a single reading for the 20 Hz to 20 kHz range, more detailed information about the frequency content is often wanted. Two types of analyser are common in acoustics: FFT analyzers and real-time filter analyzers. FFT analyzers, with very high frequency resolution, give a wealth of information and make it possible to separate closely spaced harmonics (e.g. from a gearbox). In contrast, real-time filter analyzers, Figure 27.11, use a much coarser resolution, usually 1/3-octave bands. The analysis is performed by a bank of filters (nowadays mostly digital) with well-defined frequency and time responses. Filter responses have been internationally standardized, with centre frequencies and passbands as in Table 27.2. Over 20 Hz to 20 kHz the 1/3-octave bank holds 31 filters measuring the input simultaneously. The resulting 1/3-octave spectrum resembles the subjective response of the human ear.

27.7 Pressure-Based Measurements Các phép đo dựa trên áp suất

The result of sound pressure measurements is influenced by many factors: source, source operating conditions, surroundings, measurement position, and so on. Depending on the goal these can be controlled in different ways. If the goal is to quantify the noise exposure at an operator's ear in a noisy environment, the microphone must be where the operator's ear would normally be, and the environment must equal the normal operating environment. If instead the task is to describe the source as a noise-emitting machine, the influence of the environment must be minimized.

If the aim is to describe the object as a noise source, it is customary to state the radiated sound power — a global parameter quantifying total noise radiation, to a certain extent independent of the environment. Sound power can be measured in several ways: in a free field, in a reverberation room, by a substitution technique, or by sound intensity. A free field is one in which sound radiates freely in all directions with no restricting walls or reflections; this is most often obtained in a semi-anechoic chamber, where all walls and the ceiling are covered with nearly 100 % absorptive material and only the floor is reflective. With the source in the chamber the waves radiate freely away, and in the far field they can be considered plane or spherical, so the intensity follows from pressure measurements via Equation 27.7.

As real sources seldom radiate equally in all directions, a number of measurements around the object are averaged. ISO Standard 3745 “Acoustics — Determination of sound power levels of noise sources — Precision method for anechoic and semi-anechoic rooms” [3] specifies an array of microphone positions on a hemisphere over the object, as in Figure 27.12, with coordinates as in Table 27.3. As all points are associated with the same area, and sound power is intensity times area, the total radiated sound power is:

(27.14)P = A Σ In = (2πr ⁄ ρc) Σ pn²

where A is the area of the test hemisphere of radius r and pn the pressure measured at point n.

27.8 Sound Intensity Measurements Đo cường độ âm

The calculation of sound power from pressure measurements in 27.14 rests on Equation 27.7. That equation — intensity from a pressure measurement — is valid only in a free field, in the direction of propagation. In general, with background noise or reflections from walls, it is not possible to calculate the intensity from a single pressure measurement. In these cases the intensity can be measured directly with a two-microphone intensity probe, Figure 27.13.

Sound intensity I is the product of pressure and particle velocity:

(27.15)I = p v

While pressure p is a scalar independent of direction, particle velocity is a vector quantity, directionally dependent. When the velocity is written as in 27.15 it is implicit that it refers to a certain direction and that the resulting intensity is calculated in the same direction.

For example, the particle velocity along the propagation direction, Figure 27.14(a), gives intensity radiated away from the point source, while the velocity perpendicular to it, Figure 27.14(b), is zero. The intensity from 27.15 is therefore zero in the direction perpendicular to propagation even though the pressure is the same. Sound energy flows radially away from the point source and none flows tangentially.

Measuring intensity per 27.15 requires both pressure and particle velocity. With the two-microphone probe the pressure midway between the microphones is the mean of the two:

(27.16)p = (p₁ + p₂) ⁄ 2

The particle velocity v along the probe axis follows from the pressure difference between the two measurements:

(27.17)v = ∫ (p₂ − p₁) ⁄ (ρΔr) ∂τ

where ρ is the density of air and Δr the spacing. The intensity is then the product of pressure and velocity:

(27.18)I = p v = ((p₁ + p₂) ⁄ 2) ∫ (p₂ − p₁) ⁄ (ρΔr) ∂τ

Intensity measurement is a powerful tool to localize noise sources and determine the sound power radiated by a source, even in the presence of other strong sources.

27.9 Near-Field Acoustic Holography Measurements Đo bằng holography âm học vùng gần

The term acoustic holography comes from the analogy with optical holography. It is well known how holography, as opposed to a normal photograph, allows the full image of an object to be reconstructed: it “records” both the magnitude and the phase of the light, whereas a photograph records only magnitude. Similarly, in acoustic holography both magnitude and phase of the sound field are measured over a plane surface, giving a complete description in which both are known at all points. Acoustic quantities can then be calculated, including the intensity distribution, particle velocity, sound power and radiation pattern.

The basic assumption behind near-field acoustic holography (NAH) is that the sound field decomposes into two simple wave types: plane waves and evanescent waves. Plane waves describe the part propagating away from the near field toward the far field, evanescent waves the complicated field existing in the near field. Any sound field can be described as a combination of plane and evanescent waves with different magnitudes and directions.

Magnitude and direction of the individual waves are described by their spatial frequencies, or wavenumbers. For a simple plane wave propagating in a given direction this can be described by its temporal frequency as well as by its spatial frequency. The temporal frequency, Figure 27.15(a), is obtained by looking at pressure changes with time at a point, giving hertz or radians per second. The spatial frequency, Figure 27.15(b), is obtained by looking at pressure changes at a given instant: at that instant the pressure differs from place to place, and moving in a given direction reveals a certain change corresponding to a spatial frequency, measured in cycles per metre or radians per metre. As temporal frequency tells how often pressure changes with time at a point, spatial frequency tells how often it changes with position at an instant. In the example of Figure 27.15(b) the propagation direction coincided with the axis along which spatial frequency was measured.

In this case, shown again in Figure 27.16(a), the relation between the spatial frequency k₀ (the wavenumber) and the temporal frequency f is given by the speed of sound:

(27.19)k₀ = 2π ⁄ λ = 2πf ⁄ c = ω ⁄ c

where λ is the wavelength. If the measurement axis is not the propagation direction, see Figure 27.16(b), this simple relation fails: the temporal frequency is as in 27.16(a) but the spatial frequency is lower. For one particular temporal frequency the spatial frequencies therefore carry information about propagation directions. Hence if the field is made up of several plane waves with the same temporal frequency but different directions, this appears in the spatial spectrum as several spatial frequency components.

If, for example, the field is the sum of two waves, Figure 27.17, one travelling along the measurement axis and the other at 45° to it, the spatial spectrum contains two spatial frequencies: k for the axial wave and k·cos(45°) for the oblique one. So far spatial frequencies have been defined along a single axis, corresponding to a one-dimensional Fourier transform. In NAH the field is sampled over a plane, so a two-dimensional transform is used instead, giving a two-dimensional spatial frequency spectrum — but the information is the same: direction and magnitude of the simple wave types.

The field of a point source cannot be explained by simple plane waves as in Figures 27.16 and 27.17, since the amplitude decreases with distance from the origin while plane waves keep the same magnitude over the whole plane. To describe the near-field phenomenon one must introduce evanescent waves. In the one-dimensional spectrum they are identified as spatial frequencies above k₀ = 2πf/c; similarly in two dimensions, as wavenumbers above k₀.

Each spatial frequency in the two-dimensional spectrum corresponds to a simple plane or evanescent wave in the scan plane. For each type it is easy to calculate the pressure in other planes, see Figure 27.18: for plane waves a simple phase shift suffices; for evanescent waves the amplitude change must be included, but this too is a simple transfer function applied to the spectrum. The spectrum in a new plane is thus obtained from the original data by simple transfer function operations, and an inverse two-dimensional Fourier transform then gives the sound field in that plane.

The overall principle of NAH is summarized in Figure 27.19. The field is scanned in a plane close to the object, giving an array of temporal spectra, one per scan position. Taking one temporal frequency at a time, the information corresponding to that frequency is extracted from each spectrum, producing a new array holding only that frequency. A two-dimensional Fourier transform then yields the two-dimensional spatial frequency spectrum, which is transformed to new planes by simple transfer function operations. An inverse transform finally gives the pressure distribution in the new plane.

In principle NAH requires all cross-spectra between all scan positions: at each position the cross-spectra to every other position must be determined. A simple scan with N = 1000 positions would need ½N(N + 1) = 500,500 cross-spectra. Instead of measuring them all, the system uses a set of reference transducers to reduce the number. How many references are needed for a complete description without the full data set is determined by the complexity of the field: a measurement with, for example, four references and 2540 scan positions reduces to 4N = 4000 cross-spectrum measurements.

27.10 Calibration Hiệu chuẩn

To make accurate and reliable measurements the microphone and connected instruments must be properly calibrated. Microphone calibration divides into a level calibration and a frequency response calibration. The level calibration establishes the output for a given acoustic input at a given frequency; the frequency response gives the output at other frequencies relative to the calibration frequency. Level calibration can be performed by several methods of differing accuracy.

The most accurate is the reciprocity method. It exploits the fact that a condenser microphone is a reciprocal transducer: it works as a microphone (acoustic to voltage) and as a loudspeaker (voltage to acoustic). By measuring the relationship among three test microphones driven as both transmitters and receivers, one obtains three equations whose unknowns are the three sensitivities; solving them gives all three. Reciprocity calibration is very accurate but tedious, needs well-controlled environmental conditions, and is therefore seldom used in practice.

Comparison and substitution methods are essentially identical, both measuring the difference between the test microphone and a reference of known sensitivity. The reference is often calibrated at an accredited national acoustical laboratory such as NIST, NPL or PTB, which ensures traceability. In the substitution method the acoustic output of a source is measured with the reference; the reference is then replaced by the test microphone and the output measured again. Provided the source has been stable, the sensitivity of the test microphone follows. The comparison method is similar except that reference and test microphone are subjected to the same sound pressure simultaneously, so the stability requirement is less important.

An often-used method is the pistonphone method. The pistonphone, Figure 27.20, is a very stable source producing a well-defined sound pressure level inside a closed coupler. It works by volume displacement, Figure 27.21, with a well-defined velocity, usually at 250 Hz: as the piston moves in and out the cavity volume changes and pressure varies. The actual level depends on coupler volume, piston volume displacement, barometric pressure and — to a lesser degree — humidity, heat dissipation and similar factors. Being based on a simple mechanical system, the pistonphone is very reliable and easy to use, with an accuracy around 0.1 dB. It also often serves as the stable source for comparison or substitution calibrations.

A sound pressure calibrator is basically a small self-contained comparison calibration device. The test microphone is inserted into a small closed volume and a small loudspeaker produces a single frequency, usually 1 kHz. The loudspeaker output is controlled in a feedback loop using a signal from a reference microphone. Provided reference and feedback gain are stable, the level at the test microphone is well-defined and the sensitivity can be determined. Sound level calibrators are normally not used for accurate microphone calibration but for field checks of the integrity of a complete measurement system.

The frequency response of a microphone is most often determined by the electrostatic actuator method. A conducting grid is placed close to and parallel with the diaphragm, and an electric field is established by applying 800 V dc to the actuator. A test signal of 50 to 150 V ac is superimposed, and the electrostatic forces push and pull the diaphragm, similar to a sound pressure of 1 to 10 Pa. Sweeping the test signal through the frequencies of interest records the pressure response. The technique is widely used as a convenient and accurate test method, both during production and in the final calibration of measurement microphones.
