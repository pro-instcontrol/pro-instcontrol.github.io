Resistive Displacement Sensors

Keith Antonelli, James Ko & Shyan Ku · Kinetic Sciences Inc.

Section 6.1 · 6 figures · about 3,633 words

A potentiometer is the cheapest displacement sensor there is, and the one most often misapplied. This companion page carries the six redrawn figures, the six tables, and the engineering argument behind them; the full text is in the Vietnamese edition.

What the pot actually is

A resistive element, a wiper that slides on it, and a voltage divider. That is the whole device. Its virtues are real — no electronics at the sensor, a large output signal, a proven construction — and so are its costs: friction, inertia, wear, and a bandwidth limited by the mechanics.

The one equation that matters

Everything about loading follows from Equation 6.1: the output is not x/xP but x/xP divided by 1 + (x/xP)(1 − x/xP)(RP/RL). The error term vanishes at both ends of travel and peaks at mid-scale, which is why a pot that reads correctly at 0 % and 100 % can still be 2 % out in the middle. The rule of thumb — keep RL/RP at 10 or more — holds the peak error to roughly 1 % of full scale.

(6.1)vo/vr = (x/xP) / [1 + (x/xP)(1 − x/xP)(RP/RL)]

Figure 6.3 shows the other half of the accuracy story: linearity is quoted against a best-fit line, not against the end points, so a pot with 0.1 % independent linearity can still be several tenths of a percent away from the terminal-based line a naive calibration would draw.

The decision rule is short: conductive plastic unless you need to dissipate power or survive heat, wirewound when you do, hybrid when you want both and can pay for it. Cermet and carbon composition appear in the chapter only to be ruled out.

Mounting and coupling

The bushing mount needs a drilled hole and tolerates a sloppy one; the servo mount needs a bored hole and rewards it with repeatability. Either way the pot must not be used as a bearing — the shaft takes no thrust, side or bending load, the hard stops live outside the pot, and a compliant coupling absorbs whatever misalignment the bracket cannot.

When a mechanism earns its place

Section 6.1 ends with seven mechanisms — gears, rack and pinion, lead screws, drums, cams, belts, linkages — and the reason to use one is always the same: to trade travel for resolution. A rotary pot geared to a lead screw sees many turns of wiper travel for one turn of the screw, so its quantisation is divided by the ratio. Whether that helps depends on whether the backlash you add is smaller than the resolution you gain. The chapter's own worked comparison makes the point: resolution is not accuracy, and a mechanism can buy one while selling the other.
