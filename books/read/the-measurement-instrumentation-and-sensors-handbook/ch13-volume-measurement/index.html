Volume Measurement

Rene G. Aarnink · Hessel Wijkstra · University Hospital Nijmegen

Chapter 13 · 10 figures, 1 table, 27 equations

All ten figures are redrawn as vectors — the air-plethysmograph occlusion trace, the two impedance electrode layouts, the prostate ellipsoid, the step-section planimetry, the ellipsoid theoretical model, the step-size error plot, the indicator time-concentration curve, the water-overflow schematic, and the leg disk model — plus Table 13.1 of techniques and equipment. The chapter opens with the simple cases — analytic formulae for regular solids, fluid displacement for irregular objects, and filling a cavity with fluid to measure its volume — then turns to medical volume: plethysmography, imaging-based integration, indicator dilution, and water displacement. Plethysmography surrounds the organ with a rigid chamber of water or air; the displaced fluid or changed air pressure marks the volume change from arterial in-flow. In venous occlusion (cuff at 40–60 mm Hg) arterial blood enters but venous blood cannot leave, so the limb grows each beat by the volume that entered (Figure 13.1).

Air plethysmography rests on Boyle's law, Pi Vi = Pf Vf (13.1): the cuff pressure change over the cardiac cycle is compared against removing known amounts of air. Whole-body plethysmography (the patient in an airtight box) uses the same law twice — first the box volume change equals the chest volume change, then the unknown FRC is solved from the mouth-pressure measurements.

Electrical plethysmography avoids the chamber. The strain-gage (Whitney, 1953) wraps an elastic mercury or electrolyte tube around the limb; its stretch δl gives the volume change because δV/V = 2 δl/l0 (13.2)–(13.4), and flow rate is 2(δl/l0)·(100/t) ml·min⁻¹·(100 g)⁻¹. Impedance plethysmography uses Z = ρ l²/V (13.5); a small volume change δV relates to δZ by δV/V = −δZ/Z (13.6)–(13.7). The four-electrode scheme of Kubicek (1974) drives current through the outer pair and measures voltage across the inner pair (Figures 13.2, 13.3), which removes the unknown current distribution near the electrodes. Inductive plethysmography (1978) wraps coils on rib cage and abdomen; the changing cross-section changes inductance, converted to proportional voltage, so tidal volume VT = VRC + VAB (13.8).

Section 13.2 is the numerical heart of the chapter. For an ellipsoid prostate, Vp = (π/6) H W L (13.9); more generally V = ∭ f(x,y,z) dx dy dz (13.10). The integral is approximated by a linear combination of integrand values — the quadrature (13.11) and its Riemann-sum form (13.12). In three dimensions this becomes the sum over cells (13.13), and if each section's area Sk is taken at a fixed intersection distance h, it collapses to the rectangular rule V ≈ h Σ S(a + k h) (13.14), with h = (b−a)/n. The midpoint (13.15) and trapezoidal (13.16) rules are exact for linear functions and converge at least as n−2. Figure 13.5 is the step-section planimetry in practice, and Figures 13.6–13.7 show the spheroid model used to test how step size and first-section position drive the error: when the step is no more than about 6% of the longest diameter, volume error stays under 5% [15].

Section 13.3 is the Stewart–Hamilton indicator-dilution principle: if a known mass Ii of indicator is dispersed uniformly in an unknown volume, the flow is F = Ii / ∫C(t)dt (13.18) and the volume is F·tm (13.19). The area under the time-concentration curve (Figure 13.20) is found by planimetry and the Riemann sum; recirculation must be removed. Thermodilution injects a cold bolus and recovers flow from a modified Stewart–Hamilton equation (13.20)–(13.22), with the user-supplied constant K accounting for specific weights, specific heats, and a catheter correction factor C < 1. Radionuclide imaging (first-pass and dynamic, ECG-gated) yields ejection fraction EF = (cd−cs)/(cd−cb) (13.23). Gas dilution (helium, conservation of mass) recovers absolute lung volume that spirometry cannot.

Section 13.4, water-displacement volumetry, directly measures irregular limbs with an overflow tank (Figure 13.9) but is slow, hygiene-prone, and unsuitable immediately post-operative. The disk model (Figure 13.10) divides the leg into disks of thickness h and sums V = Σ π r² h = Σ C² h/(4π) (13.24), where C is each disk's circumference; it matches water displacement in accuracy and, at a 10 cm disk height, already exceeds 95% [15, 19], so it is the method of choice for extremities.

Section 13.6 weighs the methods. Chamber plethysmography (water-filled) is temperature-stable but brings hydrostatic and sweating artifacts and is cumbersome around exercise; the air type drifts with its high expansion coefficient. Impedance plethysmography reliably detects pulsations and pulse-transit times but cannot be tied to an absolute volume, and is motion-sensitive — an active research area. A low-cost inductive design monitors ventilation via chest/abdomen bands, and acoustic plethysmography measures total body volume of preterm infants through a Helmholtz resonator whose frequency rises as the infant displaces air. Imaging-based planimetry is now routine for prostate and heart, and automated contour detection (references [24]–[27]) points toward fully automated organ volumetry.

The defining-terms list is worth keeping: plethysmography measures volume change inside a rigid air/water chamber; venous occlusion isolates arterial in-flow; the strain-gage, impedance and inductive variants are the electrical alternatives; step-section planimetry and the disk model are the numerical/geometric stand-ins for water displacement; and indicator dilution recovers an unknown volume from a known mass and a measured concentration curve.
