Force Measurement

M. A. Elbestawi · McMaster University

Chapter 23 · 17 figures, 1 table, 17 equations, 10 references

Force is a vector quantity: an action that causes acceleration or a definite reaction of a body. This chapter surveys the ways the magnitude of such forces can be determined. Measurement rests on a few principles — balancing an unknown force against a standard mass through levers, measuring the acceleration of a known mass, equalising it with a magnetic force from a current-carrying coil and a magnet, spreading it over a known area to make a pressure and measuring that pressure, or converting it into the deformation of an elastic element — and the engineering difficulty lies almost entirely in the sensor design.

23.1 General Considerations Những xem xét chung

If the forces on a body produce no acceleration they must form a system in equilibrium, and the body is in static equilibrium. Forces are internal where the particles of the body act on one another, external otherwise. A supported body under load deforms or displaces at its supports; the internal forces redistribute until equilibrium is reached, and the body is then in tension, compression or shear. Across any section the internal forces act in equal and opposite pairs, whereas external forces act singly.

23.2 Hooke's Law Định luật Hooke

Loaded in uniaxial tension, uniaxial compression or simple shear, a metal behaves elastically up to a critical normal or shear stress and then deforms plastically; in the elastic region the atoms are displaced temporarily and return when the load is removed. Poisson's ratio is the ratio of transverse to direct strain and lies between 1/4 and 1/3 for metals. Hooke's law gives stress proportional to strain, with the Young's modulus in tension or compression and the shear modulus in shear. The fractional change of specific volume follows from the elastic deformation and, for an isotropic material, equals the direct strain times (1 − 2ν); the bulk modulus is the pressure change divided by that fractional volume change. An elastic solid is therefore compressible as long as ν is below 1/2, which is normally true of metals. The uniaxial form generalises to three dimensions, and the well-established theory of elasticity underpins every force-measuring technique below. Note that force measurement is highly application specific and the choice of technique deserves care.

Basic Methods of Force Measurement Các phương pháp cơ bản để đo lực

Five routes are available: balance the unknown force against a standard mass through a lever system; measure the acceleration of a known mass; equalise it with the magnetic force between a current-carrying coil and a magnet; spread it over a known area to generate a pressure and measure that pressure; or convert it into the deformation of an elastic element. These routes produce a wide variety of instruments, and the sensor design reduces to two problems — the geometric and physical constraints imposed by the application, and the means of turning force into a workable signal such as an electronic voltage or a graduated displacement.

23.3 Force Sensors Cảm biến lực

Force sensors reveal how a system responds. Cutting forces in machining are monitored to detect tool failure, to diagnose its causes while controlling process parameters, and to judge surface quality; impact forces are monitored in the automotive industry; robotic handling and assembly are controlled by sensing the forces at the end effector. Some sensors measure the deflection a force produces, which needs deflections of several micrometres to be practical — the excellent elastic properties of helical springs make them successful here, with Hooke's law relating load to deflection. Sensors using strain gages or piezoelectric quartz crystals with built-in microelectronics are common and handle both impulsive and slowly varying forces.

Load cells form a general subgroup: a rigid outer structure, a medium that responds to the applied force, and the measuring gage. They sense large, static or slowly varying forces with little deflection and are relatively accurate, typically to about 0.1% of full scale. Figure 23.3 shows several types that sense large forces at modest cost; the hydraulic cell, for instance, uses a very stiff outer structure with an internal cavity filled with fluid, so that applying a load raises the oil pressure, which is read on an accurate gage. Piezoelectric transducers give faster response to varying loads, and pneumatic methods and strain gages cover other conditions.

Strain Gage Load Cell Load cell gage biến dạng

A strain gage load cell pairs a structure that deforms elastically under load with a strain gage network that produces a signal proportional to that deformation; beam and ring types are the common examples.

Strain Gages Gage biến dạng

A strain gage is a length of gage wire wound as a flat coil to give the wanted resistance, usually about 120 Ω, cemented between two thin insulating sheets of paper or plastic. It cannot measure deflection directly: it must first be bonded to the member to be strained, after which the assembly is baked at about 195°F (90°C) to drive off moisture and coated with wax or resin for mechanical protection. The resistance between the test member and the gage must be at least 50 MΩ, and the total conductor area must stay small so the cement can transmit the force needed to deform the wire. As the member is stressed the gage deforms and its cross-section shrinks, raising its resistivity in a way that is easily measured. Very small strains require measuring small fractional resistance changes, ΔR/R, and the change for a bonded gage is usually under 0.5%. Many gage sizes and grid shapes exist (Figure 23.4).

Rigidity and environment both demand care. Shorter gages have higher response frequencies — 660 kHz for a 0.2 mm gage against 20 kHz for a 60 mm gage — and because resistance is a function of temperature, any temperature variation at the gage calls for temperature compensation if the force measurement is to stay accurate.

A Wheatstone bridge (Figure 23.5) measures this small magnitude. No current flows through the galvanometer when the four resistances satisfy Equation 23.7. Stretching a single bonded gage R₁ by +ΔR moves the voltage at D from zero to +ΔV and drives a current through C; if R₄ is also a bonded gage changing by −ΔR at the same time, D moves to +2ΔV; and if R₂ changes by −ΔR and R₃ by +ΔR, C moves to −2ΔV and the difference between C and D becomes 4ΔV. A single gage works, but sensitivity rises fourfold when two gages are in tension while two others are in compression.

Beam-type load cells suit low-level loads. Ring-Type Load Cell Load cell kiểu vòng

Ring-type load cells use a proving ring as the elastic element. Varying the diameter D, the thickness t or the depth w covers a very wide load range, and either strain gages or a linear variable-differential transformer can serve as the sensor. The load is linearly proportional to the output voltage; with an LVDT the sensitivity depends on the ring geometry (R, t and w), on the ring material (E) and on the LVDT characteristics (S and E_i), while the range is set by the strength of the ring material.

Piezoelectric Methods Phương pháp áp điện

In a piezoelectric material, deforming an asymmetrical elastic crystal develops a potential within the distorted lattice, and the effect is reversible: a potential applied across the crystal changes its dimensions. Such elements are sometimes called electrorestrictive. The magnitude and polarity of the induced surface charge are proportional to the magnitude and direction of the applied force, with d the charge sensitivity in C/N (Equation 23.12). The force also produces a thickness change Δt (Equation 23.13) in terms of the crystal area a, thickness t and Young's modulus Y, which is just the elastic definition of Y (Equation 23.14). The charge at the electrodes gives a voltage E₀ = Q/C, with C = εa/t for absolute permittivity ε (Equation 23.15), so the voltage sensitivity g = d/ε in volt metres per newton yields Equation 23.16.

The materials used are quartz, tourmaline, Rochelle salt, ammonium dihydrogen phosphate, lithium sulphate, barium titanate and lead zirconate titanate. Quartz and other natural piezoelectric crystals are naturally polarised, whereas synthetic materials such as barium titanate ceramic are made by baking small crystallites under pressure and then placing the result in a strong dc field, after which the crystal is polarised along the axis on which force will be applied. Artificial elements escape the limits of crystal structure, can be moulded to any size and shape, and have their polarisation direction fixed during production. Figure 23.8 shows the modes of a simple plate and Figure 23.9 how two crystals bonded with perpendicular electrical axes respond to bending or torque. Piezoelectric transducers cover 1 to 200 kN.

Piezoelectric crystals also measure instantaneous change of force. A thin quartz plate used as an electronic oscillator has a frequency dominated by its natural frequency, and any distortion of the plate by an external force alters that frequency — so a dynamic force can be read from the frequency shift.

Resistive Method Phương pháp điện trở

The resistive method exploits the change of total resistance when the multiple contact area between semiconducting particles, usually carbon, and their separation change. Such transducers give a very small displacement under load: two to sixty thin carbon disks between a fixed and a movable electrode, moving together by 5 to 250 µm per interface, produce a resistance-versus-force transfer function that is roughly hyperbolic and therefore highly nonlinear, with large hysteresis and drift and high transverse sensitivity. Replacing the disks with prestressed rings on an insulated rigid core almost eliminates transverse sensitivity and raises the resonant frequency to as much as 10 kHz over a 0.1 to 10 kg range, though accuracy and linearity remain poor.

Inductive Method Phương pháp cảm ứng

The inductive method uses the change of permeability that follows a change of mechanical stress in a ferromagnetic material — the Villari effect, or magnetostriction, particularly strong in nickel–iron alloys. A coil wound on a magnetostrictive core is stressed by the force to be measured, changing the core permeability and inductance in a way that can be monitored. The applicable range is a function of core cross-section and accuracy is set by calibration; linearity is poor and hysteresis present. Permeability rises under pure torsion regardless of direction, and the frequency response is flat from 150 Hz to 15,000 Hz.

Piezotransistor Method Phương pháp piezotransistor

Devices using anisotropic stress effects are called piezotransistors: a localised stress on the upper surface of a p–n diode produces a significant reversible change in junction current. They are usually silicon non-planar transistors with an emitter–base junction mechanically connected to a diaphragm on the upper surface of a TO-type can; pressure or force on the diaphragm produces a charge. Constant temperature is advisable because semiconductor properties vary with temperature, and the attraction of the device is that it survives a 500% overload.

Multicomponent Dynamometers Using Quartz Crystals Dynamometer đa thành phần dùng tinh thể thạch anh

Force measurement uses the direct piezoelectric effect: the direct longitudinal effect measures compressive force and the direct shear effect measures shear force in one direction. A quartz disk cut normal to the crystallographic x-axis and loaded in compression yields a charge of nominally 2.26 pC/N; one cut normal to the y-axis yields 4.52 pC/N when loaded in shear in one specific direction, and forces in other directions generate no output. A charge amplifier converts the charge to a proportional voltage, its range set by a feedback capacitor, with additional variable-gain operational amplifiers scaling to mechanical units.

The building block of a multicomponent dynamometer is the three-component force transducer of Figure 23.10: a pair of X-cut quartz disks for the normal component and a pair of Y-cut, shear-sensitive disks for each shear component. Four such transducers sandwiched between a base plate and a top plate measure cutting forces in machining. They carry a preload because shear is transmitted by friction, and each sees a drastic change of load depending on the type and position of force application: an overhanging introduction of force develops tension in some transducers and so reduces the preload, while bending of the top plate adds bending and shearing stresses. Measuring ranges therefore depend not only on the individual forces but on the individual bending stresses.

Figure 23.11 shows the rotating cutting force dynamometer. A ring-shaped sensor (1) sits in a steep-angle taper socket (2) and a base ring (3), sensing the three force components at the cutting edge and the moment Mz. The quartz plates are aligned so that maximum cross-sensitivity between components is 1%; the rigid design puts the resonant frequencies between 1200 and 3000 Hz over ranges up to 10 kN. Four miniature hybrid charge amplifiers (7) convert the charges to voltages, which are filtered against aliasing and digitised at 8-bit resolution with a high sampling rate. A telemetry unit — receiver and transmitter module, an antenna at the top of the rotating system (8) and a fixed antenna (9) on the splash cover of the two-axis milling head (10) — carries the signals out, modulated on an FM carrier, to an external conditioning unit.

Any mechanical system can be treated to first approximation as a weakly damped oscillator of a spring and a mass; where it has more than one resonant frequency the lowest matters. As long as the test frequency stays below 10% of the resonant frequency of the reference transducer used for calibration, the dynamic sensitivity differs from the static calibration by less than 1%, assuming a sinusoidal force signal. A static calibration of the reference transducer remains valid for dynamic work when the test frequency is at least ten times lower than the system's resonant frequency.

Capacitive Force Transducer Đầu đo lực kiểu điện dung

A capacitive transducer measures force by directing it onto a membrane whose elastic deflection is detected as a change of capacitance; because capacitive sensing resolves very small deflections accurately, a highly sensitive force transducer is possible, with an electronic circuit converting capacitance change into dc voltage change. The sensor of Figure 23.12 is two metal plates separated by an air gap, with capacitance given by Equation 23.17 in terms of the permittivity of free space, the relative permittivity of the insulator, the overlapping area and the gap thickness. Sensitivity is inherently low: theory says a smaller gap h raises it, but practical electrical and mechanical conditions prevent high sensitivity. The main advantages are that moving one plate relative to the other needs an extremely small force, and that stability is good — sensitivity is not influenced by ambient pressure or temperature.

Force Sensing Resistors (Conductive Polymers) Điện trở cảm nhận lực (polymer dẫn điện)

Force sensing resistors exploit the fact that certain polymer thick-film devices decrease in resistance as applied force increases. One part is a resistive material applied to a film, the other a set of interdigitated contacts on a second film; the resistive material completes the circuit between the two sets of conductors, and force improves the connection so conductivity rises. Over a wide range the conductivity is approximately linear in force. There are three operating regions, with the first abrupt transition near 10 g of force, where resistance changes very rapidly — useful when designing switches. FSRs should not be used for accurate force measurement, since parts may vary 15% to 25% in resistance from one to another, but they show little hysteresis, cost far less than other sensing devices, and are far less sensitive to vibration and heat than piezofilm.

Magnetoresistive Force Sensors Cảm biến lực từ điện trở

Magnetoresistive sensors rest on the change of resistivity that metals show in an applied magnetic field when cooled to low temperature, bismuth being particularly sensitive. In practice they are severely limited by their high sensitivity to ambient temperature change.

Magnetoelastic Force Sensors Cảm biến lực từ đàn hồi

Magnetoelastic transducers work on the Joule effect, a ferromagnetic material changing dimension in a magnetic field. A current pulse in the conductor within the waveguide sets up a circumferential field along its whole length; the permanent magnet adds a field with a longitudinal component only where the magnet sits. The two combine vectorially into a helical field near the magnet, which twists the waveguide minutely at that point alone — the Wiedemann effect. Frequency response is high, of the order of 20 kHz, and materials showing the effect include Monel, Permalloy, Cekas, Alfer and a number of nickel–iron alloys. The drawbacks are that excessive stress and ageing may cause permanent change, that zero drift and sensitivity change follow from temperature sensitivity, and that hysteresis errors occur.

Torsional Balances Cân xoắn

Balancing devices using the deflection of a spring can also determine force. Torsional balances are equal-arm scale devices built with horizontal steel bands instead of pivots and bearings: a force applied to one arm deflects the torsional spring within its design limits in proportion to the applied force. Susceptible to hysteresis and temperature error, they are not used for precise measurement.

Tactile Sensors Cảm biến xúc giác

Tactile sensors are usually understood as a touch-sensing technique, but they are not simple touch sensors making a few discrete force measurements: in tactile sensing a force distribution is measured with a closely spaced array of force sensors. Tactile sensing matters both for grasping and for object identification. Grasping must be stable so the object neither slips nor is damaged, and identification covers shape, location and orientation as well as surface properties and defects. Ideally the task needs two kinds of sensing — continuous sensing of contact forces and sensing of the surface deformation profile — related through the stress–strain relations of the sensor, which yields almost continuous variable sensing of tactile forces.

Tactile Sensor Requirements Yêu cầu đối với cảm biến xúc giác

Tactile sensing is advancing rapidly in robotics, for automated inspection of surface profiles, material handling and parts transfer, assembly, and parts identification and gaging, as well as fine manipulation; some of these need only simple touch or force–torque sensing where parts are properly oriented and process information is already available. The design objective has been to mimic the human finger. Typical specifications for an industrial tactile sensor are a spatial resolution of about 2 mm, force resolution of about 2 g, maximum touch force of about 1 kg, response time of 5 ms, low hysteresis, durability under extremely difficult working conditions, insensitivity to temperature, dust, humidity and vibration, and the ability to monitor slip.

A typical array has several sensing elements, each called a taxel (Figure 23.16). Because tactile sensors are deployed where a semblance of human touch is wanted, an elastomer mimics human skin — generally a conductive material whose conductivity changes locally under pressure. The sensor has three layers: a protective covering, a sheet of conductive elastomer, and a printed circuit board. The board carries two rows of two bullseyes, each with conductive inner and outer rings forming the taxels; outer rings are connected together and to a column-select transistor, inner rings to the diodes D of Figure 23.16. Once the column is selected, current flows through the diodes, through the elastomer and thence through a transistor to ground. Excitation of a single taxel is generally impossible because applied pressure deforms neighbouring taxels too; this crosstalk is eliminated by the diodes.

Array signals provide information about contact kinematics — contact location, object shape and pressure distribution. Figure 23.17 shows the general layout of an intelligent sensor array system, exemplified by a contact and force sensing finger: four contact sensors of piezoelectric polymer strips on the fingertip surface supply dynamic contact information, while a strain gage force sensor supplies static grasp force information.
