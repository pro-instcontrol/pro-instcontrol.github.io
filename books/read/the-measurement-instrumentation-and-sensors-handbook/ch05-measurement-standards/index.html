Measurement Standards

DeWayne B. Sharp · Shape of Things

Chapter 5 · 3 figures, 2 tables

This chapter is a companion to the Vietnamese translation of Chapter 5. It carries the three figures redrawn as vectors, a map of the argument, and the glossary index; the prose — a history of standards from body measures to the caesium second, the four kinds of standards, the traceability hierarchy, and the VIM definitions — is in the translation itself.

Figure 5.1 · Who writes which standards

Sharp opens §5.2 by warning that “standard” names several different objects, and the first figure is the org chart that keeps them apart: treaty-level bodies (CGPM, CIPM, BIPM) at the top for the units themselves, ISO/IEC/ANSI/SCC for standards of practice, OIML for legal metrology, and Interpol and the FBI for forensic metrology.

Figure 5.2 · The quality chain

The Lord Kelvin quotation — “when you can measure what you are speaking about… you know something about it” — is old enough to be trite, so §5.3 turns it into a chain of dependencies: quality needs decisions, decisions need numerical data, data needs measurements, measurements need calibrated instruments, and instruments that can be compared across time and place need traceable standards. Break any link and the whole argument collapses, which is why the chapter treats traceability as the load-bearing element rather than a formality.

Figure 5.3 · The measurement assurance system

The third figure is the one worth studying closely, because it is where the abstract hierarchy becomes an institution. The US branch runs from the President through the Department of Commerce to NIST and down to working standards and instruments; the treaty branch runs from the Treaty of the Meter through CIPM and its consultative committees (CCE for electricity, CCT for thermometry) to the BIPM; other nations plug in through their national laboratories. Every arrow in the diagram is a comparison with a stated uncertainty — that is what makes the chain a chain and not just a list of organisations.

The four kinds of standards

KindWhat it fixesWho controls it
Measurement standardThe size of a unit — the metre, the kilogram, the voltCGPM / CIPM / BIPM, realised by NIST and the other national laboratories
Standard of practice (protocol)Dimensions, interfaces, procedures — a battery can, a screw thread, QA requirements for measuring equipmentISO, IEC, ANSI, SCC
Legal metrologyThe rules of trade measurement — what a gallon or a litre means at the pumpOIML internationally; NIST Office of Weights and Measures, then the states and counties, in the US
Standard reference materialThe composition or property of a substance — ores, alloys, gases, biochemicalsNIST’s SRM Program, over 1,300 items

The seven base units and the one that is still an artifact

§5.5 makes a point that dates the chapter precisely: six of the seven SI base units are defined by their unitary value, but the kilogram is defined as 1,000 g and is “the only unit currently based on an artifact” — a platinum/iridium cylinder at the BIPM. If the International Prototype Kilogram were to change, every mass standard in the world would be wrong. (The 2019 redefinition, which fixed the Planck constant, retired that last artifact; Sharp could not know it when this was written, and the translation keeps his text as it stands.) The metre has already made the same journey: it is now the distance light travels in a stated fraction of a second, so the definition of length is no longer independent of the definition of time.
