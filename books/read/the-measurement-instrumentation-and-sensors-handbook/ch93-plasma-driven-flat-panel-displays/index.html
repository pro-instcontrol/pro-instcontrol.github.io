93.1 An Introduction to Plasma-Driven Flat Panel Displays

Development History and Present Status

Plasma-driven flat panel display pixels were invented by Bitzer and Slottow at the University of Illinois in 1966 [1–3]. Figure 93.1 shows one of the inventors' early designs: parallel sets of thin conducting wires are deposited on two glass substrates, which are then mounted with the conductor sets perpendicular to one another. A spacer — here a perforated glass dielectric — maintains a gap of about 100 µm, and the gap is filled with an inert gas at roughly half an atmosphere. Pixels form where two wires cross, aligned with the perforations, and light when the voltage between the wires is enough to break the gas down. The basic design has been refined endlessly since, but the underlying idea is still in wide use.

Through the 1980s plasma products were monochrome and neon-based, producing the red-orange 585–640 nm emission characteristic of the neon atom directly in the discharge volume. Dot matrix displays of the kind in Figure 93.2 were common [3,4]. Owens-Illinois improved glass sealing and spacer supports [5,6] and IBM improved the understanding and control of the discharge [7–15]; together these made large-area, high-resolution monochrome panels possible. The largest plasma panels ever built were Photonics Imaging's 1 m diagonal monochrome displays: over two million pixels at 20 pixels/cm (50 lines/in.) [4].

Lithography, patterning and phosphor work then drove colour. Table 93.1 collects panel specifications and manufacturing plans for the major investors as of January 1997 [16]. Stanford Research put 1995 plasma panel sales at $230 million and projected $4.1 billion for 2002; NEC projected $2.0 billion by 2000 and $7.0 billion by 2002 [17]. Capacity was expected to reach nearly 40,000 units per month by the end of 1998 and over 100,000 by early 2000. Fujitsu shipped the first high-information-content full-colour panel in 1993 — 21 in. diagonal, ac-driven, 640 × 480 [17,18]. Figures 93.3 and 93.4 show two later entries.

dc and ac Plasma Pixels

Plasma pixels can be built for dc or ac operation; Figure 93.5 shows the two simplest designs. In both, parallel conductor sets sit on glass substrates — usually ordinary soda-lime float glass, to keep cost down — mounted about 100 µm apart with the sets perpendicular. The gap holds an inert gas that discharges and lights the pixel when enough voltage appears across two crossing wires.

In a dc pixel (Figure 93.5a) the gas touches the electrodes. At 53.3 kPa, a 100 µm gap and 200 V, electrons cross the gap in about 0.2 ns and ions in about 20 ns. Once breakdown starts the discharge resistance is negligible, so dc operation requires an external series resistor at every pixel to limit current. dc pixels are usually run in pulsed mode with frequency modulation setting brightness, at a base frequency of 50 kHz — too fast for the eye to see flicker, slow enough to leave room for intensity and refresh control. dc and ac pixels are easy to confuse in the literature, because dc pixels are often pulsed and often use polarity reversal to spread sputter damage over both electrodes; the reliable markers of a dc pixel are electrodes in direct contact with the gas and a current-limiting resistor in each pixel circuit.

Figure 93.5b shows an ac pixel. The geometry differs only slightly; the operation differs a great deal. The conductors are covered with a dielectric — typically lead oxide (PbO, ε ≈ 15) about 25 µm thick, with a 50–200 nm magnesium oxide (MgO) film on top, in contact with the gas. That dual film does two jobs: charge storage and secondary electron emission. Breakdown needs about 120–180 V for the parameters shown.

In an ac pixel the carriers cannot reach the conductors; they collect as surface charge on the dielectric. The field in the gap is always the sum of the applied field and the field of that surface charge, and the second cancels the first within 100–200 ns, so the pixel turns itself off and needs no series resistor. At the next half cycle the applied voltage reverses and the gap sees the sum of the new applied voltage and the wall voltage left by the previous discharge — enough to fire again even though the applied voltage alone would not break the gas down. That stored charge is the pixel's memory: its presence or absence decides whether the pixel fires at the next half cycle.

General Attributes of Plasma Displays

The strongly nonlinear pixel characteristic, with inherent memory, simplifies the drive electronics needed to refresh and update the array. The simple pixel makes large-area manufacturing problems — alignment, film-thickness uniformity — more tractable, and against colour AMLCDs, which need a thin-film transistor at every pixel, plasma wins on manufacturing and drive simplicity for large areas. It loses on drive voltage (100–275 V) and on power: plasma is a poor fit for portable products, though not restrictive for office or domestic use — Photonics Imaging's 30 in. panel draws only 300 W peak [23]. At high power plasma panels are bright enough to read in sunlight, and dim easily by amplitude or frequency modulation.

Plasma suits 0.5–5 m applications — video conferencing, large meeting-room displays, outdoor signage, wide-view simulators — and thin high-resolution colour panels are attractive for high-resolution desktop graphics. Viewing angles exceed 160° in many designs [22–24]; with metal electrodes the best angle is slightly off normal, because the front wire shadows part of the emission. That holds both for monochrome pixels emitting in the discharge and for colour panels where the viewer sees red, green and blue from VUV-stimulated phosphors. Transparent electrodes such as ITO have been tried, at a cost in power because ITO conducts worse than metal [18]; in current designs the metal line is only about 20 µm wide, so its opacity is not a major problem.

For colour pixels the gas mixture is changed to emit in the VUV; everything else about the discharge is the same. Ideally nothing visible is emitted in the discharge itself and VUV-stimulated phosphors supply the RGB. Figure 93.6 shows the ac colour pixel used by Fujitsu [18]: straight barrier structures about 100 µm tall run parallel to and between the vertical conductors on the rear plate, their sidewalls coated alternately with red, green and blue phosphor. Fujitsu uses a three-electrode surface-discharge design, slightly more complicated than the opposed-electrode cell of Figure 93.5b; the chapter returns to it after the discharge physics.

93.2 Fundamentals of Plasma Pixel Operation

Atomic Physics Processes

The pixel is a rich environment for atomic physics, electron collisions, photon production and transport, and plasma–surface interaction. Weber's diagram of these processes for a neon–argon pixel is reproduced as Figure 93.7 [4]; standard texts cover the underlying physics [25–27]. The processes form a closed loop, and the discussion starts from a single seed electron in the gap, accelerated by the field of the applied voltage. Seed particles — electrons, electron-producing photons, or metastable atoms — are usually supplied from a controlled source that may be outside the pixel being fired: electrodes at the panel edge, hidden behind opaque wires, or neighbour pixels primed by careful timing [4,19,28].

Electron transport is a balance between field acceleration and collisional loss. In Figure 93.7 the gas is 98–99.9% neon, so electrons collide mostly with Ne. The lowest excited state is 16.6 eV above ground and the ionisation energy is 21.6 eV: electrons below 16.6 eV can only scatter elastically, above 16.6 eV they can excite an outer-shell electron, and above 21.6 eV they can ionise:

(93.1)Ne + e– → Ne+ + 2e–

Excitation and ionisation move energy from the electron population to the neutral gas, and every ionisation adds an electron able to ionise again: the avalanche of Figure 93.7, seen experimentally as a rapid rise in gap current. dc panels put about 500 kΩ in series with each pixel, so with Va fixed the gap voltage Vg = Va − IR falls as the current rises, and a steady state establishes quickly; brightness and grey scale come from frequency modulation of the pulsed discharge at about 50 kHz. In ac pixels the charge that collects on the dielectric shields the gap and the discharge extinguishes itself.

Collisions above 16.6 eV also excite outer-shell electrons into the many states of Figure 93.7:

(93.2a)Ne + e– → Ne*x + e–
(93.2b)Ne*x → Ne + hν

Most of those states live for fractions of a nanosecond to tens of nanoseconds [30] and decay with the emission of a characteristic photon, hν in Eq. 93.2. The red-orange neon emission comes from 2p → 1s transitions [30,31]; two of the four 1s levels radiate to ground at 74.4 and 73.6 nm, while decay from the other two is hindered by selection rules, giving the metastable states lifetimes measured in seconds [31]. A metastable Ne atom carries 16.6 eV of latent energy that it cannot shed against ground-state neon but readily releases whenever a lower configuration is available — chiefly in collisions with argon, or at the pixel walls.

Figure 93.8 compares the level structures of the inert gases, which is what makes the energy exchange legible. Argon's ionisation energy, 15.8 eV, sits 0.8 eV below the neon metastable, so the metastable can ionise it:

(93.3)Ne* + Ar → Ne + Ar+ + e–

This is a Penning reaction, and a mixture that relies on the metastables of the majority species to ionise the minority is a Penning mixture [25,26,32]. Figure 93.9 shows how efficiently charge pairs are produced in Ne/Ar Penning gas at various argon fractions: ion pairs per volt are best at low argon fractions (0–10%) except at very large E/P, above 75 V/m/Pa (100 V/cm/torr). The data are Kruithof and Penning's original 1937 measurements [32].

Pixels run near 53.3 kPa (400 torr) for enough photon production, with fields around 100 MV/m, so E/P ≈ 18.8 V/m/Pa (25 V/cm/torr) and both charge-pair production and luminous efficiency are optimised at 0.1–10% argon, depending on pressure, gap and drive voltage. For a given voltage the product Pd measures the balance between field acceleration and collisional loss, which is exactly what the Paschen curves of Figure 93.10 plot [26,33,45]. Each gas has its minimum at its own Pd, set by its ionisation levels and energy channels: in neon, excitation and ionisation dominate; in air, much of the energy goes into vibrational, rotational and dissociative modes that do not ionise.

At fixed pressure a wider gap lowers the field per volt and needs more voltage; a narrower gap gives a strong field but too few collisions to build an avalanche. At fixed gap, high pressure shortens the mean free path so electrons cannot gain ionising energy unless the field is very strong, and low pressure gives too few collisions. Penning processes are especially efficient: 0.1% argon in neon drops the minimum from about 250 V to about 150 V, at Pd = 40 Pa·m (30 torr·cm).

Discharge Physics for Plasma Pixels

Electrons move far faster than ions. In a neutral plasma under an applied field the electrons rush to the anode, the ions follow slowly, and a local charge imbalance builds up because electrons arrive faster than ions. Poisson's equation

(93.4)∇·E(x) = 4πρ(x) = 4πe[ni(x) − ne(x)]

shows the local field that answers the net positive charge density ρ(x), with ni and ne the ion and electron profiles and e the electron charge. That field slows the electrons leaving any volume and forces them to follow the ion motion; the ions are accelerated in turn, by a factor set by the electron-to-ion mass ratio. The combined motion is ambipolar flow [25–27].

In a steady dc discharge the current is set by the applied voltage and the series resistor, and no net charge can accumulate in the gap: pairs arrive at the electrodes as fast as ionisation creates them, and ions and electrons leave at equal rates. The equilibrium is carried by the potential profile of Figure 93.11a. Because electrons shield so well, the potential is flat across the gap and the whole drop sits in a thin sheath at each electrode. The Debye length,

(93.5)λD = √[kTe / 4πe2ne(x)]

measures that shielding distance: the sheath thickens with electron temperature and thins with density. With kTe of a few electron volts and ne of order 1016/m³ the sheath is about 5 µm. The plasma potential rises to just above the applied voltage at the anode, so only the most energetic electrons surmount the anode barrier, which adjusts until electron loss at the anode equals ion loss at the cathode. An ac pixel has a similar profile, but it evolves as the pulse proceeds: charge arriving at the dielectric is trapped, the boundary potential falls (Figure 93.11b), and the discharge ends when the wall field cancels the applied field. As the carrier density falls near the end of the pulse the shielding weakens: the sheath drop becomes small but the sheath fills much of the gap [36,37].

Plasma Surface Interactions

Ion-induced secondary electron emission.

Ions crossing the cathode sheath strike the surface with kinetic energy equal to the plasma potential — just over 200 V in the example of Figure 93.12a — and capture an electron, depositing the recombination energy as well. That energy drives two processes that matter here: secondary electron emission, which raises luminous efficiency, and sputtering, which shortens life. Secondary electrons are excellent at driving ionisation: they gain a lot of energy crossing the sheath and then have the whole gap in which to ionise. The coefficient γ is the number of electrons ejected per incident ion [25,26], and it varies with ion energy and cathode material. ac panels exploit MgO, which is also a good insulator, as charge storage requires. Measuring γ for MgO is hard, especially below 500 eV, and sample charging complicates it [38]; relative yields are usually deduced from discharge intensity [11,12,39–42]. Chou, using a pulsed ion beam with surface neutralisation between pulses, found γ = 0.45 for Ne⁺ and 0.05 for Ar⁺ at 200 eV [39]. Photons and metastables also eject electrons (Figure 93.7); being insensitive to the field, they propagate isotropically and often serve as the seed population.

Sputtering.

Ions accelerated across the sheath deposit energy at the cathode and eject surface atoms. Sputtering erodes the cathode and degrades the pixel: sputtered impurities contaminate the discharge and emit visible light that spoils colour purity, and redeposited films degrade the electrical behaviour or, in colour panels, shield the phosphor from VUV. For 2 keV Ar⁺ on MgO the measured yield is slightly above one atom per ion [43]; low-energy data are hard to obtain because the yields are small, the currents must be large and the samples charge. Despite the gap in the data, ac panel makers demonstrate lifetimes well beyond 10,000 h [18,23]. Shone et al. [44] used Rutherford backscattering of 2.8 MeV alpha particles to measure MgO thickness on PbO to ±1.5 nm — an excellent research tool, but it needs a large accelerator and is unsuited to a fabrication line.

93.3 Pixel Electrical Properties

Electrical Properties of dc Pixels

The dc gap behaves as a variable resistor: open circuit before breakdown, then a good conductor with only a small resistance RP. Since R ≫ RP,

(93.6)Va = I(R + RP) ≈ IR

so the current is set by the applied amplitude and the series resistor. The drive is typically a 50 kHz square wave with a ~50 ns rise, 175–275 V, R = 500 kΩ, giving 0.35–0.55 mA. Without a large series resistance the current is limited only by some physical failure such as melting the electrodes.

Figure 93.12 shows the characteristic of a pixel that breaks down at 250 V [4]. Below breakdown only leakage flows and the pixel is off; at breakdown the situation is dynamic, current rising and gap voltage falling until the load line Va = IR intersects the discharge characteristic. At 175 V the pixel is always off, at 275 V always on, and at 225 V with 500 kΩ the two stable states are both reachable: approaching 225 V from below leaves it off, dropping from a high voltage leaves it on. The intersection with the negative-resistance region is unstable and the pixel moves to one of the two stable points. That negative-resistance region cannot be measured through 500 kΩ; the figure uses a 5 MΩ line to reach it.

Electrical Properties of ac Pixels

The ac pixel is a breakdown capacitor:

(93.7)Va(t) = I(t)R + (1/C)∫0tI(t′)dt′ = I(t)R + Q(t)/C

with Va the applied voltage, I the current, C the pixel capacitance and Q the collected charge. R is minimised to save power, so

(93.8)Va(t) = (1/C)∫0tI(t′)dt′ = Q(t)/C

The capacitance is the series combination of the two dielectric films and the gas gap:

(93.9)1/C = 1/CPbO + 1/CMgO + 1/Cgas

with, for each layer,

(93.10)Ci = εiA / di

where A is roughly the square of the conductor width. A typical pixel has 25 µm of PbO (ε ≈ 15ε0) and 50–200 nm of MgO (ε ≈ 6ε0, depending on purity and microstructure [45]). MgO contributes negligibly to the capacitance; it is there for its secondary emission. Before breakdown the capacitance is essentially that of the gap — about 500 pF for 20 µm conductors. Differentiating Eq. 93.8 gives the current:

(93.11)I(t) = C dV(t)/dt

That displacement current is the first, large peak in Figure 93.13, which shows the current response of a 45 × 45 ac array to one pulse of a 50 kHz train. The measurement used an induction loop for current and a high-impedance probe (1 MΩ, 3 pF) for voltage, captured on a 300 MHz oscilloscope.

Below the breakdown threshold only the displacement current of Eq. 93.11 appears (Figure 93.13a). Above it a second pulse — the discharge current — appears as well (Figure 93.13b), accompanied by strong photon emission. Figure 93.14 plots total charge displacement against applied amplitude for a helium–xenon (2%) Penning mixture [35], and the hysteresis is the memory: nothing flows from 0 to 180 V with no wall charge present; above 180 V the pixel fires; a single pulse below 135 V turns it off even with wall charge present.

Charge pairs from one pulse collect on the dielectric and help form the next. In a fully developed discharge the wall charge cancels the applied field, as Figure 93.15 shows — a composite of measured current and computed surface charge [34,36]. When the applied voltage reverses, wall and applied voltages add, and the gap briefly sees about twice the applied voltage. That is what produces the bistable behaviour of Figure 93.14.

Addressing follows directly. Each pixel is continuously supplied with a 160 V square wave — the sustain voltage. A pixel that is off stays off, since there is no wall charge to reinforce the sustain field. A single 200 V address pulse fires it, and the charge collected on the dielectric then shields the gap completely. At the next sustain reversal the gap sees 160 V of sustain plus 200 V of wall voltage: 360 V, far more than enough to fire again and leave a wall charge of the opposite polarity, this time exactly cancelling the applied field at 160 V. The next reversal gives 160 + 160 = 320 V and the pixel keeps firing. To stop it, a single low-voltage erase pulse — 120 V in this example — produces 120 + 160 = 280 V across the gap, a weaker discharge that does not re-establish a reversed wall charge, so the following sustain pulse finds too little charge to fire. Ideally the erase drives the wall charge exactly to zero; in practice it rarely does, and Figure 93.15 shows the residue being cleaned up by very weak, essentially dark discharges over a few cycles — which does not disturb subsequent write pulses in frequency-modulated operation.

93.4 Display Priming, Addressing, Refresh, and Gray Scale

Priming supplies the first electrons. Metastables and photons work too, since they ionise the background gas. Pilot-cell priming puts a separate electron-generating cell near the pixel to be addressed, often at the panel periphery but able to seed the whole display; self-priming creates an auxiliary discharge inside each pixel, usually hidden behind an opaque conductor. A trace of radioactive Kr85 in the gas is a passive option: its beta radiation seeds electrons uniformly, the concentration needed is low, and the betas cannot penetrate the glass, so user exposure is negligible — but handling radioactive material is a manufacturing hazard, so the technique is not used commercially.

Figure 93.2 shows the simplest scan: switches on one axis close in turn while data switches on the other decide which pixels fire. It wastes the properties that make plasma attractive, and a high-resolution display built this way would not be cost-competitive, because drivers are a large share of panel cost and driver cost rises with output voltage. The design aim is therefore the lowest possible operating voltage and the fewest driver chips, hence the most pixels per chip.

For non-memory dc pixels one way to cut switch count is to sweep priming discharges along each row, as in Burroughs' self-scan circuitry [46,47]. NHK's pulse-memory scheme for its 40 in. dc HDTV panel is now widely used [48]: high-frequency sustain exploits the residual charge pairs and metastables left by the previous discharge, so each pixel self-seeds with a population that depends on the time since the last discharge. Once the sustain train is applied the pixel holds its state until an address or erase pulse arrives. NHK uses an auxiliary anode to help the address access; Figure 93.16a is the block diagram and 93.16b the timing [48]. The pulses are dc, on-state pulses have larger gap voltages than erase pulses, and the timing is critical. Implementing the scheme needs display anode drivers, auxiliary anode drivers, cathode drivers, and an interface to decode the HDTV signal.

For ac panels the drivers need only supply address (write), sustain and erase pulses: no complex refresh scan, because a continuous sustain signal plus the pixel's own memory holds each pixel on or off. Addressing still requires well-timed waveforms on rows and columns so that the right voltage appears across the selected pixel without disturbing its neighbours; Reference 4 or 47 gives the detail.

Grey scale comes from modulating the discharge current or, more practically, the duty cycle at fixed current. Amplitude modulation is little used because the discharge current is hard to control linearly; duty-cycle modulation works for both pulse-memory dc and ac panels but needs careful timing. Brightness is set by the fraction of each refresh cycle the pixel is on. At 50 kHz a sustain half cycle is 10 µs and the VUV emission lasts under 1 µs, while visible emission persists much longer: the fastest phosphors reach 10% in about 5 ms, typical ones in 5–10 ms [50]. At a 40 ms frame (25 images/s), one-eighth brightness means 5 ms on and 35 ms off, spread through the frame by the timing circuit. NHK's 40 in. panel has 28 = 256 levels per colour, 16 million (256³) in total [48].

93.5 Color Plasma Flat Panel Displays

Color Pixel Structures

In a colour panel, photoluminescent phosphors supply the RGB primaries, so visible emission from the discharge itself must be suppressed. The usual approach is xenon as the minority species in the Penning mixture. Figure 93.17 shows NHK's 40 in. dc colour panel and Figure 93.6 Fujitsu's 21 in. ac panel; both use screen printing plus hard-mask or abrasive-resistant lithography for conductors, barriers and phosphors [51]. In NHK's honeycomb the fourth cell of each group carries a redundant green subpixel, compensating for the weaker green phosphor; Panasonic's honeycomb dc design puts a series resistor there instead [20], which complicates panel manufacture but simplifies the drive. In the Fujitsu opposed-electrode ac design the barrier ribs run between and parallel to the conductors on the rear plate, 100–150 µm tall, and both rib structures and honeycomb lattices are made from the same PbO thick-film dielectric that covers the conductors.

VUV Photon Production and Utilization

Neon–xenon and helium–xenon mixtures are both popular. Xenon's 12.3 eV ionisation energy lies below the lowest excited states of neon and helium (Figure 93.8), so accelerated electrons preferentially give their energy to xenon, and excited He or Ne transfers energy to xenon by Penning collisions. The neon red-orange emission is therefore suppressed as xenon rises: Fujitsu uses Ne–10% Xe [18], Photonics prefers a helium background where suppression needs somewhat less xenon [23].

Xenon's tendency to fill its outer shell forms the dimer Xe₂*, whose states also appear in Figure 93.8 [52,53]; its radiative dissociation gives photons near 173 and 150 nm. Figure 93.18 shows the dimer dominating the VUV spectrum as xenon rises. Because glass absorbs VUV completely, the spectra were taken with opposed-electrode pixels inside a vacuum chamber filled with the mixture, the panel edges unsealed so the discharge could be viewed edge-on through a McPherson 0.2 m monochromator and a Princeton CCD multichannel analyser [34]; total pressure stayed at 53.3 kPa (400 torr). At low xenon the atomic 1s₄ and 1s₅ lines dominate at 147 nm and, much more weakly, 129 nm; Tachibana's laser-induced measurements follow the 1s₄ state in space and time [54]. Both lines suffer strong resonance absorption and re-emission, so measured intensities depend on path length and xenon partial pressure [55]. For Figure 93.18 the LiF entrance window sat 100–150 nm from the nearest discharges, roughly where the phosphor sits in an opposed-electrode ac colour pixel.

Optimum charge-pair production per volt occurs at minority fractions as low as 0.1% (Figure 93.9), but colour pixels must optimise usable photons per watt while holding colour purity, so they run at 2–10% xenon. Figure 93.18 shows the consequence: more dimer, less atomic emission as energy flows to the lowest levels. Dimer dissociation is a three-body process, so momentum and energy conservation admit a continuum of solutions and the emission spans tens of nanometres. Dimer lines are not resonance-absorbed, so the measured intensity comes from every row in the spectrometer's line of sight — four rows for these data — whereas over 90% of the 147 nm intensity originates in the row nearest the window [34,55].

Color Plasma Display Lifetime Considerations

Phosphors must survive the discharge environment, and the degradation mechanisms are not well understood. Contamination of the discharge by phosphor material is a serious concern; modelling points to accumulated photon and metastable fluence as the main damage channel, with fringe fields and prolonged wall charge possibly adding ion bombardment [37]. Most ac panels coat the dielectric over the electrodes with MgO to boost the discharge, deposited by electron-beam evaporation as one of the last steps before sealing and gas fill. Without a mask the MgO also covers the phosphor: it protects the phosphor but attenuates the VUV reaching it. Figure 93.20 gives the transmission [34,60]: the 129 and 147 nm xenon lines lose nearly 95% through only 75 nm of MgO, while the dimer band near 173 nm keeps 30–50% through 200 and 75 nm films. Designers therefore balance discharge enhancement from MgO secondary emission, discharge loss from MgO absorption, and the process complexity — and hence the cost — of depositing it, against the ageing that MgO sputter erosion partly controls.

93.6 Inspection and Metrology

Panel manufacture borrows process steps from semiconductor manufacturing, but at much larger feature sizes — and over much larger distances across which integrity must hold. Knowing that the process is in control, or characterising the deviation from acceptable limits quickly, is essential to yield. Information is needed at several length scales: blanket depositions must be uniform from point to distant point, roughness must stay within limits, and composition and its spatial homogeneity must be measured.

The three commercial profilers — CyberOptics, Zygo and Leitz — use laser or white-light triangulation and interferometry (Table 93.3) [61–63]. They suit patterned metallisation well, are not useful for opaque blanket layers, and cannot measure blanket metal thickness before patterning. CyberOptics is widely used in electronic packaging for its low-noise signal and would gain from better vertical resolution; Zygo resolves better vertically but has no real scanning; Leitz is built for unfinished LCD components. Both CyberOptics and Zygo offer several heads with different resolutions and fields of view, but Zygo's interference range in depth is limited, which may rule out tall features.

A useful laser system costs as little as $20,000; a full-featured one $100,000, most of it in the precision transport tables that move samples under fixed heads. Since the production line may already have such tables, or need only minor upgrades, the real cost of adding metrology can be far lower — and because heads are cheap, several fixed heads can be used to speed inspection up.

Table 93.4 lists two advanced techniques. Neither was commercially available to the authors' knowledge, but both were in use at Sandia and look promising. Beta backscattering reads elemental composition from the energy spectrum of backscattered betas and has measured trace metal films over square metres in tokamaks [64]; with about 100 pm depth resolution it could underpin a sensitive surface profiler. X-ray fluorescence, routinely done in air, gives thickness and composition, but light elements such as carbon — a possible phosphor binder burnout product — need vacuum because the x-rays are soft. A larger spot is useful where averaged information is wanted. Eddy current measurement may be the fast, reliable way to get blanket opaque metal thickness before patterning, to 10% on layers as thin as 1 µm.

Most quantitative tools have a limited field of view, so techniques are also needed to find visual defects quickly in blanket and patterned layers. Visual inspection equipment exists for LCDs — Minato Electronics, Advantest, ADS, Photon Dynamics and Teradyne all use CCDs and special algorithms for line and area defects — and plasma defects may look similar enough for it to work with adjustment, though such equipment is expensive.

Everyone agrees more development is needed: real parts must be characterised on specific commercial equipment in production before the suitability of these tools can be judged, especially for harder measurements such as dielectric thickness over conductor lines. No single instrument covers all the dimensional and chemical requirements; several will be needed to inspect, characterise and control plasma display manufacturing.
