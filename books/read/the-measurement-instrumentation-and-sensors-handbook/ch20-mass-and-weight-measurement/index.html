Mass and Weight Measurement

Mark Fritz và Emil Hazarian · Denver Instrument Company

Chapter 20 · 3 figures, 19 equations, 12 references

Mass and weight are often used interchangeably but are different. Mass is a quantitative measure of the inertia of a body at rest; as a physical quantity it is the product of density and volume. Weight, or weight force, is the force with which a body is attracted toward the Earth, and equals the product of mass and the acceleration of gravity.

The embodiment of units of mass are called weights, which adds to the confusion. In SI the unit of mass is the kilogram and the unit of force is the newton; in U.S. customary units mass is the slug and force is the pound. Because the pound has been defined in terms of the kilogram since 1893, Americans use the pound to designate mass.

20.1 Weighing Instruments Thiết bị cân

Weighing is one of the oldest measurements, predating written history. The equal-arm balance — two pans suspended equidistant from a central pivot — was probably the first weighing instrument: the standard goes in one pan, the unknown in the other, and the beam is level when they are equal. Figure 20.1 shows an Ainsworth FV series equal-arm balance. A balance determines mass by measuring the force the object exerts on its support in the Earth's gravitational field, so S·G = U·G; because the pans are close together and the arms are equal, G cancels and the standard equals the unknown. Hence the need for a complete set of standards — the balance can only indicate equality, and only weakly how large a difference is.

The single-pan substitution balance was probably the first attempt at direct reading: calibrated weights inside the balance are added to the standard side with dials and levers, and the small residual is read on an optical scale. The spring scale is the least expensive device: the elasticity of the spring is assumed linear, the extension force is marked in mass units, and capacity grows with spring stiffness — but resolution falls as stiffness rises, limiting spring scales to a few kilograms. Spiral and cantilevered springs are both used. The torsion balance is a precise adaptation: the vertical load produces a torque on a band, wire or string, and the angular deflection is proportional to the load, so a calibrated torsion constant lets the deflection be read as mass — down to the microgram region. The beam balance (Figure 20.2) uses the same principle but with one pan and an offset beam; sliding weights gain mechanical advantage as they move outward, and notched positions along the beam mark the force each weight applies. Beam balances span milligram range to truck and train scales, again with resolution falling as load rises.

Next in cost and accuracy is the strain gage load cell: a resistive wire element bonded to a steel cylinder changes resistance as the cylinder shortens or lengthens, and the resistance is converted to a mass readout by the readout electronics. The force restorative load cell (Figure 20.3) is the heart of an electronic balance: the fulcrum is offset, one side carries a coil suspended in a magnetic field and the other side the pan; an LED/photodiode null detector senses departure from equilibrium and the coil current is increased until balance is restored. That current, measured across a precision sense resistor, becomes the displayed mass. Industrial scales, laboratory balances and mass comparators now use electromagnetic force compensation with joint flexures instead of knife edges — computer interfacing and no moving parts. Other methods include the vibrating cord (frequency proportional to tension), the piezoelectric effect, gyroscopic cells, capacitive and inductive converters whose reference distance changes with load, and radioactivity changes as a function of mass.

20.2 Weighing Techniques Kỹ thuật cân

For low accuracy, reading mass or weight directly from the instrument is adequate; except for equal-arm and some torsion balances, most modern instruments read directly. Errors come from instrument imperfections, bias in the standards, the weighing method, the operator and the environment. Mass is the only SI base quantity still defined by a physical artifact — the international prototype of the kilogram kept at Sèvres under the custody of the International Bureau of Weights and Measures — and its value has drifted over the past century by an unknown amount and direction.

Substitution weighing removes most instrument errors. In single substitution a standard and an unknown are both weighed on the same device and only their difference is used: U = S + (O₂ − O₁) × SW/(O₃ − O₂). This is adequate to a few tenths of a gram; beyond that, air buoyancy dominates. True mass is the mass in vacuum; in air every object is lifted by a force equal to the weight of the displaced air, so apparent (conventional) mass is defined at 20 °C and 1.2 mg cm⁻³ with brass (8.4 g cm⁻³) or stainless steel (8.0 g cm⁻³) as reference. NIST reports true mass, apparent mass vs brass and apparent mass vs stainless steel. A chemist weighing 1 L of water illustrates the problem: a 1 kg stainless standard displaces 125 cm³ (150 mg of buoyancy) while the water displaces 1000 cm³ (1200 mg) — a significant uncorrected error. Air density falls with altitude (Denver ≈ 0.98 mg cm⁻³), so it must be measured; Equations 20.5 and 20.6 give ρ_A and e_s, Equations 20.7 and 20.8 give the single-substitution buoyancy correction and the conventional mass, and Equations 20.9 and 20.10 the double-substitution equivalents, which also absorb instrument drift.

For the highest accuracy, advanced designs add redundant weighing, drift compensation, statistical checks and multiple standards. The simplest is the three-in-one design — two standards calibrate one unknown through three double substitutions (standard vs unknown, standard vs check standard, check standard vs unknown), giving twelve readings O₁…O₁₂. Equations 20.11–20.13 give the differences a, b, c; 20.14–20.16 the least-squares difference, mass and conventional mass of the unknown; 20.17–20.18 the same for the check standard, which must fall inside its control limits; and 20.19 the short-term standard deviation, whose ratio to the historical pooled value is the F-statistic, to be compared with the 99% t-value. Private and government primary calibration laboratories use these intercomparisons under the Mass Measurement Assurance Program with NIST's Mass Code; full discussion is in NBS Technical Note 952.
