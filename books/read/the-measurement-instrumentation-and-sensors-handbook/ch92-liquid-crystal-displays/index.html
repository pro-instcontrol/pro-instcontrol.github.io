92.1 Introduction

Liquid crystals (LC) are an important class of materials with applications ranging from display devices, optoelectronic devices and sensors to biological and structural materials. This chapter focuses on LCs for display applications. In general, most substances have a single melting point, where a solid possessing positional and orientational order changes upon melting to an isotropic liquid that has neither. However, some materials, when melted from the solid state, change into a cloudy liquid with orientational order at one temperature and, upon further heating, into an isotropic liquid with no order, as shown in Fig. 92.1.

An LC is therefore a mesophase existing between the melting temperature Tm of a crystalline phase and the clearing point Tc of the liquid phase: below Tm the material is crystalline, above Tc it is a liquid (isotropic), and between the two it is a liquid crystal. An LC whose mesophase is defined by temperature is called thermotropic; when the mesophase is defined by solvent concentration it is called lyotropic. Display LCs are thermotropic. The orientational order of LC materials produces important physical properties, such as birefringence, that make them useful for displays.

Because LCs offer low drive voltage, low power consumption, a thin form factor (flat panels), light weight, full colour, grey scale with a wide dynamic range, full motion video, superior image quality and high reliability, LC displays (LCDs) are the preferred approach for battery-powered (portable) applications ranging from wristwatch displays and handheld TVs to laptop computer displays. They are also replacing cathode ray tubes (CRTs) in select applications such as avionic displays because of their high brightness and readability in sunlight.

LCs are also used in projection displays for head-mounted displays (HMD) and for large-screen applications. The following sections discuss the various types of LC materials, their properties, LCD materials and fabrication processes, the various LCD modes, and display addressing methods. Many good general references exist (for example References 1 through 7). At the time of writing, LCD technology is advancing very rapidly in viewing angle, image quality, power consumption and display size. The purpose of this chapter, however, is to present basic LCD principles and technologies rather than to review the state of the art.

92.2 Types of Liquid Crystal Materials

Most LC materials are organic compounds consisting of rod-shaped or disk-shaped molecules. For displays, rod-shaped LCs are the most commonly used. According to their molecular order, LC materials are broadly classified into three types (phases) — smectic, nematic and cholesteric — as shown in Fig. 92.2. In a smectic liquid crystal the rod-shaped molecules are arranged in layers with the molecules parallel to each other. There are many smectic phases, but smectic A and smectic C are the most common. In smectic A the molecular axis (director) is perpendicular to the layers (Fig. 92.2a); in smectic C it is tilted from the layer normal (Fig. 92.2b).

In a nematic LC the rod-shaped molecules are parallel to each other, but individual molecules move relatively easily along their axis without a layer structure (Fig. 92.2c). In a cholesteric LC the molecules are arranged in layers as in a smectic, but the molecular axis lies in the plane of each layer (Fig. 92.2d); moreover, the cholesteric has a helical structure in which the director n changes from layer to layer. The same material may show different phases at different temperatures — for example smectic C at low temperature, then smectic A, then nematic, before becoming isotropic at Tc.

The nematic LC is the basis of most widely used active-matrix-addressed twisted nematic (TN) LCDs and passive-matrix-addressed supertwisted nematic (STN) LCDs. An example of a smectic C display is the passive-matrix-addressed ferroelectric LCD [8]. An example of a cholesteric display is the passive-matrix-addressed stabilised cholesteric texture (SCT) display with bistability [9]. A classic nematic material is p-azoxyanisole (PAA), nematic from 117 to 136 °C.

In PAA the two benzene rings are nearly coplanar and the rigid rod is about 20 Å long and 5 Å wide. Another historical nematic is N-(p-methoxybenzylidene-p-butylaniline) (MBBA), nematic from 22 to 47 °C.

MBBA has a central group that firmly connects the two ring cores and keeps the whole molecule linear. The terminal groups and conjugated bonds in the core are largely responsible for the dielectric, optical and other anisotropic properties. Azoxy and Schiff's-base compounds were among the earliest LCD materials. Because of environmental stability problems they were replaced by biphenyl materials. More recently phenylcyclohexane, bicyclohexane and estercyclohexane compounds were developed to meet the requirements of broad temperature operation and enhanced electro-optical characteristics. The LCs used in current LCDs are highly developed mixtures tailored for environmental stability, a wide operating temperature range, proper response to the applied field, high resistivity for matrix addressing and fast response.

The degree of order is an important parameter, defined by the order parameter S:

(92.1)S = ½⟨3cos²θ − 1⟩

where θ is the angle between the molecular axis and the predominant molecular orientation n; ⟨ ⟩ denotes an average over all space. The predominant orientation, known as the LC director n, is the average alignment direction of the long molecular axis. For perfect orientational order — all molecules parallel to the director, as in a perfect crystal — θ = 0 and S = 1. For no orientational order — a completely random orientation, as in an isotropic liquid — S = 0. In a typical nematic, S lies between 0.3 and 0.7; in a typical smectic, between 0.7 and 0.8, with higher values at lower temperatures. Fig. 92.3 shows the temperature dependence of S for PAA [10]. The order parameter falls rapidly to about 0.3 near the clearing point Tc and becomes zero in the isotropic state.

The exact dependence of S on T depends on the molecules, but the following analytical expression derived from theory is useful [11]:

(92.2)S = (1 − T/Tc)β

where Tc is the nematic–isotropic transition and β is an exponent between 0.13 and 0.18 depending on the material. (The printed source also includes a coefficient γ of order 0.98; the standard Haller form is used here.)

92.3 Physical Properties of Liquid Crystals

Because of their ordered structure, with molecules aligned with their long axes parallel, LC molecules exhibit anisotropic properties: the dielectric constant ε, refractive index n, magnetic susceptibility χ, conductivity σ and viscosity η all differ parallel (∥) and perpendicular (⊥) to the molecular axis. This anisotropy, together with the ease of setting the initial orientation (boundary condition) by surface alignment and of reorienting the molecular axis by an applied voltage, is the basis of LC displays. This section discusses dielectric and refractive-index anisotropy, the elastic constants and the electro-optical characteristics.

Dielectric Anisotropy

LC molecules exhibit dielectric anisotropy because of their permanent and induced dipoles. The dielectric anisotropy Δε is:

(92.3)Δε = ε∥ − ε⊥

where ε∥ and ε⊥ are measured parallel and perpendicular to the director. Materials with Δε > 0 are called p-type, those with Δε < 0 n-type. p-type LCs tend to align their molecular axis parallel to the applied field; n-type LCs align perpendicular to it.

Generally ε∥ decreases with increasing frequency [12] because of dielectric relaxation, whereas ε⊥ is frequency independent over a wide range. At the crossover frequency fc, where ε∥ = ε⊥, the material becomes isotropic. Depending on the material, fc lies between 100 kHz and more than 1 MHz. The dielectric constants also change with temperature [13], as shown in Fig. 92.4 for the nematic cyanobiphenyl. The two constants converge rapidly as the temperature approaches Tc, where ε∥ = ε⊥ = εisotropic.

Refractive Index Anisotropy

An LC is birefringent, with anisotropic refractive indices. It has two principal indices, no and ne, as shown in Fig. 92.5. For the ordinary index no the electric vector oscillates perpendicular to the optic axis; for the extraordinary index ne it oscillates parallel to the optic axis. In nematic and smectic LCs the director n is the optic axis of the uniaxial crystal, so the indices for oscillations parallel and perpendicular to the director are n∥ and n⊥ respectively: no = n⊥, ne = n∥. The optical anisotropy, or birefringence, is:

(92.4)Δn = n∥ − n⊥ = ne − no

Fig. 92.6a and b show the temperature [2] and wavelength [2] dependence of the indices for typical LCs. The wavelength dependence λ is usually expressed by the Cauchy equation:

(92.5)no,e = n∞ + α/λ²

where n∞ is the index extrapolated to infinite wavelength and α is a material-specific coefficient.

Elastic Constants

In uniaxial LCs the preferred (equilibrium) orientation is given by the director n, imposed by surface treatment at the boundaries or by an external field. When the LC is perturbed from equilibrium by applying or removing a field, elastic and electrical forces determine the static deformation pattern. The transition of the director from one direction to another induces curvature strain. Frank [14] showed that an arbitrary deformation can be seen as a combination of three basic operations — splay, twist and bend — with elastic constants K11, K22 and K33 in the notation of Oseen–Frank theory. Fig. 92.7 illustrates the three. The elastic part of the internal free energy F is:

(92.6)F = ½[K11(∇·n)² + K22(n·∇×n)² + K33(n×∇×n)²]

The free-energy density is thus quadratic in the curvature strains, with the elastic constants as coefficients of proportionality. The constants are temperature dependent and decrease as temperature rises. Their magnitudes can be approximated [15] by:

(92.7)Kii ∝ S²

Electro-Optical Characteristics

When a field is applied to an LC with an initial director orientation, the director changes to a new orientation because of the dielectric anisotropy (Δε = ε∥ − ε⊥). This change is accompanied by a change in the optical transmission or reflection of the LC, which is the basis of LCDs. This electrically driven optical modulation is the electro-optic effect. A field E produces an electric energy fe:

(92.8)fe = −½ε⊥E² − ½Δε(n·E)²

The initial orientation of the director is either parallel (Δε > 0) or perpendicular (Δε < 0) to the plane of the two parallel electrodes. When a field is applied, the director orients parallel to E for Δε > 0 and perpendicular for Δε < 0. The total free energy F of the deformed LC is the sum of the electric energy fe and the elastic energy. The transition from the undeformed state, the Freedericksz transition, occurs when the field reaches a critical value Ec. It is simply a transition from a uniform director configuration to a deformed one: at every point the relative order of the molecules is unchanged. The threshold field is obtained by free-energy minimisation [3]:

(92.9)Ec = (π/d)√[Kii/(ε0Δε)]

The threshold voltage Vth is therefore:

(92.10)Vth = Ecd = π√[Kii/(ε0Δε)]

In Eqs. 92.9 and 92.10, d is the LC thickness and Kii the appropriate elastic constant. When the field is perpendicular to an initially homogeneous director, Kii = K11 or K22; when parallel, Kii = K33. For a twisted orientation, Kii = K11 + (K33 − 2K22)/4.

92.4 LCD Materials and Fabrication Processes

There are several types of LCD using different LC materials and modes, discussed in the next section. However, the general assembly processes and materials are very similar for all modes. Fig. 92.8a and b show the plan and cross-section of a passive-matrix-addressed LCD. Display fabrication can be divided into three parts: (1) lower and upper glass fabrication, (2) cell assembly, and (3) polarizer and driver attachment and module assembly, as illustrated in Fig. 92.9. The following describes the materials and processes.

Glass Substrate

Glass quality matters for chemical compatibility with the LC, surface flatness, defects and dimensional stability under the processing temperatures of the various fabrication steps. With a typical cell gap of 5 to 10 µm, the importance of flatness and surface quality is clear. Defects such as voids, scratches, streaks and attached particles cause electrode defects and hinder uniform cell spacing. Depending on the display type, substrates are sometimes polished. Typical glasses include borosilicate (e.g. Corning 7059) and aluminosilicate (e.g. Corning 1737), 0.7 or 1.1 mm thick.

Color Filters

In full-colour LCDs, colour is usually generated by red, green and blue (R, G, B) colour filters at each pixel, as shown in Fig. 92.8b, using a white backlight. The filters must have suitable spectral transmission and chemical, thermal and dimensional stability. The colour gamut is a function of the backlight spectrum and the filter transmission; with a suitable choice an LCD can match a high-quality CRT, though colour purity and transmission (brightness) are sometimes traded off. Typical filter thickness is about 2 µm. Materials include dye- and pigment-dispersed polyimides and photoresists, applied by spin coating, printing, electrodeposition or photolithography. The first colour is applied and patterned, then the second, then the third. A black matrix is applied and patterned between the filters to block light from the interpixel regions. Sometimes a passivation layer such as low-temperature SiO2 is deposited to act as an impurity barrier and to smooth the surface for the subsequent transparent electrode.

Transparent Electrodes

Indium tin oxide (ITO), typically 90% In2O3 and 10% SnO2, is the usual transparent conductor. Its typical transmission is about 90%. It is deposited by e-beam evaporation or sputtering in an oxygen-containing atmosphere. The film is typically 50 to 300 nm thick, depending on the required sheet resistance and transmission. Typical resistivity is about 2 × 10−4 Ω·cm. Transmission is not linear in thickness because of optical interference.

Alignment Materials and Techniques

After the transparent electrodes are patterned, an alignment coating is applied. Depending on the mode, a homogeneous (parallel), tilted or homeotropic (vertical) alignment is achieved. The common TN LCD requires a tilted alignment to eliminate reverse tilt disclinations. Inorganic films such as obliquely evaporated SiO and organic materials such as polyimide can be used. Polyimide is the most common: it is spin-coated or printed to about 50 nm, cured at 150 to 200 °C, then buffed (rubbed) with a roller covered in cotton or synthetic fabric. For TN LCDs the process is chosen to give a pretilt of about 2 to 5°.

Cell Spacing and Sealing

Cell assembly starts after both substrates have been rubbed. To control the cell gap accurately, spacers are applied in the active area and in the peripheral seal area. Typical spacers are glass or plastic fibres (cylinders) or spheres (balls) with a tightly controlled size distribution. A sealing adhesive such as epoxy is applied at the seal area, leaving a fill hole for LC injection. The seal is typically 1 to 3 mm wide and a few millimetres from the active area. It must not react with (contaminate) the LC and must bar moisture and contamination. After spacers and seal are applied to the first (active) substrate, it is aligned to the second (colour-filter) substrate and the seal is cured by heating, typically to 100 to 150 °C, or by ultraviolet exposure.

LC Material Filling

The empty cell is then filled. Because the gap is small (about 5 to 10 µm), special techniques are needed. The most popular is to evacuate the cell in a vacuum chamber, dip the fill hole into a vessel of LC and raise the chamber pressure; capillary action fills the cell. The fill hole is then capped with an epoxy compatible with the LC.

External Components

External components include polarizers, reflectors, drivers and a backlight. A reflective LCD uses a reflector at the back, modulates ambient light and needs no backlight. In the common transmissive TN LCD, a polarizer is attached to both faces after cell assembly. In the common normally white (NW) mode the polarizers are crossed and aligned with the rubbing directions. A polarizer is a three-layer film: a stretched iodine-doped polyvinyl alcohol (PVA) film in the centre, with two outer triacetyl cellulose (TAC) films protecting it from moisture, temperature and harsh environments. Typical transmission is 41 to 45%, with a polarization efficiency of 99.9 to 99.99%.

The row and column IC drivers are attached to the row and column bond pads by TAB (tape-automated bonding) with an anisotropic conductive adhesive (ACA), or by chip on glass (COG). For transmissive LCDs a fluorescent lamp is generally used. The R, G, B emission of the backlight and the transmission of the R, G, B filters are tuned together to obtain the desired primary colour coordinates. A diffuser provides uniform backlighting, and brightness-enhancement films may tailor the intensity distribution in the viewing cone. Specialised LCDs may add a cover glass with EMI and antireflection coatings at the front and a heater glass at the back (between backlight and LCD) for low-temperature operation.

92.5 Liquid Crystal Display Modes

Many modes of operation have been developed. Historically the phase-change (PC) effect was discovered first, in 1968, by Wysocki et al. [16]. The same year, the dynamic scattering (DS) mode [17] and the guest–host (GH) mode [18] were announced by Heilmeier et al. In 1971 the TN mode [19] was reported by Schadt and Helfrich, and electrically controlled birefringence (ECB) by Schiekel and Fahrenschon [20] and Hareng et al. [21]. The physical effects and the modes based on them include:

Current effects: • DS effect
Electric-field effects: • TN effect • STN effect • ECB • GH effect • phase-change effect
Thermal effects: • smectic effect

The DS effect relies on conductivity anisotropy. Because of higher voltage and power it is no longer used. The TN and STN effects are the most widely used of all LCDs. The following discusses the various modes.

Twisted Nematic Effect

Fig. 92.10 is a schematic of a TN display. It consists of a nematic LC with positive dielectric anisotropy (Δε > 0), about 5 µm thick, sandwiched between two transparent substrates with transparent electrodes. The electrode surfaces are coated with a polyimide alignment layer and rubbed to orient the molecules along the rubbing direction with a small (~3°) pretilt. The molecules on the two substrates are oriented 90° from each other (Fig. 92.10a); the molecular axis twists continuously through 90° from the first substrate to the second. A TN display can be made normally black (NB) or normally white (NW) depending on how the polarizers are attached. Fig. 92.10a and b show the on and off states of an NW-mode TN LCD with crossed polarizers, their polarization directions parallel to the LC director on that substrate.

Because the twist pitch is large compared with the visible wavelength, the polarization direction of linearly polarized light incident normally on one surface rotates through 90° as it propagates through the cell and exits through the second polarizer. When a voltage is applied, the molecules align parallel to the field (Fig. 92.10b) and the 90° optical rotatory power disappears. The polarized light is then not rotated and is blocked by the crossed exit polarizer.

Fig. 92.10c shows the voltage–transmission characteristics for NB and NW modes. In NW, a sufficiently high voltage aligns the molecules homeotropically and there is no rotation of the electric vector, so transmission is completely suppressed regardless of wavelength. The on-state transmission is wavelength dependent, which does not significantly affect contrast but can influence colour balance. In NB the transmission is suppressed to zero [22] in the off state only for monochromatic light of a particular wavelength. In a practical display with a broadband backlight there is therefore some light leakage that lowers contrast. In a colour display the cell gaps for the R, G, B pixels can be optimised to eliminate the leakage [22].

The threshold voltage of a TN LCD is:

(92.11)Vth = π√[Kii/(ε0Δε)], Kii = K11 + (K33 − 2K22)/4

Vth depends on the dielectric anisotropy and elastic constants and is generally 2 to 3 V, with a maximum operating voltage of 5 to 8 V. This low-voltage driving, together with the low current due to the high resistivity of LCs, gives the very low power consumption (~1 mW/cm²) of LCDs. The response times, measured as the rise and decay times of the display, are [23]:

(92.12)τr = γd²/[ε0Δε(V² − Vth²)]
(92.13)τd = γd²/[ε0ΔεVth²]

where γ is the rotational viscosity. The rise time improves with a thinner cell gap d, a higher Δε and a higher drive voltage V; the decay time improves with a smaller gap. The turn-on time τr is usually shorter than the turn-off time τd. At room temperature these times are of order 10 ms, adequate for many common applications such as computer and TV displays. In a TN LCD, grey scale is generated by varying the voltage along the electro-optic curve of Fig. 92.10c. The shallow slope works well for grey scale in active-matrix displays (next section), but in passive-matrix addressing it greatly limits the multiplexibility (number of addressable rows), which led to the STN effect.

STN Effect

In a passive-matrix display the addressability, or maximum number of addressable rows N, is given by the Alt and Pleshko [24] limit:

(92.14)Von/Voff = (√N + 1)/(√N − 1)

where Von and Voff are the rms voltages at selected and non-selected pixels. As N increases, Voff approaches Von and the contrast ratio approaches 1, making the display unviewable. For N = 100, Von = 1.11 Voff: the select voltage is only 11% above the non-select voltage. This gives a very low contrast ratio with a TN mode whose turn-on curve is shallow (Fig. 92.10c).

STN displays were developed [25, 26] to achieve a steep electro-optic curve so that many rows can be multiplexed. The STN effect uses a twist of 180° to about 270° with a relatively high pretilt. Fig. 92.11 [25] shows the voltage dependence of the midplane director tilt of a chiral nematic layer with a pretilt of 28° at both substrates. Bistability is achieved for a twist Φ greater than 245°. In highly multiplexed displays twist angles of 240° to 275° and tilt angles of 5° to 30° are generally used. High pretilts eliminate a competing distortion with 180° less twist. For a 270° left-handed twist, optimum results are obtained when the front polarizer axis makes 30° with the LC director and the rear polarizer makes 60° with the projection of the director on the rear substrate. Because of interference of the normal optical modes, the display has a yellow birefringence colour in the non-select state (yellow mode). Rotating one polarizer by 90° gives a complementary image with a bright colourless state and a blue non-select state (blue mode). White-mode STN displays use retardation films. The response time of a typical STN display is about 150 ms; these displays generally have lower contrast and a narrower viewing angle.

Electrically Controlled Birefringence (ECB)

This technique controls the birefringence of the cell with an electric field. There are several types depending on the molecular orientation: DAP, homogeneous, HAN (hybrid aligned nematic) and IPS (in-plane switching). A DAP cell (homeotropic) uses an LC with negative Δε between transparent electrode substrates and crossed polarizers, as in Fig. 92.12. In the off state (no field) the incident polarized light sees no birefringence and is blocked by the crossed exit polarizer. In the on state the molecular axis is inclined at θ (Fig. 92.12), so the linearly polarized light becomes elliptically polarized and part of it passes the crossed polarizer; the transmitted intensity I is [20]:

(92.15)I = I0 sin²(2φ)·sin²[πdΔn(V)/λ]

where I0 is the incident intensity, φ the angle between the incident polarization and the oscillation direction of the ordinary light in the cell, d the cell gap, Δn(V) the birefringence, dΔn the optical phase difference and λ the wavelength. I thus depends on the applied voltage and λ.

In the homogeneous technique the cell uses a positive material with homogeneous orientation; the threshold voltage is obtained by replacing the bend constant K33 with the splay constant K11. The HAN cell has a hybrid orientation: molecules perpendicular to one substrate but parallel to the other. Both positive and negative Δε materials can be used, and since there is no clear threshold it has the advantage of a very low drive voltage. Recently an IPS-based ECB mode has been developed for extremely wide viewing angles. In IPS the LC is homogeneously aligned and switched by interdigitated electrodes on one substrate [27], using an NB mode with positive or negative Δε.

Guest–Host Type

Some organic dyes show anisotropic absorption: they absorb more in a given wavelength band when the light's E vector is parallel to the dye's optic axis than when it is perpendicular. Displays based on this are called guest–host (GH). A small amount of a dichroic dye (guest) is mixed into the LC (host); the dye aligns with the LC, so its orientation can be changed by an applied field. With a positive-Δε LC, in the off state the E vector coincides with the dye absorption axis, so light is absorbed and the transmitted light is coloured. In the on state the E vector is orthogonal to the absorption axis, so there is no absorption and the transmitted light is white. A GH LCD needs only one polarizer and, because the effect is absorptive, gives a better viewing angle than a TN LCD.

Phase-Change Type

The phase-change display relies on a change from a helical cholesteric phase to a homeotropic nematic phase and back. It uses a cholesteric LC with a long pitch and positive or negative Δε, and no polarizers. In the off state the incident light passing through the focal-conic cholesteric cell is optically scattered, so the cell looks cloudy. When a voltage is applied the helical structure changes to a homeotropic nematic phase and the cell becomes transparent.

Thermal Effects

Thermal effects rely on a change in electro-optical behaviour when the LC is heated or cooled, and are used with homeotropically aligned smectic LCs. When the cell is heated to the isotropic phase and cooled, sudden cooling makes it cloudy while gradual cooling makes it transparent. The cloudy and transparent states correspond to the focal-conic and homeotropic orientations of smectic A. The effect is used for large memory-type displays in which a laser writes the image thermally. Heating can also be done by one transparent electrode, with the other acting as the signal electrode.

92.6 Display Addressing

Addressing (driving) techniques strongly influence LCD image quality. They fall into three types: direct (static), passive matrix and active matrix. For low-information displays such as numeric displays, bar graphs and other fixed patterns using segmented electrodes, direct addressing is used. A common example is a numeric display with seven segments per digit; each segment on the front substrate and the common electrode on the back are connected directly to drive signals, and a voltage is selectively applied to each segment to show any digit from 0 to 9. For high-information displays this becomes impractical because of the huge number of interconnects, so passive or active matrix addressing is used.

Passive Matrix Addressing

A passive matrix (PM) display — an LC between a matrix of transparent row and column electrodes (Fig. 92.8) — is the simplest and least expensive matrix-addressed LCD to manufacture. An example is a colour (R, G, B) VGA display using the STN effect, with a pixel format of 640 (× 3 = 1920) H × 480 V, i.e. 1920 columns and 480 rows, for 2400 interconnects addressing 921,600 pixels. In PM addressing the row voltages are scanned in succession with Vr, while all columns in a row are driven in parallel during the row time with ±Vc depending on whether the pixel is selected on or off. As discussed under the STN effect, the contrast of PM LCDs is limited by the Alt–Pleshko addressability limit [24]. To improve the operating margin, DSTN (dual-scan STN) is used in higher-information displays: the display is split into two halves whose rows are scanned simultaneously and synchronously, essentially doubling the duty ratio of the on pixels. A major shortcoming of the passive-matrix STN display is the slow LC response, of order 150 ms — inadequate for video and barely fast enough for a computer's graphical interface. It can be improved by active addressing or multiline addressing [28, 29], which address several rows simultaneously to suppress the frame response of conventional STN LCDs.

Active Matrix Addressing

Active matrix (AM) addressing removes the multiplexing limitations [24] of PM LCDs by placing a nonlinear control element in series with each pixel, providing a 100% duty ratio from the charge stored at the pixel during the row time. Fig. 92.13a illustrates an active matrix array with row and column drivers and the associated module electronics; Fig. 92.13b is a magnified view of the array. In the figure CLC and CS are the pixel capacitance and the pixel storage capacitance. A storage capacitor CS is usually incorporated at each pixel to reduce the pixel voltage offset (Eq. 92.16) and for broad temperature operation. Fig. 92.14 shows a cross-section through an AM LCD; Fig. 92.15 shows a typical pixel with the gate and data buses, the thin-film transistor (TFT), the ITO pixel electrode and the storage capacitor.

Fabrication of the active matrix substrate is a major aspect of AM LCD manufacturing. Both two-terminal devices (back-to-back diodes and metal–insulator–metal, MIM, diodes) and three-terminal TFTs have been developed. Two-terminal devices are simple and cheap but suffer from non-uniform performance (breakdown/threshold voltage) over a large area and from incomplete isolation of the pixel when neighbours are addressed. For superior image quality, AM LCDs use a TFT, which isolates the pixel completely. Large-area AM LCDs use amorphous silicon (a-Si) TFTs [6], while polysilicon TFTs with integrated row and column drivers are used in small high-resolution LCDs [30].

Fig. 92.16 shows the equivalent circuit of a TFT-LCD pixel, the drive waveforms and the resulting pixel voltage. As in most line-at-a-time matrix displays, the rows (gates) are scanned with a select pulse Vg,sel during the frame time tf, while all pixels in a row are addressed simultaneously with the data voltage ±Vd during the row time tr (= tf/N). During the row time the select gate voltage turns the TFT on and charges the pixel and storage capacitor to Vd. After the row time the TFT is switched off by the non-select gate voltage Vg,non-sel, isolating the pixel charge until the next frame. The LC pixel must be driven in an ac fashion with +Vd and −Vd on alternate frames, with no net dc across the pixel; a net dc causes flicker and image sticking [33] due to LC conductivity, and large sustained dc voltages degrade the LC by electrolysis. The pixel-voltage shift ΔVp at the end of the row time is due to the parasitic gate-to-drain capacitance Cgd of the TFT.

When the gate voltage switches, the redistribution of charge from the gate dielectric gives the pixel-voltage shift ΔVp:

(92.16)ΔVp = [Cgd/(Cgd + CLC + CS)]·ΔVg

For an n-channel enhancement-mode a-Si TFT this shift is negative in both the positive and negative frames; it therefore helps pixel charging in the negative frame and hinders it in the positive frame. Moreover, the increased gate bias during the negative frame lets the pixel reach the data voltage much faster, so the TFT is designed for the worst-case positive frame. ΔVp is reduced by minimising Cgd (a smaller source–drain overlap) and by using a storage capacitor, and is compensated by adjusting the common electrode voltage Vcom as shown in Fig. 92.16. Since Clc is a function of Vp (Vlc) through the dielectric anisotropy, adjusting Vcom alone does not eliminate dc for all grey levels, and the grey-scale voltages must be modified to compensate.

Display Module Electronics

Fig. 92.13 is a block diagram of the electronics of an AM LCD module. The control block and the power-supply generation are mounted separately on a printed-circuit board and connected to the row and column drivers on one side and to the host controller on the other. The control block may include level shifters, timing generators and, in some cases, analog functions; it takes digital data from the host system (typically a graphics controller chip) and converts it into the timing and signal levels required by the drivers. The architecture and design of the module electronics, including the drivers, strongly affect not only system cost and power consumption but also image quality. The LC material typically needs about 5 V for optical saturation (Fig. 92.10c); with ac drive the required swing across the LC is about 10 V. To achieve this, column drivers typically use 12 V supplies. The column-driver voltage can be reduced by Vcom modulation: the Vcom node (connected to all pixels) is driven above and below the 5 V range of the column drivers, alternating every row time, so that 10 V is obtained across the LC with 5 V drivers. This needs extra components and power because of the oscillation, and to avoid capacitive injection the row drivers usually have their negative supply modulated at the same frequency. Compared with 10 V column drivers, 5 V drivers consume less power and are simpler to design and fabricate in small-geometry CMOS. The Vcom modulation method can be used only with row (polarity) inversion, which results in some horizontal cross-talk; column and pixel inversion give better image quality with much-reduced cross-talk but cannot be used with Vcom modulation.
