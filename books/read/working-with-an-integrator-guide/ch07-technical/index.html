Technical qualifications, experience, services and risk

CSIA · Guide Vol. 1 (2000) · §4.7

Chapter 7 · 6 source pages · 2 figures · companion notes
Key terms. Comfort level — the intangible but important degree of trust a client feels. · Partnering — a close working relationship that makes projects more effective as it matures. · In-house vs outsourced — performed by the CSI itself or subcontracted. · Application experience — the weighted-9 example criterion from §2.2. · Type of services — the range of services a CSI offers, also the basis of the Appendix A taxonomy.

§4.7 is the last criteria family, the longest technical one, and outside the scope of CSIA registration — the §3 shortcut only permits skipping §§4.1–4.6.5. Its four sub-groups are technical qualifications (4.7.1), project experience (4.7.2), type of services (4.7.3) and risk considerations (4.7.4). It also links straight back to steps 7–8 of the decision analysis tool.

4.7.1 · Technical Qualifications Four areas of experience

The structure repeats across all four groups A–D: each opens with “Previous CSI hardware / software / application / industry experience can be leveraged to enhance the project, add value and reduce risk”. The same three follow-up questions are used for each: is the training and experience current, are there in-house support capabilities, and are enough experienced resources available?

4.7.2 · Project Experience Four angles, one of them intangible

  1. Comfort level (B). “An intangible, yet very important, qualification is the level of comfort you feel about the firm and its staff.” The source poses five questions: do I trust this CSI? Do I have confidence in them? Have they acted professionally throughout? Are the key players' personalities compatible with my staff? Do they meet their commitments?
  2. General experience (C). Past project history and resource capability matter both to the evaluation and to project success: experience of implementation, project management and resource coordination; typical project size in dollars, time and resources; and the ability to provide multiple references — plus how good those references are.
  3. Relationships (D). Partnering enables the CSI to work closely with the client on project development and enhancement, communications and leveraging experience, making projects more “effective and efficient” as the relationship progresses. The guide asks about relationships with other clients, with vendors and with other potential project team members.
  4. Safety and security (A). At the very start of §4.7.2 the source prints a standalone note: “Special care should be given to safety and security issues”.

For each required service the source asks seven questions: what is the range of specific services; how many years of experience per service; how many resources per service; are required certifications and licences in place; are industry practices and standards followed; which services are in-house versus outsourced; and are outsourced services performed by established alliances or subcontractors, and how is the relationship managed. A final line points to Appendix A: it discusses CSIs by type of service and percent of revenue derived from services.

4.7.4 · Risk Considerations The highest score does not automatically win

This closes the loop with steps 7–8 of §2.2. In the guide's example CSI B has the higher total (57 versus 55) but carries a borderline-high turnover risk — calling for commitment of key project resources. CSI A scores lower but has marginal application experience — calling for a careful review of the specification. The final choice goes to the CSI with the best balance of total score and acceptable, manageable risk.

  • A low score is a signal, not a verdict. §2.2 suggests “low performance score could indicate risk” — investigate rather than eliminate outright.
  • Risks must be named and owned. A risk with no mitigation is not an “acceptable risk” in the standard's sense.
  • Link to §4.3.1 G. The client-involving risk-management procedure is where these mitigations get documented.
End of the criteria. §4.7.4 is the last item in §4. Page 39 opens Appendix A with the integrator/provider taxonomy and its ten service-mix charts — the subject of Chapter 8.
