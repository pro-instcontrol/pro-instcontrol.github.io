Decision analysis tool — the eight steps

CSIA · Guide Vol. 1 (2000) · §§2.1–2.2

Chapter 2 · 4 source pages · 4 figures · companion notes
Key terms. Decision analysis — stating criteria, classifying, weighting and scoring to reach a conclusion. · Kepner-Tregoe® (KT®) — the methodology the CSIA reuses with permission. · Must / Want — mandatory criterion (M, pass/fail) / desired criterion (weighted 1–10). · GO / NO GO — pass or elimination at the screening step. · Weight × performance — the product that produces the total score.

Clause 2 is the heart of Volume 1. It offers a decision analysis tool for establishing criteria, classifying their relative importance and organising evaluation into structured steps. The method derives from Kepner-Tregoe® (KT®) and is reprinted with the permission of Kepner-Tregoe, Inc. This chapter walks through all eight steps; Chapter 3 continues with the scoring scale, CSI levels and the sample analysis table.

2.1 · What the analysis tool is for Three objectives

  1. Plainly state the criteria on which a decision will be based, rather than leaving them implicit.
  2. Assign a relative weight (classify) reflecting how important each criterion is to the decision maker(s).
  3. Provide a structure for a logical evaluation/selection process and a consistent scoring method for reaching a conclusion.
The source inserts two reminders here: “Section 2.5 provides an easy-to-use table to organize evaluation and scoring”, and “The suggested decision analysis tool outlined in this section is based on the Kepner-Tregoe® decision analysis methodology”. The matching blank form is in Appendix B.

2.2 · The eight structured steps From a statement to a decision

1 · State the Decision

Write a short statement describing the intended result of a decision. The guide's example: “Select a CSI that best fits our needs”.

2 · Develop Criteria

Identify criteria that will influence the choice. Examples: over $X.XM in sales; low turnover rate; application experience. §4.0 sets out the CSIA's suggested criteria.

3 · Classify into Musts (M) and Wants

Determine the role each criterion plays: a must is a pass/fail prerequisite, a want is a criterion for comparing degrees of goodness. Example: over $X.XM in sales is marked M.

4 · Weigh the Wants (1–10)

Assign relative values to the want criteria. Example: low turnover rate = 7; application experience = 9. Must criteria carry no weight.

5 · Screen candidates through the Musts

Check whether each candidate meets every must. In the example CSI A and CSI B pass (GO); CSI C falls below the sales threshold (NO GO) and is dropped from further consideration.

6 · Evaluate and score candidates against the Wants

Rate relative performance (0–5) and multiply by the weight. The guide gives CSI A: 7×4 = 28 and 9×3 = 27, total 55; CSI B: 7×3 = 21 and 9×4 = 36, total 57.

7 · Assess risks for the client to manage

Every partnership involves risk the client must manage. Identify the risks and evaluate your ability to manage them. The guide's hint: a low performance score could indicate risk.

8 · Make decision

Select the CSI with the best balance between meeting the criteria (TOTAL SCORE) and acceptable, manageable risks. The highest score is not automatically the right choice.

When several people judge The three-resource rule

The §2.5 sample table uses three judges per CSI. The numbers in the CSI A*, CSI B*, CSI C* columns (for example 1,2,2) are three individual scores; the number in parentheses is the performance score — the average of the three, rounded up, multiplied by the user's want weight. For criterion 4.1.1 A: 1 + 2 + 2 = 5; 5/3 = 1.66 → 2; weight 2 × 2 = 4. Chapter 3 carries the full calculation diagram.

  • Consistency beats false precision. §2.3 says it plainly: because the range of criteria is so broad, any one scoring system will have moments of questionable applicability; decision makers should use their best judgment and move on.
  • A low score is a risk signal. At step 7 the guide suggests reviewing the specification carefully where application experience is marginal, and securing commitment of key project resources where turnover is borderline high.
  • No technical recommendation is implied. The examples only illustrate the use of the criteria; choosing one illustrative scheme does not mean the CSIA recommends that measurement or control method.
