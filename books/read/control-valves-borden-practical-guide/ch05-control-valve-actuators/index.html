Control Valve Actuators

Guy Borden, Jr., Editor · Paul G. Friedmann, Style Editor · ISA, 1998

Chapter 5 · 11 source pages · 4 figures · companion notes

A control valve actuator is a fluid or electrically powered device that supplies the required force to position a valve shaft or stem.

Distinct from an isolation valve actuator, a control valve actuator provides motion to a certain position in proportion to a signal sent from a system controller to the actuator. An isolation valve actuator usually provides one of two positions: full open or full closed. In contrast, a control valve actuator is seldom fully open or closed but in an intermediate position required by the controller to satisfy the process system conditions. A control valve actuator that is not primarily used for tight shutoff usually produces less output force than an isolation valve actuator. However, it provides accurate position control.

The selection process for a control valve actuator considers many attributes. Does the actuator have sufficient travel? Does the actuator generate sufficient force or torque to satisfy the valve's need for the specific application? Beyond these basic considerations are many more that will identify which actuator is best suited for the application. There are no bad or good actuator styles; they all are good for specific applications. This chapter discusses many styles and describes their characteristics to aid in the selection process.

Control valve actuators have evolved recently to keep pace with the evolution of control valves. The changes include more styles of rotary motion, higher precision actuators using hydraulics, and motor/gear drives.

Signals and Power Sources Nguồn tín hiệu và nguồn năng lượng

All control valves receive a signal from a controller. This is the characteristic that distinguishes control valves from all other valves.

Control valve actuators require two outside influences, a signal and a power source. The signal is typically an analog pneumatic 3 to 15 psig or a 4 to 20 milliamperes (mA) electric current. In addition, digital signals are beginning to emerge and will soon make a significant impact on control technology.

The most common power for actuators is compressed air in the 60 to 100 psig range. AC and DC electric power are also used, as is fluid hydraulic power. The hydraulic power can come from a central source or can be generated at the actuator from electric power. Compressed air is the lowest-cost power source and is used in the vast majority of applications. However, electricity and hydraulics have certain virtues that are not available in pneumatics. For many applications the pneumatic signal also serves as the actuator's power.

Basic Styles Các kiểu cơ bản

Actuators are available in many different styles, each with its own advantage and characteristic. Each type of actuator has its own virtue. If it did not, it would not exist in the market.

Spring/Diaphragm Màng / Lò xo

The most common and widely used actuator is the spring-opposed diaphragm, which is illustrated in Figures 5-1 and 5-2. An elastomeric/fabric diaphragm moves against a diaphragm plate that is opposed by one or more helical coil compression springs. The power usually comes from compressed air, but compressed gas or a liquid can also be used. The compressed air will move or extend the diaphragm or a liquid against the spring. As more air pressure is applied, the spring is further compressed, moving the valve stem to the required position. When the air pressure is reduced the spring will retract the diaphragm. Therefore, position may be achieved by varying the air pressure.

The orientation of the spring/diaphragm force direction can be reversed, providing either air to extend (direct-acting) or air to retract (reverse-acting). These options are chosen based on whether the valve is to fail in an open or closed position. Figure 5-1 shows a direct-acting actuator; Figure 5-2 illustrates reverse action.

The diaphragm can vary from a flat sheet to a molded convoluted rolling diaphragm. The flat sheet has the lowest cost but also has a changing effective area with travel. The rolling diaphragm is more expensive but has generally a constant effective area throughout the travel, providing greater precision. It also has a longer cycle life because it rolls rather than flexes.

The actuator's springs oppose the force from the air on the diaphragm. The springs may be singular or multiple, nested concentrically or placed in a circular row. A direct-acting actuator, also called air-to-extend, has air on top of the diaphragm moving the diaphragm and actuator stem in a downward motion. The spring will move them up when the air pressure is reduced. A reverse-acting actuator, also called air-to-retract, has air underneath the diaphragm moving the diaphragm and actuator stem in an upward motion. The spring will move them down when the air pressure is reduced.

Direct-acting is chosen when the valve should fail open when air pressure is lost. Reverse-acting is chosen when the valve should fail closed with air failure.

The springs in the air-to-extend mode are usually low force with a low spring rate that simply provides enough force to return the actuator to its initial position as air pressure is reduced. The reverse or air-to-retract mode often uses springs with higher forces and a higher spring rate to provide the required shutoff force and operating stability.

The springs often match a 3 to 15 psig or 6 to 30 psig signal. However, other ranges, such as 25 to 50 psig, are used for air-to-retract applications, in which the springs provide the shutoff force. Early spring/diaphragm actuators were used solely with a 3-to-15 or 6-to-30-psig signal directly on the diaphragm. The net thrust to the valve stem was quite small as virtually all of the signal pressure was used to compress the spring. In later years, the use of valve positioners greatly increased the actuator's thrust range, especially in direct action. The supply pressure is loaded onto the diaphragm when the valve is seated and the positioner's signal reaches the upper limit, 15 or 30 psig. Instead of a net 2 or 3 psi seating the valve, 45 psi net pressure is possible with a 60-psig supply pressure.

An actuator is often characterized by its bench range. The bench range lies between the pressure at which the actuator begins to move and the pressure at which the actuator reaches full travel. An actuator with a 3-to-15 psi spring will have a 3-to-15 “bench range.” Unfortunately, the “bench range” may distort when the valve is in service.

Consider an actuator with a 100-square-inch effective diaphragm on a valve that has an upward thrust from pressure of 200 pounds. In this service, the bench range is biased by 2 psi, the actuator begins to move at 5 psi (3 psi plus the 2-psi bias) and will reach the seat at 17 psi. The usual rule is the valve is at mid-travel at 9 psig, but in this case it is 11 psig. There are four solutions to this situation: use a valve that has a better pressure balance, use a larger actuator, bias the controller's signal to 5 to 17 psi, or use a positioner.
A dead band of 10% is not unusual. Let us add dead band to a 3-to-15 bench range actuator. This equates to a friction drag of 1.5 psi and a position error of 10%. The pressure at mid-travel while closing will be 9 plus 1.5, or 10.5 psi, and the pressure while opening will be 9 minus 1.5, or 7.5 psi. Therefore, the pressure range at mid-travel is 10.5 to 7.5 psi.

The effect of dead band can be reduced with a valve-actuator combination that has very low friction. An easier solution is to virtually eliminate dead band by adding a positioner.

The effective areas of spring/diaphragm actuators may range from 30 to 450 square inches, with 60 to 200 as the more common range. Travels may be as long as 6 inches for the larger effective areas but usually are 3 inches or less.

The force on a valve stem from the valve's trim usually changes through its travel due to the changing effective areas and changing pressure drops of the valve trim. To ensure stable operation, the rate of force change in the actuator must be higher than the force change rate in the valve resulting from the effects of the process fluid pressure and the dynamic forces from flow.

The main contributor for operating stability is the actuator's spring rate. Also contributing is compression rate on the fluid that drives the diaphragm. Low-pressure air has a very low compression rate, with the compression rate increasing as the air pressure increases. A liquid-actuating fluid has an extremely high compression rate and will make a significant contribution to stable operation.

An unstable valve will have uncontrolled cycling stem motion. The valve will be driving the actuator more than the actuator will be driving the valve.

The spring/diaphragm actuator's yoke is usually made of cast iron, but ductile iron or steel is used when additional safety margins are desired in applications such as nuclear power, where valve reliability during a seismic event is a prime consideration. Pipeline vibrations or sudden motions can cause sufficient stresses to fail gray iron. The diaphragm cases are usually made of pressed steel using curved and conical shapes to enhance strength. The diaphragm is an elastomeric-coated fabric. The strength of the diaphragm is derived from the fabric, which is usually made of nylon, polyester, or nomex. The diaphragm may be sealed with a coating of nitrite or neoprene rubber. Silicone rubber may be used for high-temperature or high-radiation situations. Valve and diaphragm materials are discussed in Chapter 11.

The spring/diaphragm actuator has the lowest cost of all control valve actuators. Its range of effective areas and travels covers most valve applications. A disadvantage is its lower stiffness, which may not be stable in some applications. It sometimes lacks sufficient travel and sufficient effective area.

Piston Piston

Piston actuators share many features and advantages of the spring/diaphragm style. The principal difference is the cylindrical sliding seal instead of a flexing or rolling diaphragm. The main advantages of a piston actuator are its capacity for longer travel and larger possible effective area. It also generally tolerates higher actuating pressures. As with the diaphragm style, the piston actuators can be spring-opposed, air-to-extend or air-to-retract. Piston actuators are also used in double-acting mode with actuating pressure on either side or both sides of the piston depending on the required direction and magnitude of stem force. The double-acting mode can also be used with the spring-opposed mode. A four-way positioner is often used with the double-acting mode, while a single-acting positioner may serve the spring-opposed mode.

The higher pressure capacity of a piston actuator is well suited for hydraulic power. The power may come from a remote source or may be generated at the actuator by an electric pump. The advantages include high stability, higher force output with higher pressures, and possibly faster travel speeds, depending on the supply of hydraulic fluid.

The design of the piston's seal is important. Two characteristics are desirable: long life and low friction. An effective piston seal is an O-ring. The seal compression must be low to prevent twisting of the O-ring as the piston travels. The O-ring has the advantage of being bidirectional. A piston actuator commonly used in hydraulic power service usually has a heavy seal that has high friction and may not be suitable as a control valve actuator.

The piston actuator's yoke is made of the same materials as the spring/diaphragm for the same reasons. The piston cylinder may be brass or plated steel. Fiberglass with low friction coating on the inside diameter is also used, and while it has low weight and friction it lacks fire resistance. The piston seal may be an elastomeric O-ring or a Teflon seal energized either by pressure or a metallic spring. Another option is an elastomeric O-ring encapsulating with Teflon, which gives both resilience and low friction.

Piston actuators usually cost more than a spring/diaphragm but offer a cost advantage when used with higher actuating pressures, with a large effective area, or with long travel. Figure 5-3 shows valves with piston actuators.

Electric Motor/Gear Mô-tơ điện / Bộ giảm tốc

An electric motor actuator with gear reduction will produce torque or force with rotary motion. For rotary-motion valves, the connection may be direct, but conversion to linear motion is required for linear-motion valves. A common form of conversion involves the actuator turning a threaded nut on a nonrotating stem. A more efficient system uses a recirculating ball nut in place of the threaded nut.

An electric motor actuator has several advantages over pneumatic actuators. They are well suited for remote locations because the transmission of electric power is more practical over long distances than is the transmission of compressed air.

Most electric motor/gear actuators will fail in place on power interruption unless the gear train has very high efficiency and backdrives. Valve applications with rapidly changing stem forces that may be unstable with pneumatic actuators will be inherently stable with geared electric actuators. Long linear travels are easily achievable. With a motor of given horsepower, changing the gearing can easily trade between torque and travel speed, sacrificing one to gain the other. An electric actuator may cost less than a pneumatic system if the cost of maintaining the pneumatic system is included.

Electric actuators in installations with few valves and without a compressed air source may cost less than installing an air compressor for the few valves.

Some electric actuators have a spring-loaded torque limit switch that disengages the electric motor as a certain torque is reached. The torque switches are adjustable, allowing the delivered output to be varied to suit the application. Position-limit switches are also used in the same system to give a signal relative to position or to disengage the motor at extreme positions.

Fluid power motors are sometimes available in place of the electric motor. The fluids can be compressed air, liquids, or the process fluid itself.

Common accessories include handwheel overrides, local position indicators, position-limit switches, indicating lights, and open/close/stop pushbuttons.

Electrohydraulic Điện-thủy lực

Electrohydraulic actuators combine the attributes of the piston with an electric-driven hydraulic pump. This combination delivers a high output force relative to the volume of the actuator and usually has high position accuracy. Springs can be used for either fail-open or fail-closed but also can easily be fail-as-is by locking the hydraulic fluids in the last position.

Electric Solenoid Van điện từ điện

A recent actuator development is the electric modulating solenoid. Within the valve bonnet or bonnet extension is a fixed core and a movable core attached to the valve stem. A solenoid coil actuator is mounted external to the bonnet tube. Energizing the solenoid coil creates a magnetic attractive force between the fixed and movable cores. The movable core is opposed by a spring inside the bonnet tube. When the attractive force overcomes the spring force, the movable core moves towards the fixed core, compressing the spring and moving the valve stem. The magnetic force acting on the movable core is proportional to the current supplied to the solenoid. The movement of the core is dependent upon the value of the solenoid current in relationship to the rate of the spring. Therefore, varying the solenoid current results in proportional motion of the core or valve stem. The stem motion is measured by a linear variable differential transformer (LVDT). The LVDT signal is supplied to the electronic positioner and to any local or remote position indicators.

The force transmitted to the valve stem from the solenoid is relatively small compared to other style actuators. Balanced trims with low forces are usually required because of the relatively small actuating forces.

The electric modulating solenoid's primary advantage is that the valve can be hermetically sealed with all moving parts located within the valve's pressure enclosure.

Vane Cánh quạt

Vane actuators have only rotary outputs. They can be thought of as a rotary piston. Located within a segmented pressure chamber is a sealed blade or vane whose end is attached to the rotary shaft. Fluid pressure on one side of the vane induces rotary motion in the shaft. The main virtue of a vane actuator is its simplicity and low cost. Its disadvantages are the lack of an internal spring-return, high friction from the vane's seal, and the difficulty of mounting the usual accessories.

Motion Conversion Chuyển đổi chuyển động

Spring/diaphragm and piston actuators have inherent linear motion while electric motor/gear actuators have inherent rotary motion. Each of these can be converted to the other motion using a conversion device.

Lever (Linear to Rotary) Đòn bẩy (Tuyến tính sang quay)

The most common method is a lever attached to a rotary valve shaft on one end and the linear actuator stem on the other. The actuator stem can be directly connected to the lever, but provisions must be made to enable the diaphragm/piston to rotate in the path of the lever pivot or a link must be added between the actuator stem and the lever pivot.

Rack and Pinion (Linear to Rotary) Bánh răng thanh răng (Tuyến tính sang quay)

A second method is a rack and pinion that is usually used with a piston actuator. The piston shaft is connected to a rack, or the piston is integral with a rack. The rack turns a pinion gear that is mounted on the rotary valve shaft.

Scotch Yoke (Linear to Rotary) Cơ cấu chạng vạc (Tuyến tính sang quay)

The scotch yoke is a variation of the lever conversion with the actuator stem connecting to a slot in the lever. Within this arrangement, neither an additional link nor pivoting is required.

Threaded Screw or Ball Screw (Rotary to Linear) Vít ren hoặc vít bi (Quay sang tuyến tính)

The center rotating member on an electric motor/gear actuator can be threaded to accept either a threaded stem or a recirculating ball screw mechanism. The stem will be guided so it does not rotate, and the rotating thread or ball screw will convert the rotary motion to linear.

Linear-Rotary Torque/Travel Relationships Quan hệ mô-men – hành trình giữa tuyến tính và quay

The relationship between output torque and the actuator stem travel with a constant stem force differs for the three different linear-to-rotary conversion methods. The output torque using the lever is greatest at mid-travel when the lever is perpendicular to the actuator stem. The output torque is constant at all travel with the rack and pinion. The scotch yoke has the greatest torque at the two extreme ends of the travel, an advantage for rotary valves that require high seating torques. These differences in torque characteristic can be a positive factor in selecting actuators, depending on the need for which the valve is selected.

Motion/Force Amplifiers Bộ khuếch đại chuyển động / lực

Mechanical devices can be used to modify the relationship between travel and force. The laws of energy conservation allow the trading of travel force. If one is reduced or increased the other will change inversely. There are several mechanical devices used for conversion: levers, scissors or toggles, gears, and a rotary lever.

Power Failure Modes Chế độ khi mất nguồn

Each actuator type has its natural mode of failure position when the actuation power is removed. A spring-return diaphragm or spring-return piston will return to its initial position with no actuating power. A double-acting piston will fail in the direction of applied stem force from the valve. Both of these cases can be prevented, if desired, with a lockup valve that seals the actuating air in the actuator upon power failure. A tank may be used as a pressure reservoir when the supply of actuating fluid is disrupted. The electric motor/gear actuator generally fails in place when power is interrupted except in cases of high mechanical efficiency, when it will back drive in the direction of applied force.

Actuator/Valve Sizing Factors Yếu tố chọn kích cỡ cơ cấu chấp hành / van

The selection of the appropriate actuator type and style is based on power, applicability to the valve's use, and cost.

The mode of action, direct, reverse-, or double-acting, should be chosen based on process system requirements.

The third area of actuator sizing and selection is the actuator's size. Actuator manufacturers will make a series of sizes. Actuator sizes are often made with the outputs increasing from one size to another in a ratio of about 1.6:1. The appropriate size should be selected to suit the application. Two factors need to be known: the required force or torque output and the required travel. The required force or torque is the summation of seating forces, pressure forces acting on the trim or unbalanced areas, and the stem area, plus seals and packing friction. Dynamic forces from the flowing fluids must also be considered.

As stated earlier, the valve's force rate of change relative to travel must be known or accurately estimated to ensure the actuator's stiffness exceeds the valve's stem rate of change throughout the travel. If the relationship reverses, the valve's stem motion will have unstable cyclic motion. Detailed discussions of actuator sizing can be found in Reference 1.

The valve's force rate of change relative to travel must be known or accurately estimated to ensure that the actuator's stiffness exceeds the valve's force rate of change throughout the travel.

Determining the valve's required force or torque is the responsibility of the valve manufacturer, while the actuator manufacturer is responsible for determining the actuator output and force characteristic. In most cases, both the valve and actuator will be made by the same manufacturer.

Actuator Accessories Phụ tùng của cơ cấu chấp hành

A control valve actuator may have accessories to complement its performance. Valve accessories are discussed in Chapter 15. The usual actuator accessories are described in the following sections.

Manual Override or Handwheel Tay quay dự phòng / Điều khiển tay

A manual override may be top-mounted on a spring/diaphragm actuator either pushing or pulling in the direction of the applied air pressure. It usually opposes the actuator spring. For piston actuators and some spring/diaphragm actuators it may be side-mounted. Figure 5-4 shows top- and side-mounted overrides. For a double-acting piston there is no spring to oppose, so the override will have a stem locking feature that allows force to be applied in either direction. Overrides for electric motor/gear actuators are usually a disengageable device that turns one of the primary gear shafts. The override is used to position the valve during a power failure and move the valve to a set position for accessory calibration, and it is sometimes used on spring/diaphragm actuators as a travel stop.

Positioners Bộ định vị

A positioner is a valve controller that compares the controller's signal with the stem position feedback. The positioner will change the power to the actuator so the valve travel matches the signal. The advantages of a positioner is higher positioning accuracy and usually faster travel speeds. Positioners can be pneumatic, electropneumatic, or electrical.

Limit Switches Công tắc hành trình

Limit switches are mounted on or in the actuator to give an electrical signal when a preset travel limit is reached. Usually, they are set at either end of the travel, but they are also sometimes set at an intermediate position. They are usually considered devices for telling where to stem is, but actually they tell where the stem isn't.

Position Transmitter Bộ phát tín hiệu vị trí

A position transmitter can be considered a reverse positioner. It will provide a continuous signal, usually 3 to 15 psig or 4 to 20 mA, proportional to the valve's travel position. Unlike limit switches, a position transmitter always tells the valve's travel position. Sometimes a position transmitter is incorporated in the positioner; this may offer a cost advantage. However, if the travel feedback linkage on the positioner fails, the position transmitter will give a false signal. The safest approach is to have separate mountings for both devices.

Solenoid Valves Van điện từ

A solenoid valve may be used as a pneumatic power or signal override. It may be used to vent or fully pressurize the actuator regardless of the signal.

Air Regulators Bộ điều áp

Air regulators or air sets are used to limit the air pressure to the actuator or its accessories in order to avoid overload damage to the accessories, the actuator, or the valve.

Volume Boosters Bộ tăng lưu lượng khí

Volume boosters are used to increase air flow volume to the actuator. Positioners or controllers may not pass a sufficient amount of air volume to achieve the required travel time. A volume booster may be installed in the positioner's or controller's output line with a connection for supplying air pressure. The booster is a pilot device that will match its output pressure with its input pressure at higher flow rates.
