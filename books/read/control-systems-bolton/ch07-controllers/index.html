This chapter discusses process controllers and the control laws they use, from simple on-off control, through the classic three-term (PID) controller, to digital systems such as PLCs and embedded controllers.

7.1 Introduction Introduction

Process controllers are components which basically have an input of the error signal, i.e. the difference between the required value signal and the feedback signal, and an output of a signal to modify the system output. The simplest form is an on-off device which switches on a correcting device when there is an error and switches it off when the error ceases. Such a method has limitations and more sophisticated controllers are often used. A form of controller which gives satisfactory control in many situations is the three-term or PID controller. The term control mode is the type of response a controller gives to an error signal; the three basic modes are proportional (P), integral (I) and derivative (D), and the three-term controller combines all three. Figure 7.1 is the bimetallic thermostat used in a simple temperature control system.

7.1.1 Compensation and controllers Compensation and controllers

Compensation is the modification of a system's performance characteristics to obtain the required ones; compensators are added to modify closed-loop performance and can be placed anywhere. Controllers have an error input and a correction output and are used at a specific point. From the design point of view there is no real difference between compensators and controllers — the terms reflect hardware differences. A controller is traditionally a stand-alone component offering control modes such as proportional gain and integral and derivative action, normally including a summing element.

7.2 On-off control On-off control

With on-off control, the controller is essentially a switch activated by the error signal and supplies just an on-off correcting signal. An example is the bimetallic thermostat (Figure 7.1): if the actual temperature is above the required temperature the strip is in the off position and the heater is off; if below, it moves to the on position and the heater switches on. The controller output can thus be just on or off (Figure 7.2).

Because the control action is discontinuous and there are time lags, the controlled variable oscillates about the required condition. With the bimetallic thermostat, when the room temperature drops below the required level there is a significant delay before the heater affects the room, so the temperature has fallen further; when it rises to the required value the system still overshoots because of reaction delay — the temperature oscillates above and below the required value (Figure 7.3). The system may also chatter near the set value; this is reduced if the heater switches on at a lower temperature than it switches off (Figure 7.4). The dead band is the range between the on and off values. A large dead band gives large fluctuations; a small one increases the switching frequency.

7.2.1 Electronic switching Electronic switching

Mechanical switches (bimetallic strips, relays) switch slowly; faster switching uses electronic circuits such as thyristors or transistors. A thyristor (SCR) can be considered a diode switched to conduct at a particular forward voltage. Figure 7.5 shows a thyristor in series with a 30 Ω resistor across 300 V; the gate controls the breakdown voltage. Figure 7.6 shows it chopping a d.c. voltage to control power to a load; Figure 7.7 is an a.c. circuit for lamps, motors or heaters where R2 sets the trigger level and a diode protects the gate.

The transistor also acts as an electronic switch (Figure 7.8): with no input the collector-emitter resistance is high and the whole Vcc appears at the output; with sufficient input it drops and Vout ≈ 0. A Darlington pair (Figure 7.9) amplifies a small base current to switch a larger load, with a protection diode. D.c. motor speed can be open-loop controlled by pulse-width modulation (PWM) (Figure 7.10): switching the d.c. voltage on and off, the larger the fraction on the closer the average to the input. Figure 7.11 is a transistor PWM circuit with a flyback diode. The four-transistor H-circuit (Figure 7.12) controls both direction and speed; Figure 7.13 uses logic gates for microprocessor control. Figure 7.14 is a closed-loop microprocessor system: tachogenerator → ADC → processor → PWM → motor.

7.3 PID control PID control

There are three basic modes. Proportional (P): output proportional to error e, i.e. output = Kp·e with Kp the proportional gain (Figure 7.15). Derivative (D): output proportional to the rate of change de/dt, i.e. output = Kd·de/dt (Figure 7.16) — the slope of the error-time graph. Integral (I): output proportional to the integral of the error with time, i.e. the area under the error-time graph, and so can be non-zero even when the error has returned to zero (Figure 7.17).

7.4 Terminology Terminology

Terms used in describing controllers: set-point is the signal representing the required output; range is the two extreme values between which the system operates (e.g. 0°–30°); span is the difference between those extremes (e.g. 30°). Consider a proportional controller in a closed-loop system (Figure 7.18): adding Kp gives a second-order closed-loop transfer function; with a step input, if underdamped there are oscillations before settling (Figure 7.19).

A second problem with proportional-only control: consider water-level control (Figure 7.20); to hold a new outflow the valve needs a new signal, but a proportional controller only gives output with an error — so the level never quite reaches the set value; this is the offset or steady-state error (Figure 7.21). Increasing Kp reduces the offset but increases the oscillation frequency and reduces damping (more overshoot) — a compromise is needed.

PD (proportional + derivative): the response to a ramp input (Figure 7.22) has the derivative term largest when the error slope is greatest, so the output rises faster and overshoot is reduced; derivative is never used alone. PI (proportional + integral): the response to a step input (Figure 7.23) removes the offset, giving a steady-state value equal to the set value; used where load disturbances occur frequently. PID combines both. Example (Figures 7.24–7.27): for the given error signal, sketch the controller output starting at 50% as (a) just proportional Kp=5, (b) P+D with Kp=5 and Kd=2.0 s, (c) P+I with Kp=5 and Ki=2.0 s⁻¹ — the output is 50%+5e, plus 2.0 times the error slope, and plus 2.0 times the area under the error-time graph respectively.

7.4.1 Proportional band Proportional band

With process controllers the proportional gain is described by its proportional band (PB) — the percentage deviation that produces a 100% change in controller output (Figure 7.28): %PB = %deviation × 100 / %change in output. A 50% PB means a 50% deviation gives 100% output change; 100% PB means 100% deviation gives 100%. Example: a temperature controller with input 0°–50° and output 4–20 mA, PB 60% → Kp = 1/0.6 × (20−4)/(50−0) = 0.53 mA/°.

7.6 Controller mathematics Controller mathematics

The basic forms (Figures 7.31–7.34): P output = Kp·e; PD output = Kp·e + Kd·de/dt = Kd(s + Kp/Kd)E(s), with Td = Kd/Kp the derivative time constant; PI output = Kp·e + Ki∫e dt = Kp(1 + 1/(Tᵢs))E(s), Tᵢ = Kp/Ki the integral time constant; PID output = Kp·e + Ki∫e dt + Kd·de/dt = Kp(1 + 1/(Tᵢs) + Td·s)E(s). 7.6.1 Bode plots Bode plots

Figure 7.36 shows the basic Bode gain plots: (a) P increases overall system gain; (b) PI decreases high-frequency gain (thus reducing high-frequency noise effect); (c) PD decreases low-frequency gain and raises it (increasing phase margin but increasing noise effect); (d) PID has no standard form since the shape depends on the ratio Tᵢ/Td (commonly Tᵢ = 4Td).

7.7 Tuning Tuning

Tuning is selecting the optimum controller settings (Kp, Ki, Kd). The most widely used empirical methods are those of Ziegler and Nichols, approximating the open-loop transfer function by a first-order system with time delay, giving two procedures: the ultimate-cycle method (closed-loop test) and the process-reaction method (open-loop test). Both aim for under-damped transients with a decay ratio of about 1/4.

7.8 Digital systems Digital systems

Direct digital control (DDC) uses a digital computer in the control system to calculate the control signal applied to the actuators (Figure 7.38). At each sample instant the computer samples the plant output via an ADC, together with the discrete input, processes them by the required control law (e.g. PID) and sends the result via a DAC to the plant. The programs repeat every sampling period, computing the proportional part, the derivative part (difference between successive error samples) or the integral part (area under the output-time graph).

7.8.1 Programmable logic controllers Programmable logic controllers

A PLC is a special microprocessor-based controller with programmable memory, programmed by engineers via a simple pictorial ladder language. A PLC system has five basic components: processor, memory, power supply, I/O interface and programming device (Figure 7.39). The programming device (often hand-held, Figure 7.40) loads the program into memory. The motor-switching circuit (Figure 7.41) is redrawn as a ladder (Figure 7.41(b)) with two power rails and horizontal rungs. Basic symbols (Figure 7.42) are normally open / normally closed input contacts and the output symbol, with END marking the last rung; Figure 7.43 is a ladder rung. Example: a PLC controls central heating (Figure 7.44) — boiler, pumps, radiators and hot-water tank with sensors and a clock; Figure 7.45 is the ladder program with inputs X400–X403 and outputs Y430–Y432.

7.8.2 Embedded systems Embedded systems

An embedded system is a control system with the microprocessor embedded as an integral element — engine management, camera exposure/focus, washing machines.
