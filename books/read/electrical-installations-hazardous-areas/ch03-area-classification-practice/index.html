3 — Area classification practice for gases, vapours and mists in freely ventilated situations 3 — Area classification practice for gases, vapours and mists in freely ventilated situations

From principles to hazardous area extents

Chapter 2 examined the mechanisms of area classification, the constitution of the teams carrying it out and the detail of the information needed to produce a classification. This chapter turns to the actual production of a classification for a particular works or plant, based on the information produced by those exercises and on available knowledge of how flammable gases, vapours and mists behave when released into an atmosphere or other area where sufficient oxygen exists to support combustion.

A tremendous amount of research has been carried out in a relatively coordinated way on methods of protecting electrical equipment for use in explosive atmospheres; the same cannot be said of work on area classification, which has been less intensive and more fragmented. BS 5345 Part 2 (1983) described the facets and procedures well but had to direct readers to other industrial and similar codes for the detail of extents, because of divergent views between those involved and between industries and companies. The reassuring thing is that adoption of almost any published industry code will produce an acceptably secure classification when applied by personnel knowledgeable in the subject, operating without undue commercial pressure and with full information about the site and the flammable materials involved. Each code, however, rests on a particular approach, and these approaches vary between codes; extreme care is therefore necessary if a plant is classified using information from more than one code. The objective here is to examine the background philosophy, give example extents based upon it, and use the mathematical formulae of Chapter 4 where appropriate.

3.1 Containment of flammable materials 3.1 Containment of flammable materials

Flammable materials may be contained in a process plant in a variety of ways; the most obvious is as a gas or vapour, usually at some elevated pressure needed to transport it or for some other process reason. In freely ventilated areas the question is then simply one of determining the release rate and the mechanism by which the gas or vapour mixes with air, usually by virtue of its own motion or by wind effects. Several other ways in which a flammable material may exist make the classification exercise much more complicated.

3.1.1 Effects of storage conditions 3.1.1 Effects of storage conditions

The material may, for instance, be contained at high pressure, so that what would be a vapour at normal ambient pressure is actually stored as a liquid. When such a liquid is released the pressure falls and vaporization takes place, much as in a refrigerator. Heat input is needed to effect vaporization (latent heat of vaporization): as the liquid vaporizes its temperature falls until it reaches its boiling point, at which point vaporization ceases until external heat is added. There is therefore an immediate evaporation of a percentage of the liquid, followed by slower evaporation when the residual liquid reaches a heat source. That percentage needs to be defined; a possible calculative method is given in Chapter 4.

The material may instead be contained at a pressure below its boiling point, and it might be thought that little vapour would be produced on initial release. That is not wholly true: the release orifice may support the formation of mists, finely divided liquid particles which behave like vapours as far as explosive atmospheres are concerned. With small orifices significant mist formation is possible at gauge pressures as low as 2 × 10⁵ N/m²; with larger orifices it is less likely, although part of the liquid release may still emerge as a mist. The mist may be ignitable even if the liquid at the point of release is below its flashpoint, provided it is appropriately mixed with air. Another typical method of producing a mist is a flammable vapour contained as a liquid at pressure: the turbulence created by pressure reduction, coupled with the vaporization already described, produces some mist in addition to the vapour at the point of release. All of this must be taken into account.

There is also the containment of a flammable liquid above its flashpoint but below its boiling point. Little flammable vapour is produced on release, so jet formation is possible. Unless contained, such jets can travel significant distances before striking the ground or an obstruction, and where they cease to travel a pool will be formed. The pool is controlled in size either by ground contours or by the evaporation rate of the material, and significant hazardous areas can be produced some distance from the source of leakage; in addition there is the risk that the liquid jet may come into contact with a hot surface, exacerbating the situation. All of these matters must be considered.

3.1.2 Effect of sunlight on storage vessels 3.1.2 Effect of sunlight on storage vessels

A further problem exists where flammable liquids are contained for significant periods and the containment is in direct sunlight, typically the ordinary stock tank. There is considerable evidence that significant solar gain can occur in such circumstances and that the vapour/air mixture in the tank above the liquid adopts a temperature significantly above ambient, encouraging evaporation. Liquids with flashpoints above ambient, which would not otherwise be considered flammable, may thereby become flammable, and some flammable liquids may reach their boiling points. These matters also require to be taken into account.

3.1.3 Oxygen enrichment 3.1.3 Oxygen enrichment

Finally, it must be remembered that area classification is based upon the mixture of a flammable gas, vapour or mist with air at atmospheric pressure; the technology is likewise based upon mixtures of flammable materials with air. The conditions necessary for ignition vary, particularly with increased levels of oxygen such as occur in oxidation processes. Equipment considered suitable for explosive atmospheres, assuming as it does atmospheric pressure and air, may not be suitable in such circumstances. Where such situations arise the classification evaluation must take account of them, and they must be identified as special so that those selecting equipment are aware that normally protected equipment may not be suitable and must be excluded in favour of apparatus shown to be suitable for the specific environment.

3.1.4 General consideration of release 3.1.4 General consideration of release

Taking account of the above, it is necessary to consider all points from which flammable material may be released and to identify appropriately the areas contaminated by those releases. Historically this was done by the generalized method of area classification, but since 1970 the more exact and considerably more objective source of hazard method has come to the fore; the examples in this chapter are based on the latter.

3.2 Generalized method of area classification 3.2 Generalized method of area classification

The generalized method is the method historically used. Knowledge of plant performance gained over many years has now permitted a more precise method, known as the source of hazard method. That does not mean the generalized method may not be used, but it has become less popular because correct application often gives more extensive hazardous areas than actually exist, increasing operating costs and sometimes causing conflict when regulatory bodies and others perceive Zones 0 and 1 as excessively large; since a principal objective of classification is to influence plant design, this leads to excessive expenditure. Occasionally, if not carefully executed, it can also fail to identify certain hazardous areas adequately, creating unnecessary danger. The generalized method does not require each source of hazard to be uniquely identified; a judgment is made from the presence of flammable materials in a particular area together with general identification of the types of process equipment known to leak. This requires a set of basic yardsticks against which such judgments may be made.

3.2.1 Generalized zonal classification specification 3.2.1 Generalized zonal classification specification

Zone 0. Zone 0 includes the interiors of closed or ventilated vessels containing flammable liquids or vapours and air. Clearly this applies only to the vapour spaces of closed or ventilated tanks containing flammable liquids, and as these tanks usually contain a varying quantity of liquid the Zone 0 extends to the lowest possible liquid level. A Zone 0 will also occur in the vapour space of an open-topped tank containing a flammable liquid, as will all other places where flammable gas or vapour is normally released.

Zone 1. Zone 1 is any area containing vessels, pumps, compressors, pipe fittings and similar items of equipment which may be considered to leak in normal operation (that is, with some degree of regularity), and any area containing relief valves, vents and similar devices designed to release flammable gases, vapours or liquids in normal operation. Areas containing sample points not specially designed to prevent release in normal operation are also Zone 1, although in the case of sample points the Zone 1 can be limited to the immediate location of the point, particularly if sampling is manual. Finally, areas where releases are so rare as not to be assumed to occur in normal operation will also be identified as Zone 1 if ventilation is restricted, including both indoor areas and other areas where, as a result of containment or airflow restriction, ventilation is considered to be restricted.

Zone 2. Zone 2 is an area containing vessels, pumps, compressors and similar equipment so well maintained that leaks can be assumed to occur only very rarely (in abnormal operation), and relief valves which operate only very rarely (abnormally); vents associated with bursting discs will also normally give rise to Zone 2 in the areas in which they occur. In all these cases the Zone 2 classification is acceptable only provided the areas in question are freely ventilated, that is, equivalent to a normal outdoor situation, so that the released vapour is rapidly dispersed. Less well-ventilated parts of these Zones 2, such as pits and trenches, will be defined as Zone 1. Because of the danger to personnel carrying out sampling, sampling points are normally designed to ensure that any area into which personnel access for sampling is Zone 2.

3.2.2 Generalized extents of zones 3.2.2 Generalized extents of zones

The extents of the zones produced by this approach tend to be arbitrary and large. Vessel interiors are well defined, but when the less hazardous areas — particularly Zone 2 — are considered, entire locations have to be classified, and it is not unknown for a whole plant to be classified. Properly used, however, the generalized method must be accepted as valid since it over- rather than under-classifies. Regrettably, there is ample scope for incorrect use, and the areas designated as Zone 1 and Zone 2 often become larger than would be acceptable if the source of hazard method were used, causing considerable loss of credibility when identified by third parties. The method is now seldom used except in very congested parts of plant, and even there only as an adjunct to the source of hazard method.

3.3 The source of hazard method of area classification 3.3 The source of hazard method of area classification

The source of hazard method was introduced progressively from around 1970. Before that time the generalized method of Section 3.2 had been used extensively, but there was growing concern in industry that the hazardous areas it described were too large to represent the true picture, particularly Zone 1, which covered significant areas. This raised questions about free access of plant personnel to the zone: the frequency of release of flammables in Zone 1 is significant and, as a significant number of flammables can be toxic or asphyxiant, exposure of personnel may not be considered acceptable. Together with the cost implications, this led to many pressures to restrict the size of these hazardous areas. The generalized method was found not to lend itself to this without introducing significant risk, and attempts to achieve the required reduction led to a bewildering variation of conclusions which threatened confidence in area classification.

These problems led to a much closer examination of the technology of area classification and hence to the development of the source of hazard method. This method differs sharply from the generalized method: each source of release and the mode of release at that point must be identified, release quantities and dispersal criteria must be established, and from this information the extent of the hazardous area emanating from a particular release is determined. The extent is derived partly from mathematical approaches similar to those explained in Chapter 4, although these are to a degree inexact, as witnessed by the failure of those involved to agree a single mathematical approach, and partly from experimental evidence and observation. Although some controversy remains, most published approaches give relatively similar results and smaller hazardous areas than the generalized method. In over 25 years of use of the source of hazard method there is no evidence that the results achieved have increased the risk of explosion where the technique has been properly applied.

Another difference brought about by the source of hazard approach is the clear identification, for the first time, that Zone 1 is not automatically surrounded by Zone 2. While this was also true in the generalized method, the fact tended to be obscured by its generality and it became common belief that Zone 1 was always surrounded by Zone 2. The source of hazard method automatically dispels that misunderstanding: to have a hazardous area it is first necessary to identify a source of release which creates it. A leak would therefore have to be identified as possibly behaving in two distinctly different ways in order to produce both a Zone 1 and a Zone 2. This in turn leads to another fact not previously clearly identified, namely that a single source of leakage can behave in different ways in different circumstances. When a source of hazard is identified under this method it is necessary to determine whether it can behave in more than one way and then to identify the hazardous area created by each possible mode of operation.

While the source of hazard method identifies each source of release and the hazardous area it creates, there remains the problem of multiple sources in the same or close locations. It is clearly not likely that all sources will release at the same time, but in multiple cases some may, and the extent of any hazardous area so produced needs to be identified. In the case of secondary grade sources the release is so infrequent that such sources need not be added in any way, but the same cannot be true of primary grade sources, which release much more frequently. In such cases account must be taken of simultaneous release. With many such sources it would clearly be unreasonable to assume that all release simultaneously, so some sort of guide is necessary. 3.3.1 Types of release 3.3.1 Types of release

In any process there are typical releases which fall into recognizable categories, and most releases normally encountered will fall into one of them. The following six are typical. The source of hazard method nevertheless requires each individual source and its resulting hazardous area to be identified, so these categories are a guide rather than a substitute for that exercise.

Release of gas or vapour under pressure. Most processes put gases and vapours under pressure to transport them, to keep them stable, or to inject them into process vessels. Such a release has a high velocity, normally the speed of sound in the gas or vapour concerned, and it mixes with air through its own turbulence; prevailing wind then has little or no effect outdoors. This remains true unless the jet meets an obstruction, when its velocity falls sharply and the wind becomes the main dispersant. Indoors, or in less well-ventilated situations, dispersion is into a limited volume of air and the effect is cumulative, so that the whole indoor or shielded area can become contaminated. Special precautions are often necessary: extensive local extract ventilation, and excluding pressurized gases and vapours as far as possible from areas where ventilation is restricted.

Release of liquids under pressure. A liquid contained under pressure will often escape as a jet, and the distance the jet travels may partly define the hazardous area. Vaporization at the point of release is minimal while the liquid is below its boiling point at atmospheric pressure, so the main source of vapour is evaporation from the surface of the pool formed by the jet. Because jets travel far, it is often wise to fit a baffle at any possible leak source to prevent jet formation and fix the location of the pool; the pool can also be limited by the design of its location, leading to bunding. Mists may also form: finely divided liquid droplets which behave like a gas or vapour, produced at gauge pressures as low as 2 × 10⁵ N/m². Where mists can form, flashpoint ceases to be a dividing criterion, because mists of liquids below their flashpoints can be ignited.

Release of liquefied gases and vapours. Where gases and vapours are kept liquid by pressure, or by a combination of pressure and temperature, release usually causes part of the material to vaporize instantly. The remainder stays liquid until it can obtain enough heat to become a gas or vapour. There is thus a hazardous area from the gas or vapour formed immediately, plus the pool scenario, and the turbulence of vaporization also creates some mist. The resulting situation is very complex indeed.

Unpressurized releases. Many containers, such as stock tanks, road tankers and paint vats, have an explosive atmosphere in their vapour space, caused by vapour from liquids above their flashpoint but below their boiling point. In normal circumstances these vapours are relatively heavy and do not leave the vapour space in significant quantity while equilibrium is maintained. Such containers are, however, frequently filled, and filling expels the flammable material into the surrounding area, creating a hazardous area.

Communication between physical locations. A hazardous area may be bounded by a wall or similar barrier which effectively sets the zonal limit. Any opening in that wall, such as a door or a cable trunk, must then be considered: it can act as a conduit carrying the explosive atmosphere from one area to another. The only effective treatment is to regard each such opening as a source of hazard in the remote area and classify accordingly.

Pressurized gases and reagents. In many cases gases or reagents used above their flashpoint are brought into, or present in, particular areas for purposes such as analysis. Although not directly related to the process, these flammable materials are equally sources of hazard and must be treated as such. The difficulty is that, if they were not identified when the plant was built, they may be considerably more sensitive than the process materials and the equipment selected for the plant may be unsuitable for them.

While the source of hazard method requires each source and its hazardous area to be identified, some generalizations are permissible and may simplify classification. They tend to give excessive hazardous areas, but they are more objective than the generalized method and can save significant time and money without endangering security. The nomenclature used for the various grades of hazardous area is shown in Figure 3.1.

3.3.2 Releases from pipe joints 3.3.2 Releases from pipe joints

There are five principal ways of joining two pipes: welding, gasketing, compression (olive) glands, 'O' rings and screwing. A welded joint makes the two sections effectively continuous, and if properly made and checked it is not considered a source of release for area classification purposes. Welds can still rupture, but so rarely that the frequency may be neglected here.

None of the other joint types is considered unreliable enough to release in normal operation if properly chosen and installed, except where human intervention is likely. Pipe joints in general are not treated as primary grade sources: they generally constitute Zone 2 and are capable of producing secondary grade releases, by a different mechanism in each case. A screwed joint leaks up its threads, releasing relatively small quantities at low velocity, so wind dispersion is the norm. A compression joint gives a small line release past the olive, also at low velocity because of its construction. An 'O' ring leaks only if it cracks; seated in a recess, it has little chance of blowout, so the leak geometry is a crack and the release velocity may be high because there is little to modify the flow.

Finally, the gasket is the most common pipe joint, with two possibilities. A normal compressed asbestos fibre (CAF) gasket is held by pressure alone, so at all but very low pressures blowout of part of the gasket must be considered, giving a fairly large orifice. The size of the blowout is normally related to the position of the flange bolts, and a typical size can be quoted. To prevent blowout, a spirally supported gasket may be used: a similar material with a spiral metal support wound into it. Blowouts are then effectively prevented, but leakage can still occur between the flange and the gasket through joint stress or relaxation; since gaskets are usually fitted to larger pipes, this leak is larger than for compression fittings.

Where joints are regularly broken, for example when pipes are spaded off to remove equipment or to allow entry to a vessel, human intervention close to the flange increases the possibility of leakage and must be taken into account. Well over 50 per cent of flammable materials are toxic at levels far below their lower explosive limit (LEL), and creating a significant explosive atmosphere where personnel are present at that moment is unacceptable. The material must therefore be removed from the pipe before the work starts, and the procedure should eliminate significant explosive atmospheres at the moment of breaking. Since such procedures can fail, it is prudent to identify a nominal Zone 1 of 1 m radius around the joint. This applies only to joint breaking on a relatively regular basis, for example possibly more than once a month on average; less frequent breaking falls under the random release scenarios giving Zone 2 dealt with in this chapter.

Taking these considerations into account, typical leaks covering most normal situations may be defined. In the following examples and tables the assumed release apertures and velocities are: CAF gasket — 4 × 10⁻⁵ m² (25 mm × 1.6 mm), velocity high when unimpeded or low when impeded, based on a piece of gasket ejected between two flange bolts; spirally supported gasket — 2.5 × 10⁻⁶ m² (50 mm × 0.05 mm), high or low velocity, based on relaxation of the joint because parts of the gasket cannot be ejected; 'O' ring — 10⁻⁶ m² (1 mm × 1 mm), high or low velocity, based on a crack in the ring; compression joint — 10⁻⁶ m² (0.1 mm × 10 mm), low velocity, based on relaxation of the metal-to-metal contact with a labyrinthine path around the olive; screwed joint — 5 × 10⁻⁷ m², small and labyrinthine, low velocity, based on exit via the threads.

Although to a degree arbitrary, these figures are fairly conservative, so they are unlikely to be smaller than what actually happens in practice, and the areas calculated from them will in the great majority of cases be larger than those which occur. If local knowledge provides more accurate figures for the particular situation, the larger values should be used. Although release velocities for gaskets and 'O' rings are given as high, any obstruction within the orifice after its smallest point or close to it will significantly reduce that velocity; unless such obstructions can confidently be ruled out, dispersion by the wind should always be considered.

Liquid releases. When a liquid is released there is the further problem of jets which can travel significant distances before striking the ground, and of pools from which vapour escapes once the liquid has landed. It is preferable to baffle the joint to prevent jet formation and then to control any pool so as to limit the resulting hazardous area. The preferred arrangement gives a pool of known, controlled dimensions under the pipe, or containment local to the pipe which collects the liquid and transfers it to safe disposal. If the pool is not deliberately contained it will grow until the rate of vaporization from its surface equals the rate of leakage. The pool becomes smaller as the liquid temperature approaches its boiling point, while the hazardous area around it becomes larger because vaporization is faster.

Pump with packed gland or mechanical seal. Where the leakage is of compressed gas, a Zone 1 of 1 m radius is present. Zone 2 should be taken from Table 3.2 for a spirally supported gasket, and account should also be taken of the proximity of the ground to the leak (see Figures 3.3 and 3.4).

Although packed gland seals can be used in all of the situations above, mechanical seals are recommended, particularly for gases and liquids above their atmospheric-pressure boiling points, because of their better wear performance. Throttle bushes are also recommended in these cases to give added confidence in maximum leak rates. Where concentrations of rotating pumps and compressors are present, this situation must be identified and the hazardous areas treated as for valves.

Moving reciprocating seals. Reciprocating compressors and pumps leak around their pistons into a crankcase or the chamber behind the piston. If the crankcase is ventilated, the hazardous area appears at the ventilation exit, which may be some distance from the point of compression, so a further hazardous area may be created elsewhere. In normal operation a Zone 1 of 1 m radius around the point where the leakage reaches the atmosphere is sufficient. For abnormal circumstances, the release at both the normal exhaust point and local to the seal should be treated as for a spirally supported gasket (Table 3.2); because of compressor duty this can produce significant hazardous areas.

3.4 Other practical well-ventilated situations 3.4 Other practical well-ventilated situations

The previous sections have discussed the performance of leaking flammable material from the more classic leak sources, such as pipe flanges and pump/compressor seals, but many particular situations require specific consideration.

3.4.1 The fixed roof vented stock tank 3.4.1 The fixed roof vented stock tank

This type of tank is extremely common on process plant for storing flammable materials that are normally liquids. Most are in well-ventilated outdoor areas with free vents to let vapour escape during filling and air enter during emptying. In direct sunlight, however, solar gain can occur, especially in the vapour space, and temperatures above the typical ambient maximum of 32 °C can be reached. The extent of this gain is not specified directly, but one company code gives 47.5 °C for small tanks, and the HSE booklet HS(G)50 (1996) defines flammable liquids as those with flashpoints up to 55 °C. It is prudent to adopt the latter as the basis here and not to store any liquid with a boiling point below 55 °C in such tanks.

Such tanks are normally bunded to contain spillages from gross overfilling or leakage, and the bunding determines the extent of the pool of flammable liquid (unless a single bund surrounds several tanks, in which case normal pool sizing applies). To be effective, the nearest point of the bund should always be more than 1 m from the tank so that liquid leaking from the tank falls into it; the bund should also hold the entire tank contents with a safety factor of about 1.1.

There are two normal situations to consider, inside the tank and outside it during filling and emptying, together with the abnormal case of overfilling that puts flammable liquid into the bund and may also involve flange leakage nearby. On these assumptions the classification of the tank and its surroundings is as shown in Figure 3.9.

Zone 0. Because the emptying cycle constantly draws air into the vapour space and mixes it with vapour, an explosive atmosphere is likely to exist somewhere in that space. Since the actual part cannot be defined, the entire vapour space must be classified as Zone 0.

Zone 1. The filling cycle exhausts a mixture of flammable vapour and air from the vent; this vapour is almost certain to be heavier than air, so an explosive atmosphere forms around the vent and tends to travel downwards and outwards along the tank top, possibly overlapping the tank edges. In abnormal circumstances the liquid pool in the bund gives off vapour which, unless the bund is very large and shallow, is impeded from dispersing, so the interior of the bund is also likely to be Zone 1.

Zone 2. If the tank is overfilled, flammable liquid exits the vent, travels down the sides of the tank and collects in the bund. Vaporization from the outside of the tank and from the surface of the pool creates a Zone 2 around the tank and beyond the bund wall. Leakage from the tank and associated pipework also produces a Zone 2, but this is contained within the Zone 2 caused by overfilling.

Limitations and other considerations. In producing Figure 3.9, a tank of some 250 m³ capacity has been used and no pumps or other pressurizing devices have been considered as present within the bund. The water drainage facilities associated with the bund have also not been considered, as they are assumed not to transmit flammable liquid or vapour from the bund; their design should achieve this in any properly designed installation, but if it does not, such possibilities must be considered. Where solar gain brings the contained liquid near its boiling point, a cover should be considered to prevent direct sunlight; this removes solar gain and reduces internal maximum temperatures to around 32 °C. If the cover is flat, the area under it all becomes Zone 1 with a distance of 1 m laterally from the edge of the cover and a similar radial distance from any vents in its top. A cover with sides is not recommended, as it can severely affect ventilation between the tank top and the cover, at worst creating a Zone 0 in that space, particularly if there are no top vents to give a chimney effect.

3.4.2 The floating roof tank 3.4.2 The floating roof tank

In these tanks the roof is supported by the liquid within the tank and moves up and down with its level. A seal is provided between the moving roof and the walls of the tank, and vapour can only be present where it, or small quantities of liquid, pass that seal. This is either a situation of normal operation or, where the amount of liquid passing the seal normally gives sufficient vapour to create an explosive atmosphere, a continuous situation. The walls of the tank above the roof may also restrict dispersion and cause the explosive atmosphere to persist, an effect exacerbated where a cover is fitted. The result is shown in Figure 3.10 and is basically as follows.

Zone 0. This does not exist within the tank, unlike the fixed roof case, as the roof is in intimate contact with the stored liquid. Provided the space in the tank above the roof is unimpeded, the retention of flammable vapour is not sufficient to create a Zone 0 in that space, so some advantage over fixed roof tanks is apparent.

Zone 1. The interior of the tank above the roof is Zone 1, extending 1.5 m above and laterally from the edges of the tank. As the vapour is generally heavier than air, Zone 1 can be expected to extend further down the sides of the tank. In abnormal circumstances the tank behaves like a fixed roof tank: liquid overflows into the bund, a pool forms and the vapour space above it contains an explosive atmosphere. Because the bund wall is likely to interfere with ventilation, there will be persistence within the bund and its interior vapour space is Zone 1.

Zone 2. As for the fixed roof tank there is the abnormal possibility of overflow into the bund, so a Zone 2 of 1.5 m from the top of the tank and around its sides will be present; this will not show where Zone 1 exists, as it has a similar extent and is thus masked. A Zone 2 will also exist from the bund limits for a vertical and horizontal distance of 2.5 m, as for the fixed roof tank.

Limitations and other considerations. As for the fixed roof tank, a tank of some 250 m³ capacity has been considered with no pumps or similar equipment contained within the bund. Drainage devices are again assumed not to transmit flammable liquid out of the bund. The effects of a cover will also be similar to those for the fixed roof tank, although a cover is more likely here in view of the construction of the tank.

3.4.3 Tanks containing gas, vapour or liquefied vapours 3.4.3 Tanks containing gas, vapour or liquefied vapours

These tanks are normally sealed as the gas, vapour or liquid will be under pressure. Leaks from them should be treated as for leaks from pipe and similar joints, since even if it is liquid that is released it will rapidly vaporize. Such tanks may, however, be bunded to take care of massive releases which, although outside the scope of area classification (for example catastrophic), may need to be provided for. If such bunds are used then, even if the releases are secondary grade (abnormal), the bunds may create conditions in which the explosive atmosphere persists within them, and their interiors, as for liquid storage tanks, should be considered as Zone 1.

For low pressure storage, such as gasholders, water seals are often used. Where this is the case, the possibility of gas exiting through the liquid must be considered if the pressure inside the holder is too high, or venting at pressure relief valves provided to prevent overpressurizing. In such circumstances the following classification may be appropriate and is shown diagrammatically in Figure 3.11. There should be no Zone 0 either inside or in the proximity of the storage holder, as this would be tantamount to storing and delivering an explosive atmosphere to a process; in any event the liquid sealing arrangements should achieve the same conditions as in a floating roof tank. Release at pressure relief valves to a limited extent is highly likely in normal operation; this limited release gives rise to a Zone 1, but as the pressure will be very low the Zone 1 will be of limited size. A figure of 3 m is suggested as adequate to cover this eventuality.

In abnormal circumstances, overpressurizing due to overfilling the tank will cause significantly more release at the pressure relief valve and, in addition, releases at the water seal or seals. The area contaminated in these circumstances will be larger, and a hazardous area of 10 m from both of these leakage points is suggested as necessary to cover this eventuality.

Limitations and other considerations. In selecting the above hazardous areas, normal venting is assumed to be from an orifice of around 2 cm² at 10⁴ N/m², the passage through the diaphragm or water seal of the pressure relief valve being considered partial. This is not so in abnormal situations, where full passage through the pressure relief valve, or a significant part of the water seal, is considered.

3.4.4 Road and rail tanks for flammable liquids 3.4.4 Road and rail tanks for flammable liquids

Liquids are regularly transported by road or rail. In general, the areas through which they pass in transit are not classified because the dwell time is short and any leakage during transit is abnormal, so the likelihood that a given area becomes contaminated with an explosive atmosphere is very low and area classification is not relevant. Care is needed, however: where transport tanks dwell for significant times, or where large numbers of them are stationary for a considerable time, area classification may become relevant. The usual dividing line is between general car parks, where tanks may reside overnight alongside other transporters, and locations specifically designated for tank parking, such as the vicinity of loading stations on airports.

This approach means that parking areas with general public access are not normally classified, whereas parking areas on sites where the materials are used and which are designated specifically for such tanks are classified. Typical examples are railway sidings dedicated to the marshalling of tanks, specific areas of dockyards dedicated to the location of tanks before loading or unloading, and similar locations in process plant areas. The generalization is not always valid, however: where many tankers regularly park, the division described above becomes harder to justify. Because such parking is uncontrolled, classic area classification cannot be used and the presence of the driver becomes critical. Routes and parking procedures need to be defined to minimize the number of tankers parked in any one location for significant periods, to reduce overnight parking and to ensure that there is no evidence of leakage when the vehicle is left; together these reduce the risk to an acceptable level. Many government regulations apply here, typically The Dangerous Substances (Conveyance by Road in Road Tankers and Tank Containers) Regulations 1981 (SI 1981/1059), which should be consulted.

Wherever tankers are loaded, off-loaded or parked when loaded in significant numbers, there is little justification for not applying the normal area classification procedures, and the tank and its contents then form a source of release unless particular sources are prevented. For example, release from tank vents may be removed by an extraction system so that no hazardous area forms beyond the immediate vicinity of the vent, but the vent exhaust still needs consideration. In addition, tanker bays used for unloading only will differ from those used for loading because there is little or no release from vents. The following treatment applies to tankers carrying unpressurized flammable liquids below their boiling point.

These tankers are normally of mostly welded construction, so the sources of release generally needing consideration are: releases from joints used for pipework connection; releases from vents during loading; and releases from flexible pipes used to connect the tanker to fixed plant. The classification below considers only the tanker itself and its immediate coupling; the fixed plant in the loading area must be dealt with separately. Zone 0: the interior of the tanker.

Zone 1. During loading or unloading there is always a danger of a small liquid leak while coupling or uncoupling. This is a primary grade source of release, so a Zone 1 must be defined around the coupling points. Its size is to a degree arbitrary because the amount of manually introduced leakage is unknown, but is not likely to be large: a Zone 1 of 1.5 m above and around the source of leakage, projected down to the ground as shown in Figure 3.12, should cover all eventualities. The downward projection reflects the likelihood that the vapour is heavier than air. In Figure 3.12, a = 1.5 m; b and c depend on the characteristics of the pool formed by any liquid release, and the dotted phi line is the limit of Zone 1 for the off-loading operation.

During off-loading the hazardous area due to the vents does not exist, and the extent of Zone 1 from the coupling is then defined by the dotted lines in Figure 3.12. This Zone 1 extends right under the tanker to allow for the restricted ventilation caused by the tanker's presence. A third primary grade source of release is the flexible coupling pipe, which is subjected to regular movement and can fail in normal operation. Failure is likely to take the form of a small crack and, because the pressure is low (usually just the tank head pressure), the release is small. For this reason the Zone 1 produced is likewise limited to 1.5 m above and around the flexible coupling, projected vertically down to the ground.

Zone 2. This results from abnormal circumstances. The first is overfilling: liquid flows out of the vents, down the sides of the tank and ultimately forms a pool on the ground. The Zone 2 produced above and around the tank by this event is no larger than the Zone 1 from vapour release and will be invisible when road/rail tankers are loaded, but because it is abnormal it cannot be assumed not to exist in the unloading scenario. A Zone 2 of 1.5 m above and around the tank will therefore be present in all circumstances and will be projected vertically down to the ground. The pool of flammable liquid grows until it is contained or until its vaporization rate equals the liquid release rate; the hazardous area created by this pool is Zone 2 and is sized as given in Table 3.7.

A second abnormal source is rupture of a flexible connecting pipe. Because of the reinforcement in such pipes, total rupture is not expected, so a release similar to that from rupture of a CAF gasket is considered a fair model. The released liquid forms a pool whose dimensions can be determined from Table 3.3, and the extent of the hazardous area around that pool is then determined from Table 3.7. As the pressure is low, mist is unlikely to form and the Zone 2 around the connecting pipe will be minimal; this is academic because the 1.5 m Zone 1 already declared will encompass it. Finally, solar gain in road/rail tankers is highly unlikely unless they are parked for long periods in direct sunlight immediately before unloading. With proper care, only liquids with flashpoints below 32 °C need be considered.

Tankers containing gases or vapours under pressure, or liquefied gases or vapours (which are of course also under pressure), behave as any other vessel in such circumstances. The principal release mechanisms are: leakage during coupling and uncoupling; leakage due to valve actuator failure; leakage due to failure of fixed pipe joints; and leakage due to failure of flexible couplings. The interior of the tank is considered Zone 0 because, even though air is less likely to enter, there is always the possibility that the tank will regularly be empty and it is better to assume Zone 0 than to define and control such periods. This assumption will not normally be unnecessarily restrictive.

Zone 1 results from three primary grade scenarios: two due to failure and one due to manual intervention. The first is the valve stem or actuator; the Zone 1 is based on that for a regularly operated valve, so a Zone 1 is assumed to exist for 1 m above and around the valve. The second is failure of the flexible coupling pipe: the orifice is expected to be small, total vaporization at the point of release can be assumed, and the release is treated as similar to leakage from a screwed joint; the Zone 1 is determined from Table 3.9 and that at ground level from Table 3.10. The third is manual intervention during coupling or uncoupling, expected to be so small that it is covered by the Zone 1 for flexible pipe failure; if the coupling point is remote from the flexible pipe it should be surrounded by a Zone 1 of similar proportions, with Tables 3.9 and 3.10 applying. Zone 2 arises from the flexible pipe, couplings, other joints and the valve actuating stem: the valve stem seal is treated as a spirally supported gasket and the flexible joint as a CAF gasket rupture, with extents derived from Tables 3.9 and 3.10. All of the above covers only the tanker and its immediate environs; fixed plant surrounding the tanker must be dealt with separately.

3.4.5 Oil/water separators 3.4.5 Oil/water separators

Oil/water separators are open vessels into which a mixture of water and (usually spilled) oils is placed and allowed to dwell for a significant time, so that the water separates from the oil and the one can be disposed of while the other enters the normal drains. Theoretically the vessel normally contains mainly lower volatility oils, since oils of higher volatility will have undergone significant vaporization before the mixture reaches the separator. Where the majority of oils used at a site are of high volatility, this type of separation is not appropriate and another method should be chosen, because the hazardous areas it produces would not be acceptable.

Zone 0 is the interior of the separator, but this zone does not extend beyond the extremities of the separator because the oils it contains are of low volatility. It would be unwise to use this type of separator if the oils were of high volatility, and some other approach should be used in those circumstances.

Zone 1 occurs where the separator contains oils of higher volatility than normal and, through wind action, a hazardous area exists outside the separator. In these cases the mix of volatility should permit use of the 50 per cent column of Table 3.7, taking the area of the separator as the pool size. Where a significant number of the oils on the plant that may be fed to the separator are of high volatility, the >90 per cent column should be used. The hazardous area is shown in Figure 3.13 for a ground level separator; if the separator is above ground level with its base at or around ground level, Zone 1 merely extends vertically down the separator sides.

Zone 2 occurs in abnormal circumstances, such as after a major release of high volatility oil, when a larger hazardous area exists around the separator. Its extent is derived from Table 3.7 using the >90 per cent column. As stated above, this area becomes Zone 1 if a significant number of the oils used on the site are of high volatility; in such cases no Zone 2 is visible because its size is synonymous with that of Zone 1. Figure 3.13 shows a ground level separator; if the ground is at or about the base level of the separator, Zone 2 is projected vertically down the separator side. A wind speed of 2 m/sec is assumed when using Table 3.7.

Finally, if the separator overflows, a pool of mixed oil and water forms. The associated Zone 2 is derived from Table 3.7 assuming the highest volatility oil is present. Because water is present and may carry the mixture further than oil alone, it is advisable to limit the pool mechanically with containment sized to hold the maximum possible overflow. That containment size should then be used in all circumstances when determining extents from Table 3.7, even where the equilibrium pool size is smaller.

3.4.6 Other open vessels 3.4.6 Other open vessels

Open vessels behave in a very similar manner to oil/water separators, except that usually only one flammable material is present. Zone 0 is the interior of the vessel. Zone 1 is as for an oil/water separator but defined, where appropriate, on the basis of the boiling point of the single flammable liquid. As before, open vessels are not recommended where the boiling point of the contained liquid is less than twice the ambient temperature of the location (in the United Kingdom this effectively means boiling points above 65 °C). This differs from the low volatility criterion used for sampling points in Section 3.4.9, where the boiling point must be at least four times ambient.

The only situation giving rise to Zone 2 is overflow, and the resulting Zone 2 should be defined as for oil/water separators in that circumstance.

3.4.7 Open drains 3.4.7 Open drains

Open drainage gullies are often used to collect released flammable liquids so that the dimensions of any pool can be accurately defined and to provide a route for collection and disposal. Unlike oil/water separators, they do not normally contain flammable liquids but may do so in cases of spillage. The presence of such liquids within them is therefore either in normal operation or abnormal only, and because the gullies actually drain the liquid away, overflow is abnormal. This leads to the following hazardous areas.

Zone 0. If the flow into the drain is in normal operation, the interior of the drain is Zone 0 because of the persistence effect introduced by containment within the drain. In other cases no Zone 0 exists; it would not be acceptable for flammable material to discharge continuously into an open drain. If release into the drain is abnormal, a Zone 0 will still exist within the drain at any point where the geometry causes liquid to collect, become static and persist until it has all evaporated.

Zone 1. The drain acts as a long narrow pool and the area around it will from time to time contain an explosive atmosphere through wind action. A Zone 1 exists around the drain (see Figure 3.14) whether release into it is in normal or abnormal operation. This is nominally 1 m; although to a degree arbitrary, it is likely to be larger than any practical distance achieved. Collection of flammable liquids at low spots in the drain is unlikely to affect this extent. In Figure 3.14, a = 1 m.

Zone 2. Normal release into the drain coincident with drain blockage can produce an external pool, which grows until the release stops or the evaporation rate exceeds the release rate. This pool produces a Zone 2 in accordance with Table 3.7, its dimensions being set by containment where this exists or by calculation where no containment exists (see Chapter 4).

3.4.8 Trenches 3.4.8 Trenches

Two types of trench are normally encountered. The first contains pipework, with the possibility of leakage inside it from pipe joints or valve seals. Such a leak can occur in normal operation (a primary grade release) or only abnormally (a secondary grade source), and the trench should be treated in the same way as a drain based on the type of leak and the quantity of flammable material leaking, if the leak is liquid. If the release is of gas or vapour, the types and extents of hazardous areas should be those produced by a leak occurring outside the trench, but should extend throughout any length of trench between blockages. Deliberate blockages are normally placed in trenches to prevent flammable materials being transmitted from one part of a plant to another. In these cases the interior of the trench is Zone 1 even if the release is only abnormal, because of the persistence introduced by the trench.

The second type is not intended to contain any flammable material, such as a trench used for a cable route. Such trenches only matter insofar as they lie in areas defined as hazardous for another reason. The hazardous area inside the trench is then: external Zone 0 → internal Zone 0; external Zone 1 → internal Zone 1; external Zone 2 → internal Zone 1. The upgrade from external Zone 2 to internal Zone 1 allows for persistence within the trench. The hazardous area within the trench again exists for the entire length between blockages. The same relationship holds where flammable liquid leaks into the drain from an external pool; in the case of liquids it is assumed that action is taken within a reasonable time to remove the flammable material from the drain, which is considered essential.

3.4.9 Sampling points 3.4.9 Sampling points

The problem with sampling points is that most are manual and, apart from the doubtful wisdom of deliberately creating a significant hazardous area by a routine action, there is the problem that the person taking the sample is inside the hazardous area at the time when flammable materials are present. Since the majority of flammable materials are also toxic (see HSE Guidance Note EH 40), this is not a situation that can be accepted. Sampling arrangements are therefore usually carefully designed to minimize the hazardous area created in normal operation, so that the only significant hazardous area occurs in abnormal operation.

Sampling liquids. Any sampling point used for sampling liquids should be so arranged that any spillages occurring during normal sampling are removed immediately. To do this, the sampling arrangements should incorporate features of the kind achieved by the system shown in Figure 3.15: the tundish or receptor of spillages below the sampling point should transfer any spillage to a closed storage facility and safe disposal system. For liquids of low volatility (suggested to have atmospheric boiling points of at least four times ambient temperature) the effects of gravity will normally be all that is needed to ensure spillage collection in the tundish, but in other cases some form of extract system will be needed to draw spillages into the tundish.

If these techniques are used, the release of flammable material into the atmosphere around the sampling point in normal operation will be minimal and no significant Zone 1 will exist. There will, however, remain the possibility of significant release in abnormal operation, due for example to failure of the sampling valve, which will release too much liquid for the tundish to deal with, or to blockage of the tundish itself. In both cases there will be significant leakage of flammable liquid and possible splashing around the sampling point. This produces a small Zone 2 around the sampling point and a pool below it which grows until it is contained physically or by the evaporation rate, then creating a hazardous area as defined in Table 3.7. The result given in Figure 3.15 is as follows: there are no Zones 0 and 1, and Zone 2 consists of a sphere of 1 m around the sampling point together with the pool and its surrounding hazardous area. In Figure 3.15, a = 1 m, b = pool and c = Table 3.7.

Where the flammable material is not toxic, simpler arrangements are possible but are not recommended, as they will probably create larger hazardous areas whose justification is doubtful given how easily the above arrangements can be used. Details of toxicity for the more usual materials are given in HSE Guidance Note EH 40.

Sampling gases and vapours. Sampling gases, vapours and liquefied vapours under pressure can give rise to a hazardous area of significant proportions and cannot be permitted. It is necessary to use the closed in-line sampling system shown in Figure 3.16 and, in some cases, even to allow for evacuation of the spaces between the sample bottle and the fixed valves before disconnection of the bottle. This is likely only with liquefied gases, since otherwise the amount of flammable material stored in the interspace will be small if the system is properly designed. Again the objective is to eliminate any Zone 1 effectively, so that a hazardous area is present only in abnormal operation. Such abnormal situations include valve seal failure, pipe-joint failure and incorrect connection by personnel, the last of which would be expected to be corrected quickly because the sampling personnel are present.

The hazardous area would therefore be most likely to arise from pipe-joint or valve failure, both of which are dealt with in sections 3.5.1 and 3.5.2 and in Table 3.2 (a numbering that does not occur in the printed text; see the note below). The result is as follows. There are no Zones 0 and 1. Zone 2 consists of an area in accordance with line 3 of Table 3.2 around the valve stem and an area in accordance with the appropriate column of Table 3.2 around each joint, as shown in Figure 3.16.

3.4.10 Walls and apertures 3.4.10 Walls and apertures

Where extents of hazardous areas are defined by a wall that contains a door, the door becomes a source of release as far as the non-hazardous side of the wall is concerned and results in a hazardous area as shown in Figure 3.17. Likewise, where a wall is short, the hazardous area extends around it as shown in Figure 3.18. In both cases the extension is based upon the original extent of the hazardous area and the distance between the source of hazard and the wall.

The key to both figures is: a = distance of wall from source of hazard; b = extent of hazardous area ignoring wall; c = b − a, the extension beyond the wall. The extension is thus the original extent reduced by the source-to-wall distance.

3.4.11 Vents 3.4.11 Vents

Vents normally operate only in abnormal circumstances, such as the opening of a relief valve or the rupture of a bursting disc. In such cases, relatively large amounts of flammable gas or vapour are released directly to the surrounding atmosphere through a vent or flare stack. This section refers only to such direct situations; in other cases secondary containment is usually the norm and can be dealt with according to earlier sections of this chapter. The flare is assumed not to be ignited, since that possibility exists.

In the case of a bursting disc there will be no release of flammable material in normal operation, whereas with a relief valve a slight leakage through the seat must be considered possible (this does not include the valve-operating stem, which should be dealt with separately). In the one case a Zone 1 may be present at the vent exit and in the other not. When the valve opens or the bursting disc ruptures, a significant flow of flammable material occurs in both cases, but only in abnormal operation. The small release from valve-seat leakage will drift from the vent outlet, whereas release from valve operation or disc rupture will be a rapid flow. The hazardous area due to the former will therefore be a small sphere, as the drift is dispersed by wind, while that from the latter will be a plume, because the jet turbulence will be mixing. This gives the rather unusual hazardous-area distribution shown in Figure 3.19, where Zone 2 allows for wind distortion of the plume.

The result is as follows. Zone 0 is within the vent stack, but only if a relief valve is used. Zone 1 is a sphere of 1 m around the vent outlet in cases where the relief valve is used. Zone 2 is a hemisphere superimposed upon the top of the vent in all cases, plus the interior of the vent stack where a bursting disc is used. The printed note to Figure 3.19 states: 'Where release is from a bursting disc Zone 0 inside vent and Zone 1 immediately surrounding the vent, both become Zone 2'. In the figure, a = 1 m and b is calculated from Chapter 4 using the jet-release extent equation and the volume of gas or vapour released.
