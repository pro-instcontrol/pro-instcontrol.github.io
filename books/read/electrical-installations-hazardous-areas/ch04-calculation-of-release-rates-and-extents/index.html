4 — Calculation of release rates and the extents of hazardous areas 4 — Calculation of release rates and the extents of hazardous areas

From assumptions to extents: the mathematical approach to outdoor releases

Methods for determining release rates and the extents of areas of explosive atmosphere (hazardous areas) created by any given leak or leaks have long been the subject of discussion and are recognised as a difficult area of technology. There are several schools of thought and, as a result, it has proved impossible to settle upon a method which has the full confidence of all relevant interests. This has resulted in the lack of any nationally or internationally agreed method, so those involved are left to select a procedure acceptable to them from a variety of industry-based and other sectorial codes. Several attempts have been made to include bases for calculative procedures in national and international codes, but disagreements as to their accuracy have almost always resulted in a failure to agree on their inclusion. Nonetheless, those involved in the classification of hazardous areas have always had to use some form of estimation or calculation to justify the hazardous areas which they specify in each particular case; and it must be remembered that, provided the methods used produce results similar to those recorded in subjective assessment — which in the past was the only possibility — the use of a limited form of calculation as described in this chapter may be justified.

As will be seen, this chapter is not concerned with absolute accuracy but merely with a gauging operation, and the assumptions made are all aimed at giving overestimates rather than underestimates. The problem experienced in any method of area classification is the lack of information from experimentation on the mixing of releases with air when such releases are accidental rather than deliberate. This causes problems both in the subjective approach to area classification and in proving that the mathematical approach is totally valid.

Most of the mathematics which has existed for some time is based upon calculation of fluid flow from orifices specially designed to ensure the maximum flow for minimum effort — situations where flow or mixing is required for operational reasons rather than as a result of an unwanted leak. The last serious attempt to produce a mathematical approach was during the production of BS 5345 Part 2 (1975–82) and, although a relatively comprehensive set of formulae based upon classical theory and experimentation was proposed, those involved did not feel able to recommend the approach in a national document, principally because of the possibility of misuse by those not sufficiently expert or failure of such people to identify the limitations of such formulae. The approach was, however, used in at least one company code and, in general, gave results with a reasonable level of confidence which have since given no indication of inadequacy.

The latest international code, BS/EN 60079-10, which is expected to replace BS 5345 Part 2 in the relatively near future, has, in common with all its predecessors, failed to effectively approach the determination of outdoor hazardous area extents by mathematical means, although it does contain a basic mathematical relationship. Its subjective approach does, however, give some yardsticks against which mathematically produced solutions can be measured; and as this code has the confidence of most of the world's developed countries, it can serve to support the mathematical approaches included in this chapter. This is helpful because, notwithstanding the difficulty in producing an approach which will satisfy everyone, there remains the necessity to carry out the business of area classification.

For the above reasons the mathematical approaches described in this chapter are included in this book. The use of these mathematical relationships must, however, be carefully approached and only by those sufficiently expert to identify their limitations. There is no evidence that the calculations described do anything but define hazardous areas at least as large as is necessary when expertly used. This is because they are largely based on ideal releases from nozzles rather than the accidental leaks which occur due to failure of such containment elements as glands and gaskets or, in the case of those in the new IEC document, because of the many safety factors added. The basis for this mathematical approach comes from sources such as Perry and work done by Sutton and Katau based on a combination of fluid dynamics, kinetic theory of gases and practical measurements. Most of these latter formulae were reported in British Standards Institution Draft Document 79/27013 in 1979 and constituted the proposal for a calculative approach which was not adopted as earlier described.

4.1 Releases of gas and vapour 4.1 Releases of gas and vapour

The release of gas or vapour from an orifice or nozzle is given by the following equation, which is widely accepted:

G = Cd a P [(δ M/(R T)) (2/(δ+1))(δ+1)/(δ−1)]0.5 kg/s

where G = mass release, kg/s; a = cross-sectional area of the leak, m²; P = upstream pressure, N/m²; Cd = coefficient of discharge; δ = ratio of specific heats Cp/Cv; M = molecular weight; R = gas constant (8312 m·kg/(kg-mol·K)); T = absolute temperature of the released gas, K.

The ratio of specific heats (specific heat at constant pressure divided by the specific heat at constant volume) has a value of around 1.4 or less for the vast majority of flammable gases and vapours, as shown by Table 4.1. If the value 1.4 is substituted for δ the mass release equation can be simplified. In addition, the coefficient of discharge Cd has a maximum value of around 0.8 in ideal circumstances, allowing further simplification, and the final equation becomes:

(4.1)G = 0.006 a P (M/T)0.5 kg/s

This equation is, however, only valid if the upstream pressure is greater than a specific multiple of the downstream pressure (atmospheric pressure in our case), which multiple is called the critical pressure ratio. This ratio is given by the following equation:

(4.2)P/Pa = [(δ + 1)/2]δ/(δ−1)

Using the value of 1.4 for δ the critical ratio is 1.9 and so, rounding up, Equation 4.1 can be assumed to be valid only when the absolute upstream pressure exceeds 2 × 10⁵ N/m². Where this is not so the effects of atmospheric pressure become significant and the mass release equation becomes:

G = Cd a [2 σ (P − Pa)]0.5 kg/s

where σ = density of the gas at atmospheric pressure and release temperature. The density of the gas or vapour can be expressed in terms of the molecular weight and the molar volume (22.4 m³/kg-mol), giving a more recognisable equation as follows:

(4.3)G = 3.95 a [M (P − 105)/T]0.5 kg/s

The results of Equations 4.1 and 4.3 can, if required, be converted into volume release by use of the following equation:

V = Vo G T/(To M) m3/s

where Vo = molar volume, 22.4 m³/kg-mol; To = melting point of ice (273 K). This equation simplifies to:

(4.4)V = 0.082 G T/M m3/s

Because several assumptions have been made and because of the point of change from one equation to the other (Equation 4.1 to Equation 4.3), the results of the two equations will be seen to vary, but only by less than 5 per cent at the point of changeover, resulting in a small variation in the actual hazardous area defined. The changeover from Equation 4.1 to Equation 4.3 is quite rapid at 2 × 10⁵ N/m² and, because of the small variation produced at critical pressure, utilizing either equation at the pressure related to the critical pressure ratio produces an acceptable result for mass of gas released in view of the type of exercise in which we are involved. It is, however, recommended that Equation 4.3 is used up to and including pressures of 2 × 10⁵ N/m².

Calculation of the extents of hazardous areas is dependent upon the velocity of air at the point of leakage and the quantity of air which is available; if sufficient air is not available, the leak will steadily increase the amount of flammable gas or vapour in air at any point and progressively increase the extent of the hazardous area throughout the period of leakage until equilibrium is reached. Therefore, the calculative methods described in this chapter (with the exception of those given in 4.4) are generally only valid in areas which are very well ventilated and where a large amount of unconfined air is available. This tends to limit the application of the equations given for calculating the extent of hazardous areas to outdoor areas or other areas with similar levels of ventilation.

If the release velocity of the flammable gas is high compared to the typical wind or ventilation velocity in the circumstances described above, the release provides all the necessary energy for the mixing needed to achieve the required dilution; but if the release velocity is low, mixing will be due to the local wind conditions. In Europe these are normally considered to be of the order of 0.5–2 m/s. Because of the nature of release the effective cross-sectional area of the gas jet is normally less than the orifice cross-sectional area and the release velocity is higher than might at first be thought; this effect is, of course, taken care of in the derivation of Equations 4.1 and 4.3.

A further problem is that the gas or vapour expands as soon as it leaves the leak orifice. It can be assumed that at, and above, an upstream pressure equal to the critical pressure the release velocity can be demonstrated to be at least sonic — the velocity of sound in the gas or vapour in question in the prevailing conditions. The gas or vapour will expand almost instantly on release and will leave the release orifice at around the speed of sound, which velocity is given by the following equation:

vs = [δ R T/M]0.5 m/s
(4.5)vs = 108 (T/M)0.5 m/s

The distance which the jet of gas or vapour will travel before mixing with air causes the resultant mixture to fall below the lower explosive limit (LEL) in circumstances where the jet is at sonic velocity, and is given by the following equation:

X = [2 × 104 d/(E M)] × [σv/σa]0.5 m

where E = lower explosive limit (LEL), in volume per cent; σv = gas or vapour density at ambient pressure and temperature, kg/m³; σa = density of air at ambient pressure and temperature, kg/m³; d = diameter of the release orifice corrected for pressure drop to ambient at release. Assuming, as previously discussed, sonic velocity, the corrected orifice diameter is given by:

d = [4G/(π σv vs)]0.5 m

Substituting for vs from Equation 4.5 and using 1.2 kg/m³ as the density of air at ambient pressure and temperature, the distance to the LEL can be expressed as:

(4.6)X = 2.1 × 103 [G/(E2 M1.5 T0.5)]0.5 m

The above equation remains valid as long as the jet velocity on release is sufficiently high in relation to the typical wind speed specified earlier (0.5–2 m/s). Where this is not so the flow of gas or vapour ceases to be the dominant factor and its dispersal becomes more and more reliant on the atmospheric conditions local to the release. In the ultimate circumstance where the release has virtually no velocity of its own, it becomes more difficult to calculate dispersal and the extent of the hazardous area is derived from the following empirical formula produced by experimentation:

(4.7)X = 10.8 [G T/(M E)]0.55 m

This equation can only be used where the upstream pressure is such that the release velocity is not considerably higher than the wind velocity (say more than 20 times) and will not be valid unless that pressure is significantly less than the critical pressure, or the extent of hazardous area produced will be excessively large. The changeover will not be at a specific point but will be progressive, and is difficult to determine as it varies from gas to gas. A further problem occurs where the release is at a pressure above the critical pressure and an obstruction is present at a distance less than the distance to the lower explosive limit determined from Equation 4.6. A composite solution is then necessary. Since the relationship between distance from the release and concentration is linear (see Equation 4.6), Equation 4.8 can be used to determine the percentage of flammable gas or vapour in the mixture at the point of obstruction. (This is, of course, only necessary at pressures above the critical pressure.)

(4.8)B = 100 − L[(100 − LEL)/X] %

where B = percentage of gas or vapour in air; L = distance to obstruction, m; X = distance to the lower explosive limit, m.

As the volume ratio is now known, a new effective molecular weight can be calculated for the mixture as follows:

(4.9)M(Mixture) = [M(Gas) × %(Gas)/100] + [M(Air) × %(Air)/100]

For this calculation the molecular weight of air can be taken as 29.

A figure for G of the mixture can now be calculated using the original released mass of gas plus the mass of air included in the mixture at the point of obstruction.

V(Gas) = 0.082 G T/M(Gas) m³/s
(4.10)V(Mixture) = V(Gas) × [100/% Gas in Mixture] m3
(4.11)G(Mixture) = V(Mixture) × 12.19 M(Mixture)/T kg

It is now necessary to calculate a new lower explosive limit for the mixture, which can be done simply by the following calculation:

(4.12)LEL(Mixture) = LEL(Gas) × [100/% Gas in Mixture] %

These new figures can then be inserted in Equation 4.7 and a distance to the edge of the hazardous area calculated. This distance, added to the distance between the point of release and the obstruction, will define the extent of the hazardous area in the direction of the obstruction.

A further problem occurs when the release is close to the ground, as the dispersion is affected. There are no mathematical procedures for determining the effects of this, but the following are typical of the procedures adopted.

First, whatever the release pressure (above or below that defined by the critical pressure ratio), provided that the lower limit of explosive atmosphere is less than 3 m from the ground then Equation 4.7 should be used in all cases for the extent of the hazardous area and the horizontal limits should be projected to the ground (Chapter 3 deals with the results of this in more detail).

Second, where the lower extremity of the explosive atmosphere is within 1.5 m of the ground the drift release equation (Equation 4.7) should be used and, again, the extremities of the explosive atmosphere projected to ground level, but in this case the ground footprint should be multiplied by 1.5 (Chapter 3 deals with the results of this in more detail).

Third, where the lower extremity of the explosive atmosphere touches the ground the extent of the hazardous area should be that produced when the result of the drift equation (Equation 4.7) is multiplied by 1.5 to take account of the distorting effect of the ground upon dispersion of gas in all directions (Chapter 3 deals with the results of this in more detail).

The above gives a calculative approach which will effectively deal with all normal circumstances where gas or vapour is released, provided that the gas or vapour does not have a density dramatically different from air. In the case of sonic releases which are unimpeded, and therefore utilize Equations 4.1 and 4.6, density will not have a significant effect in any normal circumstance (including hydrogen), as the energy in the released gas by virtue of its velocity will overcome any significant effect of density differences. Where releases become subsonic (typically where Equations 4.3 and 4.7 are relevant), differences of density will become increasingly relevant.

It is difficult to determine the exact effect of these, but as a rule of thumb the vertical extents of explosive atmosphere should be doubled wherever the density is more than 1.5 times the density of air, or less than 0.7 times the density of air. This will mean, for instance, doubling the upward extent for such gases as hydrogen and doubling the downward direction for such gases as pentane — indeed this latter will apply for most of the hydrocarbon gases.

4.3 Release of liquid above its atmospheric boiling point 4.3 Release of liquid above its atmospheric boiling point

This is the final one of the three 'outdoor' scenarios and addresses a situation where the flammable material would be a vapour in normal ambient circumstances but is maintained as a liquid by the containment pressure. On release some of the liquid evaporates and the energy needed for that evaporation so lowers the temperature of the remainder that further evaporation occurs only as it gains heat from its environment. This gain is assumed insignificant during the 'jet' life but rapid as soon as the liquid contacts the ground; because the travel distance to the ground is short, evaporation is taken to be instantaneous on contact.

The first necessity is to calculate the amount of liquid that flashes off as vapour at the point of release, although this is not normally needed for the hazardous area at the point of release. The flashing fraction is based upon the heat capacity of the material (the heat available before the temperature falls to boiling point), the latent heat of vaporization (the heat required to vaporize it) and the difference between the boiling point and the actual temperature of the liquid:

(4.27)Fraction of liquid vaporizing = [(T1 − Tb)/δ] Cl

where T1 = release temperature of liquid, K; Tb = boiling point of liquid, K; δ = latent heat of vaporization, kJ/kg; Cl = liquid heat capacity, kJ/kg/K.

There is a problem in that mist may be formed by the nature of the release: the hazardous area created by the release is then multiplied by 1.5 to allow for this, unless that would vaporize the whole of the release, in which case total vaporization is assumed.

The release is also more complicated because vaporization can take place in the leak path if it is long, reducing the amount of material released. This is said to occur where the length of the leak path exceeds 10 times the effective diameter (for a non-circular orifice, diameter = [4 × area/π]0.5). At and above this figure the release equation is modified: the form of Equation 4.13 is retained but atmospheric pressure is replaced by Pc = 0.55 × the saturated vapour pressure of the contained liquid at process temperature. This may differ from the actual containment pressure if external pressurization is used, but normally the material is maintained as a liquid by its own vapour pressure. In addition, the density of the released mixture of liquid and vapour is the composite of the two elements of the mixture.

(4.28)G = 0.8 A [2 σm (P1 − Pc)]0.5 kg/s

where A = cross-sectional area of leak, m²; σm = density of released mixture, kg/m³; P1 = containment pressure, N/m²; Pc = 0.55 × vapour pressure, N/m².

To calculate σm it is necessary to investigate the conditions at Pc, which requires the vapour pressure curve for the material. From this a value is obtained for Tc, the temperature at which a vapour pressure of Pc would be exerted. The fraction of the release that emerges as vapour is then:

(4.29)Mg = (T1 − Tc) Cl / L

where Mg = fraction of mass release which is vapour; T1 = process temperature, K; Tc = temperature giving Pc, K; Cl = heat capacity of liquid, kJ/kg/K; L = latent heat of vaporization, kJ/kg.

This allows the density of the mixture to be calculated:

(4.30)σm = 1 / [(Mg/σv) + ((1 − Mg)/σl)] kg/m3

where σm = density of released mixture; σv = density of vapour at T1; σl = density of liquid at T1. The results give the velocity of release together with the amount of liquid in the jet. This jet can then be treated using Equations 4.14, 4.15 or 4.16 as appropriate. The amount of liquid reaching the ground is then assumed to evaporate instantly, and the extent of the hazardous area due to this calculated using Equation 4.7, corrected for a low-level point source.

4.3.1 Example of liquid release above its atmospheric boiling point 4.3.1 Example of liquid release above its atmospheric boiling point

Example 5. Liquid propylene is contained in a vessel at 15 °C and a pressure equal to its own vapour pressure at that temperature. It leaks down a drain line of 0.019 m bore (2.8 × 10−4 m² area) and 0.3 m length. Basic information: storage pressure (vapour pressure) 8.94 × 105 N/m²; process temperature 288 K; material boiling point 225 K; temperature giving Pc 267 K; latent heat of vaporization 469 kJ/kg; heat capacity of liquid at 288 K 2.26 kJ/kg/K; liquid density 520 kg/m³; vapour density 9.43 kg/m³; molecular weight 42; LEL 2 % in air.

Since 0.3/0.019 > 10, vaporization will take place within the leak path and the fluid leaking will be a mixture of vapour and liquid. First calculate the fraction vaporizing in the leak path from Equation 4.29: Mg = (288 − 267) × 2.26/469 = 0.1.

Using this fraction in Equation 4.30: σm = 1/[(0.1/9.43) + (0.9/520)] = 81.3 kg/m³.

The fraction flashing off as vapour inside and just outside the release orifice, from Equation 4.27: (288 − 225) × 2.26/469 = 0.304.

Pc = 0.55 × 8.94 × 105 = 4.92 × 105 N/m². Using the mixture density in Equation 4.28: G = 0.8 × 2.8 × 10−4 [2 × 81.3 (8.94 × 105 − 4.92 × 105)]0.5 = 1.81 kg/s.

The amount appearing as vapour at the outside of the orifice and flashing off immediately afterwards, from Equation 4.27: Gv = 1.81 (288 − 225) 2.26/469 = 0.55 kg/s. The liquid assumed to reach the ground: Gl = 1.81 − 0.55 = 1.26 kg/s.

The vapour formed at the orifice behaves as a vapour and is jet dispersed according to Equation 4.6: X = 2.1 × 103 (0.56/22 × 421.5 × 2880.5)0.5 = 11.5 m (say 12 m). Due to the nature of the release some mist is assumed and the distance is increased by 50 %: X = 18 m.

The liquid reaching the ground is assumed to vaporize immediately, so no pool forms; the vapour is wind dispersed using Equation 4.7, with no mist allowance: X = 10.8 (1.29 × 288/(42 × 2))0.55 = 24.5 m.

Printed-error note. The substitution in Equation 4.6 uses 0.56 kg/s where the calculated vapour rate is 0.55 kg/s; the substitution in Equation 4.7 uses 1.29 kg/s where the calculated liquid rate is 1.26 kg/s. The printed figures are retained.

The result is a hazardous area extending 24.5 m plus the distance travelled by the jet before it reaches the ground in a horizontal plane, and a hazardous area around the source of release extending 18 m in all above-horizontal directions, projected vertically down to the ground hazard.

The foregoing equations are used as follows.

4.4.1 Gas and vapour releases 4.4.1 Gas and vapour releases

First determine the mass release of gas or vapour using Equation 4.1 if the release pressure is greater than 2 × 105 N/m², and Equation 4.3 otherwise. Then convert this to a volume using Equation 4.4. If the release pressure is greater than 2 × 105 N/m² calculate the extent of the hazardous area using Equation 4.6; if it is less than 2 × 105 N/m² use Equation 4.7. In all cases multiply the result obtained by 1.5 if the release is within 1.5 m of the ground.

If the release is at a pressure greater than 2 × 105 N/m² but there is an obstruction within the distance calculated using Equation 4.6, calculate the amount of gas in the mixture using Equation 4.8 and then the molecular weight of the mixture using Equation 4.9, taking the molecular weight of air as 29. The total volume of the gas/air mixture at the obstruction follows from Equation 4.10. The lower explosive limit of the mixture when further mixed with air can now be calculated using Equation 4.12, after which the wind-dispersal distance is calculated using Equation 4.7 taking account of ground proximity. This is added to the distance between the release and the obstruction to give the extent of the hazardous area from the release point in the direction of the obstruction.

4.4.2 Liquid releases below boiling point 4.4.2 Liquid releases below boiling point

The mass of liquid released is calculated using Equation 4.13. The distance travelled by the jet of liquid is calculated using Equation 4.15 if the release is above the horizontal, or Equation 4.16 if it is below the horizontal; if the release trajectory is not known, use Equation 4.15. In both cases it is necessary to calculate the release velocity using Equation 4.17. Using Equation 4.19 calculate the vaporization rate and then Equation 4.23 will permit calculation of the pool area. Finally, using Equation 4.26, the extent of the hazardous area above and beyond the pool edge can be calculated.

The hazardous area can then normally be defined as the horizontal distance from the leak to the pool centre, plus the pool radius, plus the extent beyond the pool; vertically it is just the extent beyond the pool. This takes no account of mists: if these are present they should be assumed to be vapour and treated using the procedure in Section 4.4.1.

4.4.3 Liquid releases above boiling point 4.4.3 Liquid releases above boiling point

It is first necessary to calculate the amount of liquid vaporizing in the leak path using Equation 4.29 and, from this, the amount of vapour in the emerging mixture using Equation 4.27 and the mixture density of the gas/liquid mixture emerging from the release using Equation 4.30, after which the mass of mixture released can be calculated using Equation 4.28. The mass of vapour immediately outside the leak, due to that released and that flashing off immediately, is calculated using Equation 4.27 again but at atmospheric pressure; the extent of the hazardous area thus formed is calculated using Equation 4.6 taking account of ground proximity. This figure is multiplied by a further factor of 1.5 because of the likelihood of mist formation.

The remaining liquid is assumed to reach the ground; the point at which it does so is calculated as for liquids below their boiling points, using Equations 4.15 or 4.16 as appropriate. Vaporization is expected immediately contact is made with the ground, and the extent of the hazardous area is calculated as for gas/vapour using Equation 4.7 with the result multiplied by 1.5 because of ground proximity. The hazardous areas are thus around the leak due to vaporization at the leak, and at ground level with an extent from the leak of the distance travelled by the jet plus the radius of the area created by vaporization at ground level. The height of this will be the radius created by ground-level vaporization.

4.5 Releases in areas which are not well ventilated 4.5 Releases in areas which are not well ventilated

All of the foregoing calculations depend upon wind reaching all parts at around 2 m/s. In poorly ventilated areas, such as indoors, this wind does not exist in the same form and is replaced by artificial ventilation in most circumstances. In addition, the enclosed volume that limits the natural ventilation is not unlimited. Thus all of the foregoing calculations must be considered suspect unless the ventilation in all nooks and crannies of the enclosure can be considered equivalent to natural ventilation. In BS/EN 60079-103, which will replace BS 5345 Part 2, there is an attempt to define more globally the extents of and persistence of explosive atmospheres; the following equations are based upon that.

The minimum flow of air needed to dilute a given release of flammable material to below its lower explosive limit is given by Equation 4.31 as printed: Qa = 0.12 × Gf × Ta2/To × M × LEL m3/s, where Qa is the theoretical minimum air flow rate required (m³/s), Ta is ambient temperature (K), Gf is the maximum mass release rate of flammable material (kg/s), M is molecular weight and LEL is the lower explosive limit (% v/v).

(4.31)Qa = 0.12 × Gf × Ta2/To × M × LEL m3/s

This simplifies to Qa = 0.03 × Gf × Ta2/M × LEL m3/s, the form used in Example 6.

Equation 4.31 caveat. The grouping and units are reproduced as printed: the full form carries Ta2/To and a factor M × LEL with the unit m³/s. Example 6 instead evaluates 0.03 × 0.475 × 295²/(28 × 2.7), that is with LEL in the denominator, so the printed grouping is ambiguous. Neither form has been silently corrected.

If Qa can be related to the number of air changes around the source of release (i.e. the airflow velocity), a further calculation determines the size of the explosive atmosphere around the gas cloud: Vm = Qa/C m³. Because of the degree of uncertainty, two safety factors need to be added, one for the uncertainty in LEL and another for the uncertainty in ideal airflow; this gives Equation 4.32.

(4.32)Vm = Qa f/(C k) m3

Here Vm is the volume of explosive atmosphere, Qa is the airflow at the leak source, C is the number of air changes per second based on airflow around the source of release, and f is the effectiveness of ventilation: f = 1/2–4/5 (1 for ideal airflow, 2–4 for intermediate grades, 5 for the most obstructed airflow). The safety factor applied to the LEL is k = 0.25 for continuous and primary grades of release and k = 0.5 for secondary grades.

Equation 4.32 caveat. The printed unit for Vm is m³/s, which is a typo for a volume; the display uses m³ and the discrepancy is recorded here rather than changed silently.

In the case of an enclosed (defined) area, C can be calculated from Equation 4.33, where Vo is the volume of the enclosed space.

(4.33)C = Qa/Vo
Equation 4.33 caveat. The equation is printed with the unit m³, evidently a typo for an air-change rate; no unit is attached in the display. It relates to defined (enclosed) spaces only.

For the open-air situation a further equation is derived from Equation 4.32. An unrestricted volume of air outdoors of around 1000 m³ (assumed circular, as that is the worst case) is swept with air in around 6 seconds, giving C = 1/6 = 0.16; hence Equation 4.34.

(4.34)Vm = Qa/(0.16 k) m3

Due to the nature of airflow in outdoor conditions, Equation 4.34 is likely to give a pessimistic (overlarge) volume of explosive atmosphere. The series also includes Equation 4.35, intended to allow the calculation of dispersion time after the release has stopped, where LEL is the lower explosive limit (%) and UL is the maximum concentration (% v/v).

(4.35)t = (f/C) Ln (LEL k/UL) s
Equation 4.35 caveat. The printed grouping 'Ln (LEL kUL)' is ambiguous; the form shown follows the brief's reading of Example 6, Ln(0.5 × 2.7/100). That logarithm is negative and the book reports its magnitude as 27 s.

To assist in the use of this equation where ventilation is poor (indoors, where no extra ventilation is added), the text directs the reader to 'Table 4.1', said to be derived from a table in the guide book of the Building Services Institute7 used to calculate the heating needed for a building from losses due to natural ventilation.

Example 6. The ethylene release discussed in Example 1 occurs indoors in a building which is well ventilated (f = 1). The text states a mass release rate of 0.55 kg/s; the substitution that follows uses 0.475 kg/s.

4.6 Conclusion 4.6 Conclusion

This chapter contains a relatively comprehensive set of calculations for determining the extents of hazardous areas. Those concerned with outdoor areas for vapours and for liquids have been extensively used for several years and basically agree with the subjective judgements found in several codes, and thus have some pedigree. The formulae in Section 4.4, however, are newer.

Until they have been in use for a considerable time and evidence has amassed, they should be used with extreme care. It is suggested that initially they be used as yardstick calculations only (for example, to determine whether the persistence time in a particular circumstance would cause a secondary source of release to produce a Zone 1 indoors). It is stressed that there is no intent to suggest that they are not accurate, but merely that experience must be accumulated by practising engineers. This set of formulae will also be more difficult to use in cases of liquid release, particularly where the liquid is below its boiling point.
