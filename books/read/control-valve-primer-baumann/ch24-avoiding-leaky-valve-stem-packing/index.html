Avoiding Leaky Valve Stem Packing

Hans D. Baumann · Control Valve Primer, 5th ed. (ISA / Wiley)

Chapter 24 · 6 source pages · 1 figures · companion notes
Key terms. valve stem packing · leakage · stem friction · dead time · Teflon V-ring · Chevron packing · TFE-asbestos packing · Kalrez · lubricator · split ring · packing follower · thermal expansion coefficient · effective packing depth · valve travel · groove wear · rotary valve · misalignment · bellows seal · backup packing · telltale connection · internal/external pressurization · double-walled bellows · hydrostatic line test · water hammer · fatigue · pressure pulsation · pulsation damper · mechanical damage · packless valve

A valve stem packing is one of the most important elements of a control valve, yet one that is not fully understood. A worn packing causes external leakage, but a tight packing can make the controller go unstable. The combination of low friction and low stem leakage is what makes a good packing — low leakage, not zero leakage, because there is no such thing as zero leakage.

Packing materials and installation practice Packing materials and installation practice

In former times when asbestos was the standard packing material, packing boxes had lubricators whereby the maintenance people could inject grease into the packing, which not only reduced friction but also provided a high-viscosity interface between the packing bore and the stem exterior to improve sealability. Any maintenance department had to stock a variety of packing greases with varying viscosities (depending on service temperature) specially composed to resist corrosive attacks by certain fluids.

Teflon changed all that, and the all-Teflon V-ring or Chevron packing, preferred by some, and the tetrafluoroethylene (TFE)-lubricated asbestos packing, preferred by others, became the industry standard. Now, a variety of plastic materials, such as Kalrez, are also available. The TFE-graphite or TFE-Kevlar packings now current are made in the form of a split-ring and enable the emergency repair of a valve in the line by slipping one or two extra rings under the packing follower. The disadvantage was, and still is, that its tightness depends on the “feel” of the maintenance person. Many a carefully tuned process controller has gone unstable because of added stem friction (increased valve dead time) due to an over-tightened stem packing.

Rules for avoiding trouble with valve stem packing Rules for avoiding trouble with valve stem packing

  1. Make sure the packing is tight before placing the valve in operation. Some manufacturers ship the valve with a loose packing flange or nut.
  2. If hot fluids are involved, retighten the packing after the valve has fully warmed up when first placed in service.
  3. During installation, do not rotate the actuator (e.g., to change positioner location) without loosening the packing before moving the actuator. Realize that the center of the stem and the center of the packing are not perfectly aligned. A loose packing will adapt to a change in the center.
  4. Do not allow the control loop or actuator to become unstable for a long time. Remember, even one cycle per second cyclic motion can translate into 2½ million cycles of packing wear in only one month! The packing is completely worn at that time.
  5. Do not use packing material that has a high coefficient of thermal expansion in a system containing steam or hot fluids.
  6. Total effective packing depth should always exceed the length of valve travel. This will avoid creating a groove inside the packing bore in case there is some “nick” on the surface of the valve stem.
  7. Packing for rotary valves is superior in performance to those for reciprocating stem valves as long as the rotary valve shaft is well supported to absorb the perpendicular actuator thrust or the off-center strain caused by a misaligned stem-coupled rotary actuator.
  8. Misalignment between the valve stem and the actuator stem causes wear and friction. This can happen when an actuator yoke, made from steel or aluminum, bends following a drop during shipment. Cast iron yokes cannot do this — they break. Misalignment can also happen when a heavy actuator is mounted in a horizontal position without proper support.

How much leakage to expect How much leakage to expect

Although no packing is tight, remember that the permeation rate [1] through solid steel alone is already within the measurement sensitivity of mass spectrometers. So, do not expect too much. Here are some rough examples of what you can expect in the form of packing leakage:

  • A typical 5/16-inch stem diameter packing with solid TFE Chevron rings (preloaded) exhibited a stem leakage when subjected to 55 psi air at 70°F of about 0.4 scc/min (standard cubic centimeters per minute) after 100,000 full travel cycles, or about 550 scc/day, which is roughly 2 × 10⁻⁵ kg/h and is still below the 500 parts/million limit. The same valve exhibited a leakage of 1 × 10⁻⁵ kg/h of water under the same conditions. These test data are based on laboratory measurements and exclude environmental effects such as corrosion or dirt, which make things worse in real life.
  • There are many 100,000 full travel cycles in a stable control loop situation, and a valve packing may not need to be replaced for several years. In other cases, a tight maintenance schedule must be instituted. You may want to consider that the packing wear equivalent to 100,000 × 1/2 in travel cycles is 625 days of operation. However, an unstable valve with average stem excursions of ±0.30 in at a rate of two cycles per minute accumulates 1.05 million cycles in a year! This demonstrates the need for a stable loop and a stable actuator.
  • When all else fails, specify a packless valve such as a diaphragm valve (metal or elastomers), a pinch valve (elastomers only), a flexible sleeve valve (thermoplastic elastomer, plastic, or elastomer), or a bellows seal valve.

Bellows seals Bellows seals

The use of bellows (see Figure 24-1) is one method for avoiding valve stem leakage and meeting Environmental Protection Agency–recognized avoidance of hazardous leakage from valves [2] (other methods include the use of diaphragm and pinch valves). Bellows seals work well if properly maintained and not overstressed. They should always be used with a backup packing and a telltale connection between the bellows seal and packing that leads to a pressure switch or pressure gauge to inform or alarm the operator in case of bellows failure. Bellows pressurized from the outside typically give higher pressure ratings.

Finally, double-walled bellows are available for added leakage protection. Note: Removability of the bellows requires a good gasket at the interface between the static portion of the bellows and the bonnet.

Causes of bellows failure Causes of bellows failure

Overpressurization Overpressurization

This is usually caused by subjecting the valve with bellows to hydrostatic line test pressure for which typical bellows are not designed (consult the manufacturer). Other sources of excess pressure are pressure waves such as “water hammer,” which are caused by a sudden valve opening or the thermal expansion of liquid trapped in the valve and pipe due to ambient temperature changes (a relief valve may be required).

Fatigue Fatigue

This can be caused by excess valve cycles. Most bellows are rated for a minimum of 100,000 full travel cycles, which means many years of normal throttling service (two full open-to-closure cycles per day will yield 100,000 cycles in 137 years!). However, if this valve is used in an on-off mode at one cycle every 5 min, 200,000 cycles are reached in about 1 year.

However, fatigue problems can be more subtle. For example, having the bellows subjected to the pressure fluctuations of a positive displacement pump operating at 100 cycles per minute can partly deflect the individual bellows convolutions at a rate of 1 million cycles per week and subsequently break the bellows (here a pulsation damper downstream of the pump is the answer). An unstable, cycling electronic control signal to the actuator can do the same damage by subjecting the stem and bellows to millions of minute cycles.

Mechanical damage Mechanical damage

This is usually caused by improper installation or maintenance. The most common problem is the twisting of the bellows when the valve stem is turned. A good valve design prevents the possibility of twisting the bellows during assembly or disassembly of the valve. Remember: Typically, instructions are read only after things have gone wrong! Also, be careful when removing the actuator; you might inadvertently push or pull the valve stem (with the attached bellows) above the normal travel distance.

Pressurization of the exterior of the bellows increases resistance to working pressure by about 50%. However, this requires the retaining bonnet to be pressure and/or corrosion-resistant. Adding balancing pressure inside the bellows will increase the pressure rating. However, the fluid has to be inert, and in the case of liquids, adequate relief must be provided to allow for venting displaced volume during valve travel.
