Flashing

Hans D. Baumann · Control Valve Primer, 5th ed. (ISA / Wiley)

Chapter 23 · 2 source pages · 1 figures · companion notes
Key terms. flashing · vapor pressure Pv · outlet pressure · vacuum · dissolved vapor · undissolved vapor · cavitation · choked flow · water/vapor mixture velocity · velocity head · flash tank · discharge to open air · flow coefficient Cv · flow rate Q (gpm) · absolute inlet pressure P1 (psia) · vapor-pressure factor Ff · critical pressure Pc = 3206 psia · pressure recovery factor FL · 3-inch eccentric ball valve

Flashing is defined as a liquid flow condition where the outlet pressure drops at, or below, the vapor pressure of the liquid. The vapor created during the high-velocity phase remains dissolved, lacking the necessary pressure differential to cause cavitation.

Definition and mechanism Definition and mechanism

Figure 23-1 shows a water jet exiting a valve outlet. The photograph clearly shows the undissolved vapor mixed with the water. This vapor will later harmlessly dissolve back into water in the open air. The sound level of such a mixture is turbulent and can be estimated using the pressure drop ratio of 1. Flashing will occur whenever the downstream pressure is in a vacuum or at the vapor pressure of a specific fluid.

Flashing poses no problem except when the resultant backpressure due to the high velocity of the water/vapor pressure causes cavitation. Assume that the downstream pressure is 7.75 psia (0.5 bara). Under these conditions, the amount of entrained water vapor is 5% of the total mass. The result is that the ratio between the water and vapor is 290:1. Ts means that the total flowing mass is 14 times that of the liquid alone. Assuming that the normal liquid pipe velocity is 10 ft/s (3 m/s), the combined water/vapor velocity increases to 140 ft/s (42 m/s). The resultant velocity head of this mixture now is 8 psi (0.6 bara). This, together with the 7.75 psia vacuum, can cause cavitation either in the downstream pipe or at the valve outlet.

Unfortunately, this process has often been misunderstood and flashing has been blamed for the resultant damage. To prevent this from happening, always mount the valve outlet directly on a flash tank or in the open and avoid all downstream piping. Here, water vapor stays in the solution. Because of the atmospheric pressure or vacuum, there is not enough pressure differential to cause the vapor to implode.

How to size for flashing conditions How to size for flashing conditions

Cv = Q / [(P1 − Pv × Ff)0.5 / FL]
  • Q = gpm
  • P1 = psia
  • Ff = 0.96 − 0.28 (Pv / Pc)0.5 Pv
  • Pc = 3206 psia

Example Example

Q = 500 gpm, P1 = 60 psia, Pv = 10 psia, Pc = 3206 psia, FL = 0.6.

Cv = 500 / {(60 − 10 × (0.96 − 0.28 (10 / 3206)0.5 × 10))0.5 / 0.6}
Cv = 117

A 3-inch eccentric ball valve could be used.

Source quirks. The OCR text layer garbles the exponent placement in the sizing relation; the formula and example here were re-read from the rendered printed pages 145-146. The printed photograph of flashing was redrawn as a vector schematic with the outlet, the two-phase jet and the vapor redissolving in open air. The printed example states Cv = 117, but substituting its own figures into the printed relation gives about 41.6; the printed result is retained.
