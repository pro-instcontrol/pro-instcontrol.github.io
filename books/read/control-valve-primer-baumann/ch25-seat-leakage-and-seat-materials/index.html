To save money and piping space, most users look for a control valve that can double as a shutoff valve. Alas, this is hard to accomplish.

Seat Leakage and Seat Materials Seat Leakage and Seat Materials

First, to be “drip tight” (ANSI/FCI Class VI, see Table 25-1), there has to be a soft interface between the seat ring and plug, such as an elastomer or a plastic material. This then excludes high-temperature applications.

Second, control valves throttle all the time. This means that seating surfaces can be subjected to very high fluid velocities that can cause erosion. Often there are entrained particles within the controlled fluid that hasten the wear. This implies that whatever the manufacturer certified as measured leakage when the valve left the plant may not be applicable when the valve is put in service.

Nevertheless, there are applications where a control valve can be used as a shutoff device, even if only in an emergency. A steam bypass valve in an atomic power plant comes to mind. There are other noncritical loop applications where a small leakage can be tolerated, where the amount of leaked fluid has no material impact on the process, and there is no safety hazard.

Another criterion is rangeability. The minimum flow rate that you want to control should not be less than the leakage rate (see Table 25-2). This can be a consideration with small flow valves or swing-through butterfly valves. The type of seat material often determines the expected seat leakage rate. As mentioned previously, a soft seating material will provide the lowest leakage rate, typically Class VI of ANSI B16.104-1976 (FCI 70-2).

Elastomers such as Buna N are used for moderate temperature (to 180°F), while Teflon is widely used when corrosive fluids are involved. The temperature limit for Teflon is 450°F. Attention should be given if Teflon is used as seating material in certain rotary valves such as butterfly or ball valves. Static or “break-away” friction can sometimes be two or more times the operating friction (dynamic friction). It may take considerable actuator force to overcome this static friction leading to temporary valve instability.

Metal-to-metal seats and seat-material selection Metal-to-metal seats and seat-material selection

The same may be said about some metal-seated valves such as high-performance butterfly valves, although the problem is less severe. Metal-to-metal seat valves are used in nearly all globe control valves. For low pressure drops (up to 500 psi), Type 316 stainless steel is the preferred choice where the usual leakage rate for single-seated valves is Class IV and for double-seated valves Class II. Type 440 C is a hardened stainless steel often used for steam applications up to 800°F. A cobalt alloy such as Stellit is the preferred seat material for higher temperatures (up to 1500°F) and high pressures. Again, the typical seat leakage (single seat) conforms to Class IV, although, as with the stainless steel seating, Class V can be met with special lapping techniques.

The Class V leakage specification using gas as a test medium is a relatively new standard. The allowable quantities are derived from the water test volumes assuming laminar flow conditions. For example, with a 4-inch seat diameter, the allowable leakage is 4.7 × 4 = 18.8 cc/min.

How Does a Valve Close? How Does a Valve Close?

There are two distinct sealing mechanisms in control valves: push-down seals (such as in globe valves) and the sliding seals (found in butterfly and eccentric rotary ball valves). Push-down seals require extra force to stop leaking, whereas sliding seals require extra rotation beyond the point where fluid flow is stopped. This fact must be realized when calibrating valve positioners. Positioners for globe valves in the fail close position should be calibrated at 4 psi (5 mA) when the plug sits on the seat (to stop flow), so when the signal drops to 3 psia (4 mA), there is sufficient force from the actuator to stop the leakage. For fail open mode, signal selection should be 14 and 15 psi (18 and 20 mA), respectively.

Rotary valves need about five extra degrees beyond fluid stopping to engage a sliding seal sufficiently to stop leakage. Again, allow at least one extra signal psi beyond the point of fluid stoppage.

If you do all this correctly, there will be no leakage in the field. Realize too, actual fluid pressures in service are much higher than the 50 psi test pressure in the shop.
