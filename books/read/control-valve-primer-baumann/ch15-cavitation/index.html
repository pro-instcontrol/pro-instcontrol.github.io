Cơ chế xâm thực The cavitation mechanism

I used to say, only half in jest, that cavitation is the manufacturer's best friend because it supplies a steady demand for replacement trim parts. No wonder, because the mechanism of cavitation (violent collapse of vapor bubbles in a liquid following deceleration of the liquid flow and subsequent pressure recovery) can cause impact pressures on the surface of the valve plug, the inner body wall, or the pipe wall up to 1.5 × 10³ N/mm² for water (218,000 psi!). There is no material known to man that will withstand cavitation damage. However, a harder material (higher elastic stress limit) will last longer than a softer material. For example, here are the relative lifetimes of some materials (same material loss under the same cavitation conditions) [1]:

MaterialRelative lifetime
Soft aluminum alloy1
Type 1020 carbon steel2.4
Type 304 stainless steel6.5
Hardened 42% chromium (Cr), 4% molybdenum (Mo) steel13

Cavitation, the violent implosion of tiny vapor bubbles in a fluid stream, probably is the primary cause for maintenance on control valves that handle the pressure reduction of liquids. It is therefore important to understand this phenomenon and to be able to calculate the resultant noise as an indicator of the degree of destruction that it may cause. The following is an explanation of the physical process involved in cavitation.

Note. One might believe that only water cavitates. This is wrong. Cavitation also happens with valves controlling crude oil in pipelines.

When the downstream pressure in a valve decreases at and beyond a corresponding pressure ratio called X_Fz, the static pressure in the valve's orifice reaches the vapor pressure of the liquid. This causes evaporation of some of the liquid with the simultaneous formation of small vapor bubbles. At this point, the liquid reaches its highest velocity. After passing the orifice, the liquid velocity starts to decrease while the static pressure increases. When the liquid static pressure exceeds vapor pressure, the bubbles start to implode, creating sonic shock waves. This causes acoustic sound waves that follow the laws of gas acoustics and reach sound levels up to 125 dB.

X = (P1 − P2) / (P1 − Pv)

Further reduction in outlet pressure causes more of the diameter of the jet to enlarge and its velocity to decrease in proportion to the increased static pressure following the laws of Bernoulli (assuming constant inlet pressure). Note that there is choked flow (that is, the orifice velocity stays constant during cavitation). The resultant sound pressure from the cavitation increase corresponds to 60 log(X/X_Fz) following the acoustic gas laws; here X is a pressure ratio as given above, and X_Fz is a coefficient of incipient cavitation. Figure 15-1 shows the relationship between X_Fz and the resultant sound level.

Cavitation avoidance Cavitation avoidance

This process can be avoided in most cases by selecting a high F_L type trim and by relocating the valve to a point of higher static pressure, by placing an additional restrictor downstream, or by injecting air or gas into the valve near the plug or vane (see Figure 15-2). Selecting a hardened trim will only prolong the service and, of course, will increase the revenue of your vendor from the sale of spare parts (see also Chapter 14). Some cavitation, especially with hot water (condensate), is due to placing the valve too far away from the flash tank.

I contributed new knowledge about the understanding and sound estimation of cavitation in Figure 15-1.

  1. Cavitation is an aerodynamic process independent of the underlying turbulence. This can be seen by the 60 log(X) slope of the curve between X_Fz and Xy (the pressure drop X where max cavitation occurs).
    60 log(X)
  2. A maximum of cavitation sound is achieved when the internal jet velocity head and the static head are equal at Xy. Here
    Xy = (1 + X_Fz) / 2
  3. The external turbulent sound profile is equal to 30 log(X) up to X = F_L².
    30 log(X) (X ≤ F_L²)
  4. The acoustical efficiency factor for liquids is: (Uvc/ci) × 10⁻⁴. Here Uvc = jet velocity and ci = speed of sound of liquid.
    (Uvc / ci) × 10⁻⁴

Application pointer Application pointer

Problems can arise when faced with high-pressure let-down applications, such as releasing 1000 psi water to the atmosphere. The normal wisdom is to specify a multistage anticavitation valve with assumed six identical stages, each with a pressure ratio (X) of 0.25. This leaves a final pressure drop at the last stage of 180 psia to 14.5 psia.

Rather than such an expensive solution, I recommend using a conventional globe valve and reducing the pressure in one sloop. This valve then has a pressure ratio of X = 0.99.

The graph in Figure 15-1 indicates that there is no more cavitation at a pressure ratio of 0.99 and therefore no damage or cavitation noise (there still is turbulent noise but at a much lower level).

What happens with the 1000 psi kinetic energy? It still is gone by producing millions of tiny vapor bubbles. However, because the downstream pressure is 14.5 psia, there is insufficient pressure difference to make them implode; therefore, they quietly turn back into liquid. Here, we have a process of “flashing.”

Note. This scheme only works if the valve discharges directly into an open tank or into air to avoid back pressure.

Test data for an anticavitation butterfly valve (Figure 15-3) are shown in Figure 15-4. Note the exceptionally high X_Fz factor of 0.64, resulting in a cavitation amplitude of only 6.4 dB at a pressure ratio of 0.82. A conventional butterfly valve with an X_Fz of 0.3 (Xy = 0.65) would see a maximum cavitation sound level of 110 dB under the same flow conditions.

Damage caused by cavitation Damage caused by cavitation

Figure 15-5 shows the type of damage caused by cavitation. In this case, prolonged cavitation ate away one-half of the original parabolic valve plug. Similar destruction happened at the corresponding valve orifice.

Destruction similar to that shown in Figure 15-5 happens when the external sound level exceeds 80 dBA. Jon Monsen [2] suggests a sliding scale such as 85 dBA for valve sizes 4 in to 6 in, 90 dBA for sizes 8 in to 14 in, and 95 dBA for larger sizes.

Even though hardened trim such as ceramics or Stellite can prolong service life, there is no known valve rim material that can withstand cavitation damage due to the extremely high-pressure waves (exceeding 100,000 psi) of the imploding vapor bubbles.
