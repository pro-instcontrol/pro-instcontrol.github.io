Khí thải thoát hưu Fugitive emissions

Our enhanced concern for a clean air environment has significantly toughened the allowable amount of emissions of fluids or gases from control valves.

Control valves typically have two places of possible leakage:

  1. The gasket between a valve housing and bolted bonnet or threaded closures to define a pressure-tight seal
  2. Packing on sliding or rotary valve stems (see Chapter 24)

Mặt ghép thân–nắp van và bộ lót dạng lồng Body–bonnet joints and cage trim

The first problems can more easily be solved by using proper gaskets (flexible ring gaskets with multiple metal rings interspaced by deformable plastics such as Teflon are popular). The bolts depressing those gaskets must be strong enough not only to maintain the maximum fluid pressure but also to deform the gasket sufficiently to eliminate tiny voids. The minimum bolting load should be 2.5 times what is required to contain the pressure.

Attention must be paid when bonnet flanges simultaneously retain a cage trim. When the cage material has a higher coefficient of thermal expansion than that of the housing and hot fluid is encountered, there have been instances where the cage tried to lift the bonnet from the housing and caused seal leakage.

Nắp van kéo dài Extended bonnet

An extended bonnet is always helpful. In the case of liquids, it cools the temperature of the liquid at the packing. This increases the viscosity and thus reduces leakage. In the case of steam, a longer bonnet will condense the steam into water, which again reduces packing leakage. However, this method only works when a valve is installed upside down; otherwise, the condensed liquid drains back into the valve housing.

Ma sát đệm chèn và ổn định vòng điều khiển Packing friction and loop stability

Packing friction can affect the loop stability. Here is a rule of thumb: Typical stem friction forces due to packing friction can be estimated as the cross-sectional area of the stem times the inlet pressure.

F_friction ≈ A_stem × P1

For example, a 1/2-inch stem diameter and 100 psi inlet pressure may have a friction force of 19.5 lb. Using a 500-square-inch diaphragm actuator, this force could create a deadband of ±0.04 psi, or 0.6% of span, quite tolerable with a valve positioner. These values are based on a spring-loaded Teflon V-ring packing.

A_stem = π(0.5 in)² / 4 ≈ 0.196 in²
F_friction ≈ 0.196 in² × 100 psi ≈ 19.5 lb
Deadband ≈ ±19.5 lb / 500 in² = ±0.04 psi ≈ 0.6% of span

Using graphite as packing material will produce several times higher values. It is common to spring load packing followers to achieve constant friction and to compensate for thermal expansion and future wear.

Đệm chèn thân quay Rotary stem packing

Rotary stem packing has advantages. Stem abrasions due to particles in the fluid, which can lead to longtime leakage problems with reciprocating stems, are avoided. Instead, for a force, rotary packing friction produces extra torque.

For example, a 1-inch stem may have 80 lb of stem friction. This would translate into 40 in-lb of torque. Assuming a rotary actuator producing 100 in-lb of torque per psi, it would require 0.4 psi of signal change.

T = F × r = 80 lb × 0.5 in = 40 in-lb
Δsignal = 40 in-lb / (100 in-lb/psi) = 0.4 psi
