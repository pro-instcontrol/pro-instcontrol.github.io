When using a plastic-lined metal valve body, make sure the surrounding housing is pressure-tight and that a secondary stem packing and telltale connection are provided to protect against liner or plastic bellows failure. Bellows made of tetrafluoroethylene (TFE) — Teflon have particularly limited life cycles and are permeable to chlorine.

Valve end connections may be threaded in sizes 2 in and below for nonhazardous fluids but should be flanged for the handling of chemicals. High-pressure steam valves usually are welded into the pipe, as are some aseptic valves in bioprocessing systems.

While it is usually OK to have your favorite valve vendor select the valve type (which saves me from adding all the usual catalog pages to this volume), base your final judgment on the criteria mentioned here. The maintenance and past experience of a given type of valve is always an important consideration.

One of the most embarrassing things that can happen is seeing a valve you chose six months earlier on the trash heap and then listening to unflattering remarks made by the maintenance supervisor.

The most common causes of premature valve failure Các nguyên nhân thường gặp nhất gây hỏng van sớm

By far, the most often encountered causes of premature valve failures are:

  • Cavitation damage (usually high-pressure drop with water);
  • Wire drawing of trim (cavitation or flashing of water, condensate);
  • Erosion of trim (embedded solids in fluid);
  • Plug vibration (unstable flow pattern with liquids);
  • Corrosion(corrosive fluids);
  • Excessive stem packing leakage (valve/actuator may be cycling).

Cavitation damage can be avoided by selecting a low-pressure recovery trim (high FL), by installing a secondary flow restrictor, or by choosing a better valve location. Wire drawing of trim is usually encountered with wet steam (condensate). Here the carbon steel of valve housings can get eaten away underneath the stainless-steel seat ring if there is no gasket or weld seal. The moral: Always specify chrome-molybdenum alloy or stainless steel as the body material where saturated water is encountered or where you suspect steam may condense (practically any process steam line that gets shut down at night or on weekends).

For the third problem, erosion of trim, specify hardened trim or use angle valves. There are, in my book, only two cases when you should use an angle valve: (1) for flashing condensate at the inlet of a condenser and (2) for erosive fluids. In both cases, the flow should be in the direction to close the plug (check the actuator for stability). For erosive service, use a reduced trim and a long, straight, (ideally) oversized downstream pipe to avoid particle impingement on the pipe interior.

Plug vibration can sometimes be cured by reversing the flow direction; otherwise, increase the stem diameter or reduce the weight (mass) of the valve plug. Corrosion can be dealt with when selecting materials with the aid of a good handbook (see Chapter 13, “How Good Is the Valve I Purchased?”). For hints on solving the problem of excessive stem packing leakage, see Chapter 24, “Avoiding Leaky Valve Stem Packing.”

Larger globe valves and the cage design Van cầu cỡ lớn và thiết kế kiểu lồng

Larger globe valves may have a pressure-balanced plug and could be cage-guided. Figure 3-1 is a typical example of an improved cage valve design. Cage valves have several advantages. They are easier to maintain because removal of a seat ring is relatively easy compared to removal of a threaded seat ring in a single-seated globe valve, especially after years of service. Pressure-balanced plugs greatly reduce the stem forces and can save considerable amounts of money because smaller actuators can be used.

However, some problems can arise. First, cage-guided plugs are susceptible to entrained particles leading to friction and stickiness. Second, there can be a problem with thermal expansion of the cage on high-temperature service that could result in crushing (leakage) of the cage gaskets. Third, the cost of a cage valve body (and its weight) is higher than that of a single-seated globe valve due to the heavy bonnet bolting. Finally, most cage plugs (pistons) are sealed against the gauge bore using piston rings (either metal or plastics), which can cause considerable friction during operation and lead to deadbands. The positioner may have to be de-tuned to prevent cycling of the actuator (valve instability). Such valve actuator instability should not be confused with instability (hunting) of the control loop, although both may interact.

Dual-stage throttling — overcoming the cage valve's drawbacks Tiết lưu hai cấp — khắc phục nhược điểm của van lồng

The diagram of an idealized cage valve in Figure 3-1 shows how the inherent disadvantages of cage valves can be overcome. Most intriguing is the concept of dual-stage throttling. Here, instead of the plug having a conventional contoured surface (which determines its flow characteristic), the contoured surface is now part of a radially expanding inner cage wall, located just above the valve seat.

The clearance between the tip of the plug (lower arrow of letter A) and the contoured inner cage wall now becomes the first throttling stage. At the same time, we see that a second protrusion of the plug (upper arrow of letter A) starts to expose a portion of a window cast into the cage. The resultant opening thus becomes the second stage of this dual throttling element. Fluid will pass past the valve seat and lower tip of the plug into a concave indentation, and from there, restricted by upper point A, into the open window areas and finally exiting the valve body.

The valve plug allows for double-stage throttling (A in Figure 3-1) for increased cavitation resistance and high rangeability. Friction that causes sliding seals is avoided by using a flexible top seal (B) allowing for balanced forces and for enduring machining tolerances and thermal expansion. A similar feature is used to seal the bonnet against the cage by using a compressible top seal (C). The cage is screwed into the housing (D) eliminating the heavy bolting required to compress two gaskets on conventional cage valves. Finally, the stem connection to the plug is staked (E) instead of using a pin, which may become loose [1].

Smaller globe valves Van cầu cỡ nhỏ

Smaller globe valves usually feature single-seated designs as shown in Figure 3-2. These are much simpler and can use smaller actuators because of low stem forces due to smaller orifices and generally lower pressures. This also allows for smaller stem diameters and lower packing friction (less deadband). Many of these valves can operate without a positioner if operated using a 3 to 15 psi pneumatic signal.

Rotary control valves Van điều khiển quay

Rotary control valves fall into two broad categories:

  • 1. Those designed exclusively for modulating control
  • 2. On-off valves modified by the addition of an actuator and positioner (also called automated valves)

Valves in the first category have many features commonly seen in globe valves, such as adjustable stem packing, the option of a hard-faced trim, extension bonnets, and, most important, a backlash-free connection between the rotating “closure member” and the actuator stem. Some more refined types even have two-stage pressure reduction at low travel, as well as field-attachable, low-noise, anticavitation inserts (see Figure 3-3).

The second category, while admittedly less expensive, can be perilous. Here the main concern should be the backlash or play between

  • the ball (in case of a ball valve) and the valve stem tang1;
  • the valve stem and the actuator stem tang; and
  • the positioner feedback connection and the actuator stem tang.
1 The flattened portion at the end of a valve stem.

Here is an example to illustrate the abovementioned problems: Assuming the valve has a 1-inch stem diameter and 0.015-inch tang clearance, a little mathematics will prove that the actuator will have to move 2.5° or 2.8% of 90° travel before the ball will move in the opposite direction. This is acceptable in some applications, but not for critical control.

Another area of concern is “breakaway” friction with some ball or butterfly valves. This is overcoming the static friction of the ball or disk in order to open the valve, a problem especially irksome on so-called high-performance butterfly valves. On start-up, the actuator might be so charged up with air pressure that the valve may fly almost wide open (after breaking out of the seat) before the positioner can move the valve back to the desired position.

A good rule to remember is to limit the normal valve travel of the ball and high-performance butterfly valves to 15% of the maximum opening, that is, make sure your flow rate does not require a Cv (flow coefficient) that is less than that given for a 15% opening.

Criteria for Valve Selection: A Summary Tiêu chí lựa chọn van: Tóm tắt

Ability to Control Well (Performance) Khả năng điều khiển tốt (Hiệu năng)

  • Is the valve sized properly?
  • Does the valve have low deadband, sometimes confused with hysteresis?
  • Will it be able to operate without a positioner (also a cost factor)? Does it have adequate rangeability?
  • Is the flow characteristic acceptable (installed gain)?
  • How is its dynamic performance (frequency response, time constant)?

Installation and Effects on the Environment Lắp đặt và ảnh hưởng đến môi trường

  • Is the face-to-face such that it can be replaced with another make? How heavy is it? Are pipe supports needed? Is it too tall?
  • Does it shut off tightly (if required)?
  • Is it going to be noisy under the given flow conditions? Would packing leakage produce a hazard?
  • Is there adequate air pressure (in case of cylinder actuators)? Can it withstand the corrosive effects of fluid and the environment? Would the valve be cavitating under the given flow conditions?
  • If outdoors, can the valve and positioner stand extreme weather conditions?

Maintainability and Long-Term Cost Control Khả năng bảo trì và kiểm soát chi phí dài hạn

  • Is it easy to repair?
  • What are the costs of spare parts?
  • Can the positioner handle dirty air (if applicable)? Can the positioner tolerate a vibrating environment? How is the actuator protected from corrosion?
  • Is the stem packing field-replaceable? Must the valve be drained?
  • Is the positioner able to diagnose the valve?

Only after reviewing these criteria and deciding on the proper valve type should the subject of cost be considered.
