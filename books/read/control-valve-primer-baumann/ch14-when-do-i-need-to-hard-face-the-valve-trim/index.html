When Do I Need to “Hard Face” the Valve Trim and Other Questions Concerning Valve Material

Xói mòn — nguyên nhân chính gây hỏng van Erosion — the main cause of valve failure

Besides corrosion, which can be handled by consulting a good handbook when selecting a suitable material for wetted parts, erosion is the cause of most valve failures. We must distinguish the causes of erosion to affect a durable solution.

Erosion caused by solid particles in the fluid stream or granular materials such as coal slurry Erosion caused by solid particles in the fluid stream or granular materials such as coal slurry

If the velocity of the erosive fluid is low enough (e.g., less than corresponding to a 10 psi valve pressure drop), then elastomer lining works well, particularly with natural rubber or GRS (Buna-S) types of elastomers, and a pinch valve or rubber-lined butterfly valve should be considered.

Remember, there must be a pressure drop across the valve of at least 1 psi to keep particles in suspension (3 to 7 ft/s velocity). For higher pressure drop or where temperature is elevated, consider angle valves in the “flow tending-to-close the plug” direction. Try to discharge the flow into a downstream pipe that has a diameter of at least four times the orifice size to avoid cutting through the pipe wall. Hard facing of the seating surfaces, the seat ring bore, and the outer contour of the plug is a good practice to prolong service life. For tight shutoff, it is best if there is a difference in hardness between the plug (40 to 46 RC)1 and seat ring (56 to 62 RC). The same can be said for the hardness difference between the plug guide and the guide bushing. For smaller size plugs, solid ceramic (ALO2) or carbides [1] (such as tungsten carbide) are effective. However, the key is to streamline the fluid flow in all areas that approach the plug/seat restriction (vena contracta) and to avoid anything that would cause direct fluid impact on adjoining valve housing surfaces. Ideally, only the valve orifice should accelerate the fluid.

Footnote 1. Rockwell C-Scale hardness.

The actual deceleration (pressure drop) with the resulting destructive high turbulence should take place inside a large pipe or vessel downstream—but not close to the valve plug.

For an example of how brief the service life of valve plugs and seat rings can be, see Reference 2, in which a 6-inch seat ring made from 316 stainless steel was completely destroyed within a few weeks while handling fine clay in suspension in hot oil at a pressure drop of only 50 psi. (I suspect that the valve also underwent cavitation, which, of course, accelerated the destruction.) In another example, a fully Stellite No. 1–coated, 1½-inch-diameter valve plug was completely worn away by uranium ore slurry within 2 weeks, even though the pressure drop was only 100 psi.

Remember, the hardened and expensive valve trim will only delay the destruction, not prevent it. In some cases, it is more cost-effective to buy the cheapest valve possible and to replace it on a regular basis. This has been the practice with the control of bauxite slurry where a simple carbon steel butterfly valve was used and periodically replaced.

Erosion by wet steam Erosion by wet steam

On one side of the temperature scale is water condensate, which attacks carbon steel. Therefore, only use chrome-molly alloys, bronze, or stainless steel as valve body material, or make sure a gasket is placed under the stainless-steel seat ring in case a carbon steel body is employed. On the other side is superheated steam, which can discharge water droplets during isentropic throttling in a valve. Here the use of martensitic, chromium stainless steel trim is recommended, especially AISI type 440C, which can be hardened to about 58 RC. For lower temperatures (<750°F), 17-4 pH stainless steel is also useful if heat treated, usually at 1075°F, yielding a hardness of about 38 RC. Watch the pH level of the steam. A caustic condition (>7 pH) can cause stress corrosion on parts harder than 35 RC [2]. Incidentally, this also applies to “sour” (sulfur-containing) natural gas.

There is no hard-and-fast rule for when or under what pressure conditions a trim should be hard-faced. Remember, gaseous fluid undergoes sonic velocity in the orifice (typically 1350 ft/s for steam) regardless of whether the pressure drop is from 3000 psi to 500 psi or only from 15 psi gauge to atmosphere.

Guide material and bearing material Guide material and bearing material

Regarding stem or shaft guide material, does natural gas coming from a well contain sand? These are all questions that should be asked when specifying the valve material. Otherwise, the general-purpose trim material, that is, AISI type 316 stainless steel, will do just fine with clean liquid or gases even if the pressure drop exceeds 1000 psi, even though most handbooks limit the allowable ΔP for stainless steel trim to between 150 and 200 psi [3].

A final word regarding guide material: For globe valves and low-pressure service (<500 psi ΔP), both plug or stem guide bushing can be 316 stainless steel, as long as the 316 stainless steel plug surface or the 316 stainless steel stem surface is somewhat work hardened by suitable machining or roller burnishing techniques.

The reasons for trim wear are more subtle. Is the steam handled by the valve fairly wet because the valve is far removed from the boiler? Does the fluid contain a solid catalyst? Does the fluid tend to crystallize under certain conditions? Can two-phase flow (liquid plus gas) exist? Does natural gas coming from a well contain sand? These questions are addressed in the next chapter.

Heavier valve types use type 440C stainless-steel guide bushings or, for corrosive service, bushings made of or faced with a cobalt-chromium-tungsten alloy such as Stellite. For rotary valves, tetrafluoroethylene (TFE)-dispersed glass fiber bushings work well up to 350°F and TFE-dispersed bronze bushings to 450°F. Other suitable bearing materials are Nitronic 60, a stainless-steel alloy suitable to 800°F or, if properly retained, a solid graphite bushing. For higher temperature (to 1250°F) and/or corrosive service, use cobalt-chromium-tungsten alloy or graphite.

Recommending suitable materials for the infinite variety of corrosive chemicals is not possible. However, when in doubt, a polytetrafluorethylene (PTFE; Teflon)-lined valve is usually the best bet. Your choice is between globe valves with Cv ranges from 0.001 to 100 or rotary valves for larger capacities.
