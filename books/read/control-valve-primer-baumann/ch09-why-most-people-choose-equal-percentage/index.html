Why Most People Choose “Equal Percentage” as a Flow Characteristic

Hans D. Baumann · Control Valve Primer, 5th ed. (ISA / Wiley)

Đặc tính vốn có: Cv theo hành trình The inherent characteristic: Cv versus travel

One of the most vexing questions is, what inherent flow characteristic should my valve have? First, a definition: the inherent flow characteristic is the relationship between the flow coefficient Cv and valve travel. If it is linear, then you get (theoretically) the same amount of Cv increase for an equal increase in travel. Typical values are a 9.8% increase in Cv for every 10% increase in travel. Aha! You might wonder, why not 10% Cv? At, for example, 1% travel, the flow is already 2% of maximum Cv due to clearance flow between the plug and the seat ring. Therefore, the change is only 9.8% per 10% increments (see Figure 9-1).

For the equal percentage characteristic, the typical Cv increase is about 43% above the previous number for each 10% of travel; for example, at 20% travel the Cv number is 143% of that at 10% travel; assuming the Cv at 40% travel is 10, then at 50% travel it is 14.3 and at 60% travel, 20.5, and so on. This means that the flow increases exponentially instead of linearly. This characteristic is typically represented by a straight line on semi-logarithmic paper.

Finally, there is the quick-opening characteristic, which means the valve plug is no longer contoured but is usually a flat disk. Here, the Cv increase is approximately linear, up to a travel equivalent to one-quarter of the orifice diameter. After that, the flow stops increasing for all practical purposes. You may safely ignore this characteristic for throttling control valves.

Cảnh báo: đường cong vốn có chỉ đúng khi ΔP không đổi Caution: inherent curves apply only at constant ΔP

Now a word of caution: after the valve is installed in a piping system, the above-mentioned flow characteristics are only applicable when:

  • the pressure drop remains constant with flow changes;
  • there will be no onset of flashing, cavitation, or sonic flow at certain flow conditions (quite possibly due to changes in the FL factor of the valve, with changes in valve travel);
  • the valve is not installed between reducers.

Ideal conditions that prevent any of these situations from occurring will prevail only in the manufacturer's flow lab. To account for the real world, we have to deal with the installed flow characteristic. This is the relationship of the flowing quantity to valve travel, accounting for the difference in pressure drop between the maximum and minimum flow [1]; the possibility of choked flow along the line; and the distortion that reducer head losses create in the inherent valve flow characteristic. This installed characteristic is what finally affects controller stability.

Điều khiển PID và độ lợi không đổi PID control and constant gain

An ideal controller proportional, integral, and derivative (PID) action setting will demand a constant “gain” of the final control element. This gain is the rate of change in flow (q) for a given change of signal. Usually, the valve travel (h) is proportional to the signal (s). This makes δ(q)/δ(s) = constant, for the ideal valve.

Ideally, we want a “linear” installed flow characteristic, which is about a 10% increase in flow (not Cv) for every 10% increase in travel (this would result in an installed “gain” of the valve of 1). However, nothing in life is ideal, therefore we have to compromise. As a rule of thumb, the valve gain can vary ±50% from the average constant gain value (it does not need to be 1.0) without requiring an adjustment in the controller's PID settings. This means, for example, that one can accept an installed characteristic where the flow can vary 5% per 10% travel at one end and 15% per 10% travel on the other. This certainly makes life easier. Figure 9-2 shows an example of a typical installed valve flow characteristic.

Here, the controller was tuned at 20% flow or 28% of signal. The slope of the curve at that point (15% flow per 20% signal) serves as a reference. In this case, it just so happens that the gain changes in either direction. Therefore, the ±50% (i.e., 1.5 and 0.5) “constant gain” slopes determine the limits' superimposed 50% control valve gain variations from the original controller setting, indicating limits of expected stable controller performance. Retuning the controller may be necessary if the flow range exceeds the shown limits.1 The figure caption describes a valve having a constant gain of 0.75 with good controller stability; the two quoted characteristics are 10% flow (8% signal) and 57% flow (78% signal).

Footnote 1. Change the proportional band of the controller to 50% if the average gain is around 2, for example.

Quy tắc chọn đặc tính Rules for choosing the characteristic

Based on the information in Figure 9-2, we can make the following rules.

  1. First, check your process and identify the ΔP across the valve at the minimum required flow rate and the ΔP at the maximum flow rate. Establish a ratio between the two.
  2. Second, if the ratio is less than 2:1, choose a linear inherent valve characteristic. If the ratio is above 2:1, choose an equal percentage plug.

So much for science. Now let us look at the more mundane aspects of the subject. As I have already stated, most valves are oversized, primarily due to overestimating pipe pressure losses that tend to lower the sizing ΔP and allowing for future increases in throughput. Typically, you wind up using only 40% of the valve's rated Cv at maximum flow. A linear valve will be about 40% open (using only 40% of the controller's signal span). An equal percentage valve, on the other hand, will be at about 75% travel. Which will make the better optical impression on your supervisor? Never mind that the valve's deadband is now spread over 75% of the signal span instead of only over 40%. You guessed it: the answer is the valve with the equal percentage plug!

Ví dụ bộ trao đổi nhiệt Heat-exchanger example

Consider another argument. Assume the liquid flow through a heat exchanger may vary 3:1 and the corresponding ΔP across the valve, typically to the square of the flow rate, that is, nine times the ΔP at one-third of the flow. Our rangeability, or turndown, that is, the ratio of maximum to minimum Cv, is now [1/(1)^1/2]/[0.333/(9)^1/2] = 1/0.111 or 9:1. Dividing the 40% of rated Cv at maximum flow (see above) by 9 now yields 4.4% of rated Cv at minimum flow.

[1/(1)1/2] / [0.333/(9)1/2] = 1 / 0.111 = 9:1

For a valve with a linear characteristic, this occurs at about 3% travel or in the “danger” zone (below 5% travel) where the plug may bump against the seat following a slight controller instability. The equal percentage valve, on the other hand, has about 14% travel at the minimum flow! These are arguments you should keep in mind.

Bộ điều khiển tự chỉnh và đường cong tín hiệu lập trình được Self-tuning controllers and programmable output curves

With the availability of self-tuning controllers and computer chips that can vary the shape of the output signal curve to your valve, the question of what to choose as an inherent valve flow characteristic becomes more irrelevant. Keep in mind that most rotary control valves have “natural” inherent characteristics that cannot be defined in either of the classic definitions, yet they all seem to work well.

This proves how forgiving most control loops are. Remember, any flow characteristic is linear when viewed under a magnifying glass. For continual processes where the flow rate does not vary by more than 20%, the gain change is less than 2:1 regardless of the type of inherent characteristic, so you might as well choose equal percentage because of the advantages outlined earlier.

Cam định tính trong bộ định vị Characterized cams in positioners

Here is a final word regarding the use of characterized cams in positioners attached to rotary control valves. These are of limited use. First, if the valve has a nearly linear inherent characteristic, then 4% Cv is still only 3% travel, regardless of the 14% signal input required by the equal percentage cam. Second, the modified relationship of travel to signal is correct only under static conditions; that is, it only works with large process time constants such as temperature control. Finally, characterized cams are inaccurate and difficult to adjust.

If the process is fast, you have a control valve that has a variable time constant with travel, which can raise cane with the stability of the loop. Why? Because if you have an equal percentage cam to convert (e.g., a linear inherent valve characteristic), it requires the valve actuator to move about 21% of its rated travel for a signal change from 90% to 80% but only 1.6% of travel for a signal change from 20% to 10%. Expressed differently, it takes 13 times longer for the valve to move at the top of the signal change than near the bottom of the signal span! This can drastically interfere with the time constant of the process loop. Therefore, avoid cams for pressure or flow control (fast loops).

Cách tính đặc tính lưu lượng «lắp đặt» How to Calculate the “Installed” Flow Characteristic

Before you sign a purchase requisition for a given control valve, you may want to know if your controller remains stable in a control loop where the flow rate can vary over a wide range, allowing only the maximum control valve gain change of ±50% mentioned earlier as a criterion. However, this cannot be done without calculating or plotting the installed flow characteristic of the valve and piping system combination, which, when completed, should look similar to the curve in Figure 9-2.

Assume we have a liquid flow system that consists of a storage tank, a centrifugal pump, the control valve, more than 20 feet of 6-inch pipe, four elbows, and a process vessel like the example shown in Figure 9-3 [2]; the figure is courtesy of Jon Monsen.

To do the job, we need the following information.

  • The maximum and minimum flow rates, given as 1250 and 250 gpm, respectively.
  • The pump characteristic (converted from feet to psi), given by the manufacturer (see the table below).
  • The friction loss in the pipe and elbows, calculated as 28 psi at a flow rate of 1250 gpm. If there is no problem with viscosity or flashing, we can assume that the friction loss is proportional to the square of the flow rate, that is, ΔP pipe = 28 psi/(gpm/1250 gpm)²; as in the table below.
  • The static head change (elevation difference) from the pump intake to the process vessel (converted from feet to psi), given as 23 psi (remains constant).
  • The back pressure in the process vessel, given as 52 psig (remains constant).

First, we calculate the available pressure drop across the control valve at maximum flow (1250 gpm):

ΔPvalve = pump head − ΔPpipe − static head − backpressure = 110 − 28 − 23 − 52 = 7 psi

From the process data, we know the specific gravity equals 1.1. We now can calculate the maximum required valve Cv using Equation 5-1:

Cv = 1250 / (7 / 1.1)0.5 ≈ 496

Because no tight shutoff is required, and based on rangeability and cost considerations, we might consider a 6-inch, angle-seating butterfly valve with a rated Cv of 825. From the manufacturer's catalog, we get the inherent flow characteristic, which is:

This tells us that the valve will be about 75% open at maximum flow—almost perfect. Now we will calculate the percentage of valve travel for our other flow rates, that is, the percentage of controller signal, which (because we did not specify a positioner cam) is about identical to the valve travel. Going to 1050 gpm, we again calculate the valve pressure drop as above. This is 121 psi (pump head) – 19.8 psi (ΔP pipe) – 23 psi – 52 psi = 26.2 psi, and the Cv = 1050/(26.2/1.1)0.5 = 215. Extrapolating from the Cv table of the valve, we identify the travel at 43.3%.

121 − 19.8 − 23 − 52 = 26.2 psi; Cv = 1050/(26.2/1.1)0.5 = 215

The flow versus % signal is now our installed flow characteristic as shown in Figure 9-4, curve A, which turns out to be highly nonlinear with a gain variation of about 6.7:1 between low flow and high flow. This obviously will result in an unstable controller situation. Unfortunately, we are stuck with the inherent characteristic of the butterfly valve. But even an equal percentage globe valve would not have been much better,

with the pressure drop across the valve varying over a ratio of 74 psi / 7 psi = 10.5:1. Incidentally, if the line size would have been 8 in, the required reducers would have distorted the installed characteristic even more. Another argument for line-size butterfly valves!

So, what can we do? A quick check in a popular control valve manufacturer's catalog shows the maximum Cv of a 6-inch cage valve with equal percentage trim is 357, which is too low. An 8-inch size valve is available with a rated Cv of 570. This would fit the bill, but the cost is high, and it would be awkward to install a valve larger than the pipe size. Besides, the characteristic would not have made that much of an improvement [3].

A cam-modified positioner characteristic is of no use for anything other than a slow temperature control system (printed as “see Chapter 9,” although the cam discussion is in this very chapter), so we have to rely on the modern miracle of electronics. Assume we modify the output signal Si from a computer instead of sending it directly (via a digital-to-analog converter) to the valve in the standard 4 to 20 mA range. If Si is the signal generated by the internal controller logic and So is the final output signal to the valve, we can program the following modification in this case:

So = 0.9 (Si)²
Note. Digital positioners need no conversion to analog.

In this case, I use 0.9 to bring the valve travel closer to the maximum signal and use the square function to linearize the shape of the installed flow characteristic. This yields the following relationship:

We can now replot the resultant characteristic, for example, by taking the flow at 32% travel (So) from curve A (880 gpm) and moving it to the corresponding 60% computer signal (Si) point. After transferring all points, we now have the relationship between flow and controller logic signal (Si) in the form of curve B. While we still have some gradual change in slope, the resultant gain variations from 1.92 to 0.72 (240 gpm/10% to 90 gpm/10%) are all within the stated limitation of ±50%. If the controller is set to a valve gain of 1.35, then the upper limit = 1.35 + 50% = 2.025, and a lower limit = 1.35 − 50% = 0.68, both above and below the actual gain variations.

Footnote 2. The control valve gain is the percentage of flow change divided by a percentage of signal change.

Thus, a gain of 1.35, in this case, will yield 13.5% of flow (125 gpm) increase per each 10% of signal increase.
