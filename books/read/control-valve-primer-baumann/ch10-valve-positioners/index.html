Bộ định vị van — bộ điều khiển vị trí thân van The positioner as a valve stem position controller

Although positioners complicate overall loop stability, most control valves cannot operate without them. Broadly speaking, the use of a valve positioner, which really is a “valve stem position controller,” adds another control loop within a control loop. Here the set point is the controller signal to the positioner; the feedback comes from the lever attached to the actuator stem sensing the latter's position; the controller is the flapper-nozzle, or pilot valve, sensing the difference between the force created by the controller signal and that of a feedback spring attached to the lever; and the final control element is the relay that sends air pressure to the valve actuator. While the latter description refers to pneumatic positioners, the basic functions are identical for digital positioners, except that here the signal-created input force comes from a voice coil, a torque motor, or a solenoid.

Like any other loop, the positioner loop has its own dead time, time constant, and gain, which often interfere with the original process control loop dynamics [1]. This can make the task of the process controller more difficult when fast loops, such as those for pressure or flow control, are encountered.

Những trường hợp không thể thiếu bộ định vị When a positioner is unavoidable

So much for outlining the problem; now to the benefits. First, there are instances in which the use of a valve positioner is unavoidable:

  • When a cylinder actuator that requires air pressure in excess of 15 psi is used. Here, the positioner is used both as a pressure multiplier and a stem position controller.
  • When split-ranging two valves: connect the controller output to the first valve that has a positioner with a signal range of 3–9 psi (4–12 mA). Connect the same signal to a second valve that has a positioner calibration of 9–15 psi (12–20 mA). This way, the first valve will be fully open when the second valve takes over. Good practice is to have the two positioner signals overlap, such as 3–10 psi and 8–15 psi, to get a smooth transfer of fluid. A common practice is to select a small valve size together with a larger one to get a wider flow range. Another case is to use a first valve (flow-to-close) handling heating fluid together with a second valve handling cooling fluid (flow-to-open) for better temperature control.
  • When valves (usually one of two) are operated in a reverse-acting mode. That is, when a valve fails closed (air-to-open actuator) for safety reasons, it shall be operated in the signal-to-close direction.
  • When the expected deadband of the actuator/valve combination exceeds 5% of the signal span (0.6 psi or 0.8 mA, or an equivalent bidirectional electronic signal) because of excessive stem or trim friction.
  • When a booster has to be used for high travel speed and the valve may have excessive deadband.
  • When you want to have diagnostic capabilities online (smart positioners only).

I am not aware of any other reasons a valve positioner should be used, except perhaps to increase the speed of response in case a valve is located too far away from an I/P transducer installed in the control room. But even there, a valve-mounted transducer or booster relay will perform better with an ordinary valve not meeting any of the criteria outlined earlier. Think also of how much money will be saved, not only in hardware but also in piping, air sets, and maintenance.

However, because a positioner is required in most cases, try to specify a modern version that offers the ability to vary its (and the connected valve's) operating characteristic. Formerly, valve positioners were like process controllers without proportional band and reset adjustments. One may get lucky, and everything will be stable; however, the valve (or the complete control loop) might “hunt” in an unstable mode. It used to be educational to walk through a typical refinery or chemical plant and count all the control valves in operation with the valve positioner disconnected or in the bypass mode!

Tốc độ, độ lợi và tỷ lệ hằng số thời gian Speed, gain and the time-constant ratio

To make life easier for today's process operators, many new positioners offer speed and gain adjustments or a combination of the two. This is like adding the adjusting knobs back onto the controller housing. This feature enables you to change the frequency response characteristic (or time constant) of the final control element in optimum relation to that of the process. You probably know that a loop cannot be stable if the process time constant is equal to or close to that of the controller/valve combination. To have a stable loop, the ratio between the valve and the system time constants should be at least 3:1 (or 0.3:1) or, better yet, 5:1 (or 0.2:1). Ideally, the valve/positioner loop should be faster than the process loop, which is sometimes hard to accomplish.

With a conventional (and nondigital) valve/positioner combination, you had no choice but to either reduce the controller settings (high proportional band—long reset action) or crimp the output tubing from the positioner to the actuator to slow the valve down, particularly when the positioner was unstable due to excessive valve stem friction. Now, hopefully avoiding the pitfalls of having somebody ignorantly playing with the adjustments in the field, you can change the speed of the valve travel (typically by 5:1) and/or the open-loop gain1 setting (sensitivity) of the positioner (typically by 10:1) even while the valve is in operation. (A word of caution: You may have to realign the zero adjustment somewhat after changing the gain setting.)

Footnote 1. The definition of the “open-loop gain” of a positioner is the change in the output signal over the change in the input signal (when the feedback is disconnected). Thus, a gain of 200:1 causes a 20 psi output signal change per 0.1 psi input signal change. Gain is the reciprocal of percent band; therefore, a gain of 200:1 is equivalent to 0.5% proportional band. Some positioners have gains only as low as 30:1; however, some exceed 200:1. Some digital positioners that employ push-pull solenoid valves for I/P conversion may need a lower gain setting to avoid “chattering” of these internal devices.

Lời khuyên khi không nắm rõ động học quá trình Advice even without a notion of process dynamics

Even if you do not have the faintest notion about your process dynamics, keep the following advice in mind:

  • If you have a slow process such as temperature control, set the gain at or near maximum. This way, the valve will respond to the smaller incremental signal change (due to the reset action of the controller). However, set the speed (air output) near minimum, thus avoiding an unstable actuator.
  • If you have a fast process such as pressure or flow control, set the gain near minimum and the actuator travel speed at its maximum value, thus creating a highly responsive but stable valve.

Chọn bộ định vị hay bộ chuyển đổi Choosing a positioner or transducer

Most control loops use a 4 to 20 mA analog or digital signal, so you are forced to transduce the electronic signal into a pneumatic one or use a digital positioner. If you have to use an analog signal (4 to 20 mA), then an I/P positioner is your choice; an alternative is to specify an I/P transducer and direct its output to a pneumatic positioner. Note: although pneumatic positioners may be on the way out, they still have cost advantages and are, by design, explosion-proof.

Kiểm tra điều kiện lắp đặt Checking the installation conditions

First, consider whether the valve is installed in an area where flammable liquids are handled and vapors may exist. You may need an explosion-proof (or intrinsically safe) version.

Second, check the vibration level at the valve location. For example, if the valve is close to a centrifugal pump, pipe vibrations can exist in the 60 Hz frequency range or multiple modes thereof. Any good I/P positioner or transducer should maintain steady output under vibration levels of at least 3 G at 30 cps and 1 G at 100 cps (G = acceleration of gravity, 31.1 ft/s²).

Third, consider the quality of the air supply. Do you use dehydrated and oil-free instrument air, or air taken directly off the factory compressor? In the latter case, avoid all positioners that utilize built-in amplifying relays, because the small passages may be subject to plugging.

Fourth, consider the effects of weather on an outdoor installation, or high ambient temperature if the valve is located near a furnace, for example.

Đáp ứng tần số và dung lượng khí Frequency response and air capacity

Finally, where good frequency response is a must (flow control, turbine or compressor bypass, pressure control, etc.), do not use an I/P transducer that is rack mounted on a remote instrument panel. Instead, mount the I/P transducer on the valve actuator to avoid signal delays.2

Footnote 2. This is especially important for “fast loops” such as flow or pressure control applications because time lags due to long transmission lines between transducers and valve actuators may vary from 0.2 to 2 s per 10% signal change [2].

Make sure the air output capacity of the transducer or positioner is sufficient to give adequate travel speed. Note: some rack-mounted I/P transducers have air capacities of 1 to 4 SCFM, which limits their use to temperature applications and the operation of smaller valves only (if no positioner is used).

As a rule of thumb, allow at least 1 SCFH at 20 psi supply per in² of diaphragm area (3 to 15 psi signal). For example, a valve with a 100 in² diaphragm actuator should need a positioner with at least a 100 SCFH air output capacity.

For very large actuators, you may need a booster relay between the positioner and actuator. In this case, a 1:1 pressure-ratio volume booster with a 1 psi differential and an adjustable bypass needle valve is recommended. The 1 psi differential ensures that the booster is activated only when the signal output from, for example, the positioner jumps by more than 1 psi. This is usually the case if the valve should open fast in the event of an emergency (a typical compressor surge control application). The adjustable needle valve can be tuned to avoid the instability of the booster relay.

Các tính năng nên có Desirable features

Other desirable features are: all exterior linkages to be type 18-8 stainless steel; all aluminum parts to be anodized (interior and exterior) and epoxy coated; positioners to have at least a built-in gain adjustment; I/P transducers to have the capability for a 1 to 17 psi air output range (see Chapter 11 for information on off-balance plug forces); and, if of “closed-loop” design (with built-in pressure transducer feedback), a gain or deadband adjustment to avoid instability when mounted close to a small diaphragm actuator.

Bộ định vị thông minh là gì? What is a smart positioner?

As is the case with many marketing catchwords, the term “smart positioner” is not yet well defined. It generally is the equivalent of a remote programmable transmitter using the equivalent HART 3 protocol or a bidirectional digital signal from a fieldbus device.3 Nevertheless, most still work internally with a 4 to 20 mA analog signal.

Footnote 3. HART stands for Highway Addressable Remote Transducer Protocol.

On the input side, there are digital electronics that allow for programmable input, such as the inherent characteristic between the signal and valve travel (note the resultant nonuniform valve positioner time constant — refer to the previous section, “What to Look for When Specifying Positioners or Transducers”), the adjustment and calibration of valve travel, split-ranging, and deadband. Nevertheless, the final output signals to the air conversion mechanisms are usually in analog form.

Most of these devices do not need external power and are, therefore, intrinsically safe (explosion-proof) when the proper barriers are installed. Fieldbus positioners can perform the functions of a proportional, integral, and derivative (PID) controller, acting as a decentralized controller with inputs from digital transmitters or computers. Failure of the electronic positioner circuit affects only the local loop.

While these positioners cost about one-third more than their analog counterparts, they can monitor the control valve. For example, they can tell the computer the current travel position, and by measuring the relationship between the positioner signal and the air output pressure to the actuator, they can identify the onset of too much deadband (e.g., a maintenance person tightens the stem packing too much). On the other hand, remote calibration of the positioner's zero and span setting after installation, while touted as an important feature, will rarely happen.

Phần cứng và điều khiển tại chỗ Hardware and local control

Perhaps of more interest is the hardware in these positioners. In one model, a microcontroller commands one piezoelectric valve to open to admit air to the actuator on an increase in signal and can command a second piezoelectric valve to open to exhaust the air from the actuator on a decrease in signal. If there is no signal change, then both piezoelectric valves stay closed; hence the claim of low air consumption. These devices require a minimum deadband in order to prevent “hunting” of the opposing valves. However, the majority of smart or digital positioners employ built-in I/P transducers to generate the pneumatic output signal. All these positioners can operate from the current 4 to 20 mA signal. No auxiliary power is required.

Digital positioner electronics can include PID control circuitry to perform the primary control functions (the computer will do the supervisory override and the changing of set points). Future versions may offer more advanced control functions such as “fuzzy logic.” Be aware of the local environmental situation at the valve's location before using the positioner as a local controller. For example, the valve may be situated in a boiler room with a hot and humid atmosphere, or it may handle a high-pressure drop with the associated high vibration level, all of which potentially affect the reliability of the control loop. Some vendors offer remotely mounted positioners using electronic stem position measuring devices that tell the positioner the valve's travel position.

Van thông minh với lưu lượng kế tích hợp Smart valve with a built-in flow meter

Using the valve as a decentralized control system is already done in some smart valve designs (shown schematically in Figure 10-2) that include a built-in flow meter consisting of piezoelectric pressure-measuring elements upstream and downstream of the valve orifice plus a temperature sensor. Knowing the orifice area (Cv) at a given travel position, the differential pressure across it, and the density (a function of pressure and temperature), the flow rate across the valve can be calculated and controlled (for a similar schematic, see Figure 10-3).

Smart valves have many advantages. First, they offer a higher range of flow rates (up to 100:1) than flowmeters (about 25:1) because the movable valve plug acts as a variable orifice. Second, the use of smart valves will eliminate the errors produced by converting from analog to digital signals. Finally, they offer a faster response to loop upsets.

While they are much more expensive than their conventional counterparts, such valves can be an important element in more complex control schemes; for example, when you want to control the temperature in a fractionator (a temperature control system with long time constants and time lags) in which the heating medium is steam. This needs a pressure or flow control system that interacts with the temperature control in a typical cascade system. Here the smart valve is the ideal choice for controlling the supply of steam to the heat exchanger. Although the temperature controller experiences time lags (typical for temperature changes), the smart valve controlling the steam can react nearly instantly to any upsets in, for example, the local steam pressure.

Remember, even the smartest valve is no substitute for a flow meter. The variable geometry of the trim, wall attachment phenomenon (Coandă effect), incipient cavitation, choked flow, and, last but not least, the noise picked up by the pressure transducer due to valve turbulence make it much too inaccurate. Nevertheless, this does not matter for control. Here it is not the absolute value of the flow rate that counts, but the deviation thereof that must be controlled.

Bộ định vị số nâng cấp van hiện có Digital positioners enhance existing valves

As a result of progress in the automated control field, smart or digital positioners enhance the function of all currently available control valves and, therefore, offer a higher degree of flexibility. Figure 10-3 shows a schematic view of such a positioner. Communication from a supervisory computer can travel over the present 4 to 20 mA signal wires or through fieldbus wiring. These same wires give feedback information regarding the valve's status to a computer or a display panel. This feedback information can be transmitted in digital form via HART protocol over the analog 4 to 20 mA signal. This can be done even while the valve is operating, although special software is required on the receiving end to decode the messages. In fully digital communication protocols such as Foundation Fieldbus, both signal and return information will be transmitted in digital form.

The printed circuit board in Figure 10-3 acts as the onboard controller and signal processor. This is typically an encapsulated and field-replaceable element. The circuitry compares this signal level to the signal coming from the stem position sensor and, if not identical, puts out a corrective signal to the I/P transducer element whose output is amplified in a separate relay before being fed to the pneumatic valve actuator as “output pressure” to correct the stem position error.

A separate pressure sensor is a key element for the integral diagnostic circuit. For example, by measuring the required output pressure to move the valve stem to 50% travel, and then comparing it to the same pressure (at 50% travel) measured when the valve was brand-new, one can detect wear or blockage, or perhaps a too-tight packing box. Such data is fed back, as previously explained, to alert operating personnel and perhaps initiate maintenance work on the valve. Such preventive maintenance testing can be done either after the process has been shut down or in the field under actual operating conditions (in situ). The latter has the advantage of experiencing actual working conditions such as the local temperature and pressure environment. However, such testing can only be done by moving the valve stem in small increments to avoid large upsets in the controlled variable. Understandably, this is not widely practiced.

Tóm tắt: thiết bị số so với thiết bị tương tự Summary: digital versus analog field devices

Here is a summary of digital devices compared to analog field devices, taken from Reference 3.

  1. Increased accuracy, 0.1% to 1%, compared to analog 0.3% to 2%.
  2. Improved stability, about 0.1%, compared to 0.1% to 0.75% analog.
  3. Wider rangeability (without sacrificing accuracy), up to 50:1 compared to 10:1.
  4. Capable of multifunctioning, such as adapting to process dynamics.
  5. Diagnostics and self-testing.
  6. Ability for miniaturization.
  7. Ability to calibrate and adjust without physical access to the instrument.
  8. Ability to utilize physical effects that cannot be measured or assessed by analog means.
  9. Digital processing can be done with little energy consumption, enabling intrinsic safety under the “triad” policy encompassing the CSA (United States) · CENELEC (European) · CESI (Japanese) codes.

Phụ kiện và phần mềm Accessories and Software

Common accessories for digital positioners include built-in microswitches. These typically operate at low voltage (24 V). Such switches can provide a safety shutdown of the valve, initiated independently of the controller. Digital or analog travel-position indicating devices are also available. These positioners require software to perform the required diagnostic or remote calibration functions. Here are some capabilities that such a software package should support [3]:

  • Reading the valve's diagnostic data
  • Supporting a graphical diagnostic trend window display
  • Valve configuration and a valve configuration database
  • Monitoring device capabilities
  • Configuration of valve characteristics
  • General processes and a valve database
  • Security levels for users
  • A diagnostic database

Kết nối mọi thứ vào hệ điều khiển Connecting It All to the Control System

To interact properly, the plant's control system needs an architecture in which transmitters, positioners, controllers, annunciators, computers, and so on, together with their associated software, are integrated into a structure that lets these devices communicate effectively with one another and perform in an efficient manner.

An efficient, field-based architecture can build process management solutions by networking intelligent field devices, scalable control system platforms, and software. Besides intelligent field devices (for example, smart positioners), this requires modular performance software. Done properly, such software can redefine management, expand process control, and provide solutions to asset-management problems. The software should:

  • reduce installed cost;
  • improve performance;
  • be compatible with existing devices;
  • reduce process offsets;
  • be scalable;
  • reduce maintenance cost;
  • improve plant reliability;
  • reduce training time; and
  • be adaptable to changing processes.

As shown in Figure 10-4, such an architecture will effectively change the previous typical 4 to 20 mA analog-signal-based “distributed control system (DCS)-centric” system into a new field-based architecture. This will effectively send the operator from the control room to the plant level.

Smart field devices such as smart valve positioners with control functions (PID) can do all of the following on a local level: loop control, signal processing, data acquisition, analyses, alarm detection, trend collection, and maintenance recording.

Phụ kiện Accessories

According to Bud Keyes [4], the process of transferring functionality out of the central control room and into the field devices is analogous to the computer industry's move from mainframe computing to PC-based networks.

The overall aim is to create a real-time management control system integrating all aspects of plant management, production control, process control, plant efficiency, and maintenance.

The maintenance program for control valves could include, for example, online diagnosis, remote calibration, preventive maintenance plans, and control of spare-parts inventory.
