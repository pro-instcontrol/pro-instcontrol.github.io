The Bus System to the Rescue, or What the Future May Bring The Bus System to the Rescue, or What the Future May Bring

Will the conventional (throttling-type) control valve cease to exist? Not likely. Remember, the automobile did not completely replace the bicycle. It goes against our grain to “waste” energy in a throttling valve. However, despite attempts over the centuries to invent “perpetual motion,” that is, a device that works without “using” energy (converting energy to a higher entropy level), we have found none. The same is true for the control of fluids in a pipe. The basic laws of physics still apply.

Attempts have been made to obtain control more elegantly. For example, by applying electric current to a fluid [1], you can reduce the flowing quantity by changing the turbulent or laminar flow pattern (see Figure 30-1). This works like a magnetic flow meter in reverse; however, it is not a practical approach. Microelectronic valves are available to control small flows. One such design1 relies on the interaction of a suspended mass with a valve body to open and close. In the closed position, the suspended mass is seated directly over an outlet port in the valve body. Displacement of this mass opens the valve.

1 This design is manufactured by IC Sensors.

The displacement is achieved by changing the electric voltage supplied to a diaphragm made of a bimetallic-silicon composite. The diaphragm surrounds the suspended mass to provide suspension. It is made up of resistors diffused into a thin layer of silicon and covered by a layer of aluminum. When an electric voltage is supplied to the metallic materials, it affects a change in the temperature of the diaphragm and a corresponding displacement of the suspended mass.

The gap between the valve seat and its mating surface can be adjusted in proportion to the electrical input, which enables proportional control of flow and the usual fully open or fully closed functions of a valve. The valve can control flow rates in the range of 0 to 150 cc/min at input pressures between 1 and 50 psig.

Speed-controlled pumps have been tried with limited success. So, what is the outlook for the future?

Electronics will still bring us important changes as far as actuating software and hardware are concerned. The biggest impact has been the use of fieldbus standards that now tend to replace the current analog 4 to 20 mA signal with a digital communication signal between the controller or computer and the valve positioner. The benefit is increased functionality (see Chapter 9).

Some positioners already have a proportional, integral, and derivative (PID) function chip and do all the control based on the sensor input via the fieldbus. This reduces hardwiring costs (a single pair of twisted wire connects everything; see Figure 30-2) and offers simplified data transfer with less noise and increased accuracy. At the same time, the fieldbus can transmit important data back to the computer regarding the valve position, for example. Other sensors could be embedded in a positioner to inform about seat leakage, stem packing friction, actuator friction, air leakage in the actuator, and so on. However, I pity the poor operator who must be absolutely overwhelmed by monitoring perhaps 10 continuous data input channels per valve in a typical process plant having 500 individual loops! So, selective use of this option is certainly in order. Being informed of a higher-than-usual valve deadband could explain sudden loop instability and may trigger preventive maintenance, for example. Being aware of the stem package leakage could avoid dangerous spills.

More serious problems exist with the amount of electric current a fieldbus can handle without losing its “intrinsically safe” label. Of course, a low current means fewer valve positioners on the line. The solution is to multiplex (time-share) so that only one positioner in the loop receives a signal change at any given time. This will require a memory device or pneumatic lockup device to freeze the valve positioner until the next “switch on,” which in turn might slow down the valve travel speed to a level that may be unsuitable for flow or pressure control. This is one example of several problems that must be addressed prior to the wide acceptance of the fieldbus system.

The likely solution would be to have separate cables to supply electric power to local actuating devices. This, of course, negates some of the advantages of having a fieldbus system, namely, savings in cable lines to individual components. Nevertheless, there is a basic goal that can be reached, that is, tie in all local loop components such as sensors, controllers, and valves into an independent local working system (distributed control system) to be connected only for purposes of supervisory control and data logging to a central computer network. This will result in major cost savings in cabling as well as in the amount of data being transmitted.

Smart valves Smart valves

Smart valves, on the other hand, will have a future. The trouble is, what is a smart valve? My preferred definition is the “ultimate in distributed control.” First, you have to realize that every control valve controls the rate of flow passing through it. Nothing else. The difference between a level control valve and a pressure control valve is determined by the fact that the first one employs a level transmitter instead of a pressure transmitter. If the identical valve handling the same flow is in a loop where the feedback comes from a temperature transmitter, then we have a temperature control valve.

Once we realize that each valve controls only the rate of flow, we can go a step further and create a closed-loop flow control system with the valve as its centerpiece. Referring to Figure 30-3, a flow meter, preferably a vortex meter (for rangeability and a wide range of applications, including steam), is attached to the valve inlet (point of lowest turbulence). A flow controller is either attached to the flow meter or is made part of the I/P positioner, while the set point for the flow controller is given by the main process controller or computer (shown as part of a temperature control system). The computer sets the flow rate to achieve the ultimate controlled temperature. As long as there is no disturbance in the system, everything will be fine. However, assume the heating medium passing the valve is steam, and, because of demand elsewhere, the inlet pressure drops by 10%. This can cause a sudden reduction in the steam flow rate by 10%, assuming choked flow. In a conventional temperature system, it may take several minutes2 for the controlled temperature to begin to drop and for the computer to react and open the valve farther. This leads to an upset in the controlled temperature.

2 For example, the temperature measurement lag of a 1/2-inch bulb thermal well sensing a heavy liquid at low velocities can vary between 60 and 180 s.

For more advanced types of flow control, see Chapter 6, “High-Recovery Valves.”

With the smart valve, on the other hand, the flow meter will sense the change in flow immediately, will make the valve increase the travel, and will restore the flow before the usually slow thermodynamic effects make themselves felt downstream. Thus, the temperature stays at the set point, and the computer probably never even detects the upset in steam pressure. This is, of course, a form of cascade control, except good portions of the loop are now in a tidy, compact package. Despite the added cost (and perhaps added wiring), a case can be made to use such a smart valve in critical loops where the valve can absorb process disturbances before they can affect the ultimate controlled variable.

Another favorite of mine, and a few others, is to resurrect the good old pressure regulator with its excellent frequency response and high flow range. Using a temperature control system similar to that described above, the regulator shown in Figure 30-4 can have a set pressure determined by the air signal from an I/P transducer signal coming from a temperature controller (see loop diagram). This regulator controls the pressure of a heat exchange fluid, such as oil or steam, ahead of an orifice (FO) or an adjustable restrictor. Sized correctly, the combination orifice area and pressure ahead of the orifice will effectively control the rate of flow (and, therefore, Btu) to the heat exchanger. Any demand for an increase in temperature is met by the controller increasing the signal to the I/P transducer, which, in turn, increases the air load below the actuator diaphragm and causes the valve to open. This increases the pressure drop across the orifice and the flow of heating fluid. Any local disturbance, such as a sudden decrease in supply pressure, is now sensed by the valve diaphragm, which leads to a nearly instantaneous correction without the disturbance being noticed by the temperature transmitter (TE) at the heat exchanger. We have a smart valve with nearly the same performance characteristics as those in Figure 30-3, but at a fraction of the cost.

Wireless Ethernet Wireless Ethernet

The ISA-100 wireless standard currently being developed and the already published wireless HART standard will make it easier for the user to convert serial networks to Ethernet communication. This will offer faster polling cycles and, of course, eliminate a lot of wiring.

Problems to be solved are security, possible interference, and noise. Wireless communication devices also need power—batteries, for example. Distance is also a problem (20 miles maximum between sender and receiver). It is for these and other reasons that the initial use of wireless systems will be restricted to data transmission and open-loop or “soft” closed-loop control systems.
