The Mystery of Line Pressure-Produced Valve Stem Forces, or Selecting the Correct Actuator Size

Hans D. Baumann · Control Valve Primer, 5th ed. (ISA / Wiley)

Bí ẩn lực thân van do áp suất đường ống, hay Chọn đúng kích cỡ bộ truyền động The mystery of line pressure-produced valve stem forces, or selecting the correct actuator size

The selection of an adequate valve actuator is usually left up to the vendor. Most of the time this works well, especially if the pressure conditions the valve has to handle are low or the valve size is small. However, it will be of value to know some of the consequences of selecting the wrong (usually too small) actuator size. Remember, the valve vendor who got the job was probably the lowest bidder and, often, the bidder who cut the size of actuators in order to reduce costs. Following is a description of what can happen after installation.

Van không đóng kín đúng cách The valve does not close properly

The economically dictated demise of bypass installations around a control valve has led to the requirement that a control valve should have ANSI/FCI Class IV seat tightness (to double as a shutoff valve when the system is down). This works well as long as the initial spring setting, the air pressure, and the size of the actuator are adequate to overcome the fluid pressure acting on the valve plug (assuming a single-seated globe valve).

To start with, it is important to tell the vendor what the maximum inlet pressure is! This is usually the pressure differential across the plug when the valve is shut off and not the lower inlet pressure and substantially higher outlet pressure that the valve sees during normal throttling or that is used for calculating the valve Cv. A responsible valve vendor will always assume the valve has to shut off against the maximum inlet pressure and with a zero-gauge outlet pressure. Therefore, P1 must be stated on the specification form (see Figure 11-1). There are two types of closure: one to stop flow and the other to stop leakage [1].

There are also two types of valve closure mechanisms. A push type, such as a valve plug, closes against an orifice. To stop leakage, additional force above the one needed to overcome the off-balance is required. This additional force for Class IV tightness should be at least 2.5 times the contact seating area times P1. For example, a 2-inch orifice has a beveled contact area of 2 in × 3.14 × 0.063 in = 0.4 in². Thus, when P1 is 100 psi, one would need 100 psi × 0.4 in² × 2.5 = 100 lb extra seating force.

2 in × 3.14 × 0.063 in = 0.4 in²
100 psi × 0.4 in² × 2.5 = 100 lb

For Class V, one might need a compression force equal to the yield point of the contact area.

The second form of closure is sliding contact. This is typical for lined butterfly valves and segmented ball valves, where the closure member has to slide in and partially deform the seal. While the initial contact with a seal ring might stop flow, it is insufficient to stop leakage. An extra 5° of actuator travel is required to move the closure member from stopping flow to stopping leakage. This must be considered during the initial positioner and actuator calibration.

For example, a heating valve with a 2-inch orifice handling hot oil will see, at the normal flow rate and 75% valve travel, an inlet pressure of 125 psig and a downstream pressure of 110 psig. The stem forces sensed by the actuator under these conditions are (2²π/4) × (125 − 110) = 47 lb.1

(2²π/4) × (125 − 110) = 47 lb
Footnote 1. Neglecting the area of the valve stem for simplicity.

However, if called to close, the inlet pressure will be 140 psig due to the increase in pressure following the pump's characteristic, while the downstream pressure may be zero. In this case, the minimum stem force will be 140 psi times the seating area of the plug (use for seating diameter at least 1/16 in plus the orifice diameter) plus the packing friction.

(2.063²π/4) × 140 = 468 lb; stem force (closed) = 468 lb + packing friction

Packing friction can vary considerably; for example, tests with tetrafluoroethylene (TFE)-containing packing material showed that starting friction (or breakaway friction) was 1.5 times that of dynamic (constant movement) friction [2]. However, as a rule of thumb, use 1.5 × stem area × maximum P1. In this case, assume a 3/8-inch stem, packing friction = 1.5(0.375²π/4) × 140 psig = 23 lb. Total stem force (closed) is, therefore, 468 + 23 = 491 lb.

packing friction = 1.5(0.375²π/4) × 140 psig = 23 lb
Total stem force (closed) = 468 + 23 = 491 lb

As you can see, there is more than a tenfold increase in actuator force requirement. In the case of a piston actuator, and assuming the availability of at least 80 psi air, the minimum cylinder area should be 491 lb / 80 lb/in² = 6.2 in², or a 3-inch piston diameter for air-to-close action. You need a minimum spring force of 491 lb if the valve should fail close on air failure.

491 lb / 80 lb/in² = 6.2 in²

If you want a spring-diaphragm actuator to fail close and you specify a valve positioner, a 6 to 15 psi span spring inside a 100 in² actuator would be adequate. The output signal from the positioner would vary as follows:

  1. At zero travel (valve shut), the force created by the spring is 6 psi × 100 in² = 600 lb. However, because the seating stem force here is 491 lb (trying to push up), it leaves only 600 − 491 lb = 109 lb for the actuator to open the valve. Therefore, the air signal is 109 lb/100 in² = 1.1 psi.
    6 psi × 100 in² = 600 lb; 600 − 491 = 109 lb; 109 lb / 100 in² = 1.1 psi
  2. Remember, at 75% travel (normal operating conditions) our stem forces are reduced to 47 lb. At this point, our spring exerts a force of [(15 psi − 6 psi) × 0.75 + 6 psi] × 100 in² = 12.75 psi × 100 in² = 1275 lb. Subtracting from this value the 47 lb acting up (i.e., against the spring) = 1275 − 47 = 1228 lb net spring force. This translates into a positioner output signal of 1228 lb/100 in² = 12.28 psi.
    [(15 psi − 6 psi) × 0.75 + 6 psi] × 100 in² = 12.75 psi × 100 in² = 1275 lb; 1275 − 47 = 1228 lb; 1228 / 100 = 12.28 psi
  3. We thus have an installed positioner output signal span of 1.1 to 12.28 psi between zero and maximum flow at 75% travel. This is in contrast to a controller signal to the positioner of 3 to 12 psi. This contradicts a common fallacy that the output signal of a positioner has to match the input signal.

As you can see from this example, if you were to disconnect the positioner, the valve would never close with a constant 3 psi input signal from an I/P transducer (electro-pneumatic converting instrument). This is exactly what has happened in many plants that utilize computers or single-loop digital controllers for process control [3]. These devices lock in the 4 mA signal at zero process conditions.2

Footnote 2. It is possible to adjust your computer to underrange or overrange the signal by 5% or 0.8 mA. However, allowing the signal to decrease below 2 mA can cause the failure of certain I/P transducers with dire consequences for your process loop!

If you have an I/P transducer that is calibrated to have a 3 to 15 psi output range for a 4 to 20 mA input signal (and most are), you will continuously feed 3 psi to the valve and, as a result, could cause valve leakage. Unfortunately, this situation most often leads to a call to the vendor informing it that its valve is bad (poor quality, etc.).

What can be done about it? In order of cost-effectiveness and engineering preference:

  1. If you can, reduce the zero setting on your I/P transducer to 1 psi at 4 mA, or specify an I/P transducer with a 1 to 17 psi output per ANSI/FCI standard 87-2.
  2. Ask your valve vendor to select an actuator spring with an 8 to 15 psi “bench range.” (This, in the earlier example, will give you an installed range of between 8 minus 4.93 when seated and 15 minus 0.5 psi3 at 100% travel, which is 3.1 to 14.5 psi, close enough to be called a 3 to 15 psi range!)
  3. Specify a valve positioner.
Footnote 3. The 4.93 psi seated correction corresponds to 492 lb/100 in² (the closed stem force divided by the 100 in² actuator area), and the 0.5 psi correction at 100% travel corresponds to 47 lb/100 in² (the normal-flow stem force divided by 100 in²).

Note that simply cranking up the initial spring setting from, say, 3 psi to 6 psi will not do. You will end up with a bench range of 6 to 18 psi, and your valve will not open enough to handle flow upsets. (Remember, a standard I/P signal cannot exceed 15 psi at 20 mA input!)

Van không ổn định The Valve Is Unstable

The instability caused by dynamic fluid forces on a plug, disk, or ball should not be confused with positioner or control loop instability (sometimes hard to distinguish). As shown in the prior example, forces acting on a valve plug or across a butterfly valve (or rotating plug, for that matter) can vary drastically with flow conditions. Many papers have been written on the subject [4], most requiring you to become a math major! Therefore, I will try to keep the matter simple.

For a valve trim to be stable, the change in stem force per inch of stem motion has to be less than the change in stem force per inch of travel exerted by the actuator. The latter is called the actuator stiffness factor, Kn, and is expressed in-lb/in. For a spring-diaphragm actuator, this is simply the spring rate of the actuator spring (or all the springs combined if multispring), ignoring the pneumatic damping.

With piston actuators, things get a bit more complex. Here, the force generated by compressing the internal actuator air pressure when the piston is moved over a given distance determines the stiffness. Obviously, this force varies with stem position, initial cylinder volume (L), and absolute air pressure. As a rule, piston actuators are much stiffer [5] than spring-diaphragm actuators and, therefore, can operate single-seated globe valves in the flow-to-close direction, a feat normal spring-diaphragm actuators cannot handle.

The general practice is to install single-seated globe valves in the flow-to-open direction, where the problem of dynamic instability seldom arises. However, it can be a problem in force-balanced cage valves and angle valves handling cavitating or flashing fluids where discharging occurs downstream of the valve plug (flow tending-to-close). What happens is that not only does the effective area of the plug increase as you approach the seating point but, in addition, the downstream pressure decreases rapidly, roughly to the square of the flow rate decrease. We all have experienced this phenomenon in the bathtub: with water in the tub, as we slowly let go of the chain, the rubber plug approaches the drain, but, at about 3/16 in from the seat, the plug suddenly surges down and slams onto the seat. The same thing happens in a valve. The trick is to have the actuator stiff enough to hold the valve plug in place.

As a rule of thumb, consider that the off-balance plug area goes from zero to maximum within a distance that is the lesser of either 1/3 of valve travel or 1/4 of the orifice diameter. Further, assume that the differential pressure will increase from P1 − P2 at normal flow to P1 maximum within this same distance.

Here is an example: A 3-inch angle valve with a 2.5-inch orifice diameter has a 1/2-inch valve stem diameter. Normal P1 = 165 psig; normal P2 = 125 psig; maximum P1 = 175 psig; flow-to-close actuator travel = 1.5 in. Because the area of the stem is relatively small, we can ignore this and assume the off-balance plug area of the 2.5-inch orifice, which is 4.9 in².

First, consider the distance of area change: 1/3 of 1.5 in travel = 0.5 in; 1/4 of 2.5 in orifice diameter = 0.625 in; therefore, we chose 0.50 in as it is smaller. The rate of change in stem forces is now

1/3 × 1.5 in = 0.5 in; 1/4 × 2.5 in = 0.625 in; choose 0.50 in
(11-1)Off-Balance Area × ΔP change / Distance

where

ΔP change = maximum P1 − ΔP = 175 − (165 − 125) = 135 psi

Therefore, from Equation 11-1, 4.9 in² × 135 psi / 0.50 in = 1323 lb/in.

4.9 in² × 135 psi / 0.50 in = 1323 lb/in

Assuming the actuator is a spring-diaphragm with an area of 100 in² and a 3 to 15 psi bench range, this makes the spring rate (15 − 3 psi) × 100 in² / 1.5 in travel = 800 lb/in.

(15 − 3 psi) × 100 in² / 1.5 in = 800 lb/in

This is quite insufficient, and we either have to recommend a 175 in² actuator or change the bench range of the spring (or springs) to 6 to 30 psi.

(30 − 6 psi) × 100 in² / 1.5 in = 1600 lb/in

This would increase the actuator stiffness to 1600 lb/in, which will provide a sufficient safety margin. Air compression within the actuator will increase the stiffness and, therefore, provide a safety factor, although such stiffness is much less than in cylinder actuators due to larger air volume.

Rotary control valves have similar stiffness problems. They are more complex because we have to deal with torque instead of force. Consider the combined torque characteristic of a butterfly valve (shown in Figure 11-2, taken from Figure 4 in Reference 6). Here, the greatest change in torque per degree occurs at 60°, where the combined torque changes at a rate of (0.011 in-lb / d³ psi) × P1 for ±5° travel, that is, from 0.064 at 55° to 0.075 at 65°. Assume we have a 10-inch valve with a maximum differential pressure (when the valve is closed) of 87 psig. We have a torque change at 60° opening (and assuming the valve has to open above 60° to pass the maximum flow) of 0.011 in-lb × 10 in³ × 87 psi = 960 in-lb per 10°.

(0.011 in-lb/d³ psi) × 10 in³ × 87 psi = 960 in-lb per 10°

Assume we have a cylinder actuator with a 75 in² area that travels 4 in to move the vane from a 0° to 90° opening. Neglecting angular cosine effects, we can assume that at a 65° opening, the piston travel will be at (4 in / 90°) × 65° = 2.88 in, and at 55° vane, travel = (4 in / 90°) × 55° = 2.44 in.

(4 in / 90°) × 65° = 2.88 in; (4 in / 90°) × 55° = 2.44 in

What we need to know is the volume change if the actuator is going from 2.44 to 2.88 in piston travel and assuming 0.3 in of overtravel in the cylinder. Let us calculate the ratio of air volume between both travel positions:

Volume Ratio = A(hi + ho + Δh)/A(hi + ho)

Therefore,

75 in²(2.88 in + 0.3 in) / 75 in²(2.44 in + 0.3 in) = 239 in³ / 206 in³ = 1.16

Assuming further that the positioner output pressure in the cylinder at 65° travel is 65 psia, we can now calculate the force change due to air compression caused by the actuator suddenly moving from 65° to 55° because of dynamic torque effects (remember that in most rotary valves, torque tends cause the valve to close).

Actuator force change = (Volume ratio − 1) × Area × Air pressure (10 − 2) = (1.16 − 1) × 75 in² × 65 psia = 780 lb

All we have to do now is to convert this force change to torque. Because we know the travel is 4 in at 90° rotation, we can assume that the effective length of the lever turning the shaft is equal to actuator travel/2 × tan 45° = 4/(2 × 1) = 2.00 in.

actuator travel/2 × tan 45° = 4/(2 × 1) = 2.00 in

Thus, the force change of 780 lb translates into a torque change of 780 lb × 2 in = 1560 in-lb per 10° travel. This is above the dynamic vane torque change of 960 in-lb / 10°, and we could expect stability of operation because the friction of the piston seal will provide extra resistance to sudden change.

780 lb × 2 in = 1560 in-lb per 10° travel

If this had been a spring-diaphragm actuator with the same 4-inch travel, a torque requirement of 960 in-lb per 10° vane travel would demand a 960 in-lb / 2 in effective lever = 480 lb force change per 0.44 in actuator travel and, therefore, would require a 480 lb / 0.44 in = 1091 lb/in spring rate.

960 in-lb / 2 in = 480 lb per 0.44 in; 480 lb / 0.44 in = 1091 lb/in

Neglecting added stiffness due to air compression, this is equivalent to a 180 in² actuator with a 6 to 30 psi bench range—a drastic increase in actuator size.

While the above example assumed a conventional vane, so-called low-torque vanes would reduce the stiffness requirement and would, therefore, be effective when allowing for the use of spring-diaphragm actuators despite their lower stiffness factor. Note: A 60° opening with the 15° angle-seating, low-torque vane occurs at 45° vane travel (see Figure 11-2).

Note. The source prints «15-inch angle-seating»; it is presented here as 15°, as intended.

The following is a summary of stiffness equations [8].

  • Spring-diaphragm actuator (ignoring lesser stiffness due to air compression5):
    Kn = (Diaphragm area × Signal span)/Travel lb/in
  • Cylinder actuator (single-acting):
    Kn = {[AC(hi + ho + Δh)/AC(hi + ho)] − 1} × S × AC/Δh lb/in

    where AC = effective area of the cylinder, in²; hi = initial travel position, in; ho = overtravel of the piston (travel not used), in; S = signal pressure at given travel position, psia; Δh = change in travel due to disturbance, in.

  • Cylinder actuator (double-acting). Results from single-acting cylinder actuator times two.
Footnote 5. Assumed to be isothermal.
