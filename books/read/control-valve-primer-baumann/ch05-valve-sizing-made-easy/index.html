Định cỡ van Valve Sizing

This activity can be defined as picking the right size valve for the job or, in instances where a valve size is predestined, the correct trim size.

Do not read on if you have a computer fully programmed with ANSI/ISA-75.01.01 equations [1]. But then again, you may want to know what you are doing, or if nothing else, you may want to double-check the computer results.

To help matters, about 60 years ago some clever soul invented a valve sizing coefficient (Cv), which combined the flow area of the valve orifice, the contraction coefficient, and the head loss coefficient in one; thus,

(5-1)Cv = q / √(ΔP/Gf) = q √(Gf/ΔP)
(5-2)q = Cv √(ΔP/Gf) = Cv (ΔP/Gf)0.5

where

  • q = the flow rate in US gallons per minute
  • Gf = the specific gravity of a liquid at a given temperature (water at 60°F has a Gf of 1)
  • ΔP = inlet minus outlet pressure in psi

Therefore, one Cv equals the flow of one US gallon per minute of water at 60°F and under a pressure drop across the valve of 1 psi. Now the same valve will flow 10 gpm at a pressure drop of 100 psi because the flow is proportional to the square root of ΔP, thus following the laws of fluid mechanics. It is worthwhile to memorize this equation because you will use it on at least 80% of all sizing problems for liquids. Unfortunately, not all in life is so simple; therefore, we must recognize two limitations in the use of the abovementioned equations.

First, they only apply to turbulent flow, that is, Reynolds numbers above about 5000. However, you can ignore this limit on required Cv numbers above 0.1 and when the kinematic viscosity is below 40 centistokes (90% of all liquids are). There will be more on this subject later in this chapter—required reading only if you control tar or equally sticky substances and polymers.

Second, in some conditions funny things occur inside the valve that seem to block the flow passages. What really happens is that at a certain ratio of pressure drop to inlet pressure defined by a pressure recovery factor (FL2), the flow becomes choked at the orifice. (FL originally was called the critical flow factor [2], Cf, and was used as such.) In the case of liquids, this is caused by the evaporation of some of the liquid (also see Chapter 15, “Cavitation”), and in the case of gases, the onset of sonic velocity. This would be inconsequential was it not for the fact that now the flow is independent of the downstream pressure (P2). Now the flowing quantity can only increase if you use a higher inlet pressure. See Chapter 6.

To avoid embarrassing errors, the first step is to determine whether you have critical (choked) flow or not using the following equations (this also helps to discover any cavitation or noise problems you may have to worry about).

Note, use (FLp/Fp)2 instead of FL2 in the following equations whenever you have a valve between pipe reducers; FLp is the pressure recovery factor that applies when valves are installed between reducers.

(5-3)FLp = FL / √{1 + (FL2 Cv2)/(0.1 d4) [1 − (d/D)4]}

Here d = valve size (in), D = pipe size (in), and Fp = the piping geometry factor.

For Fp, the piping geometry factor, see the “Pipe Reducers” section in this chapter.

Đối với dịch vụ chất lỏng For Liquid Service

Subcritical Flow — occurs when ΔP is less than FL2 (P1 − Pv). Critical Flow — occurs when ΔP is more than FL2 (P1 − Pv).

Volumetric Flow

(5-4)Subcritical: Cv = q √(Gf/ΔP), q = Cv √(ΔP/Gf)
Critical: Cv = q/[FL √(Gf(P1 − Pv))], q = Cv FL √(Gf(P1 − Pv))

Flow by Weight

(5-5)Subcritical: Cv = W/[500 √(GfΔP)], W = 500 Cv √(GfΔP)
Critical: Cv = W/[500 FL √(Gf(P1 − Pv))], W = 500 Cv FL √(Gf(P1 − Pv))

where

  • Gf = specific gravity at flowing temperature (water = 1 at 60°F)
  • P1 = absolute inlet pressure, psia
  • P2 = absolute outlet pressure, psia
  • ΔP = P1 − P2
  • Pc = pressure at thermodynamic critical point, psia — water = 3206 psia
  • Pv = vapor pressure of liquid at flowing temperature, psia — water = 0.4 psia at 70°F
  • q = liquid flow rate, US gpm
  • W = flow in pounds per hour

Trường hợp đặc biệt Special Case

When outlet pressure is lower than or equal to vapor pressure, use ΔPs in place of ΔP in the volumetric flow and flow by weight equations.

ΔPs = P1 − [0.96 − 0.28 (Pv/Pc)] Pv

Đối với dịch vụ khí và hơi For Gas and Steam Service

Surprising as it may sound, the basic Cv equation can also be applied to gases and steam. The difference here is that the density of the gas changes with ΔP, and, because this is a gradual process, the relationship to flow is no longer linear but curved. To its credit, the International Society of Automation (ISA) Sizing Committee for ANSI/ISA-75.01.01 has labored hard to predict the exact shape of this curve with the aid of an expansion factor [3], Y, where

(5-6)Y = 1 − X/(3 Fk XT)

Note that Y cannot exceed 1 and that it cannot be less than 0.67 because the latter is exactly the density of the gas, that is, 67% of the inlet density when the valve is choked (when it sees sonic velocity in the valve orifice).

1 There is evidence that the Cv number on certain valve types, such as angle valves, certain rotary valves, and double-ported globe valves, is different between liquid and gases, but we shall ignore this.

In the preceding equation,

  • X = ΔP/P1 (Note: P1 or P2 is always absolute pressure, that is, gauge psi plus 14.7.)
  • Fk = the ratio of the specific heat k of a gas (air = 1.4), Fk = k of gas/1.4
  • XT = pressure drop ratio (this is the ΔP/P1 at which there is no longer any flow increase)

Note that XT includes some flow increase after the onset of choked flow due to changes in the size of the flowing jet (vena contracta).

Now the required flow coefficient becomes:

(5-7)Cv = q √(Gg T1 Z) / [1360 Y √(X P1)]

Or

(5-8)q = 1360 Cv Y √(X P1 / (Gg T1 Z))

where

  • q = flow in standard cubic feet per hour (SCFH)
  • Gg = gas specific gravity (air = 1), also ratio of molecular weight of gas to that of air (air = 28.9)
  • T1 = absolute upstream temperature °R (°F + 460)
  • Z = compressibility factor (Ignore this if your pressure never exceeds 2000 psia; otherwise, consult handbook tables.)

Now, if this begins to tax the capabilities of your handheld calculator, you may ignore all the above and use the simplified version that follows [4]. (The penalty is a maximum sizing error of ≈ 8% at the transition from nonchoked to choked flow and not worth the trouble.) It is also strongly recommended that you use these simplified equations to make sure your computer-generated answers are at least in the ballpark.

Subcritical Flow — when ΔP is less than FL2 (P1/2). Critical Flow — when ΔP is more than FL2 (P1/2).

Volumetric Flow

Subcritical: Cv = q √(Gg T)/[963 √(ΔP (P1 + P2))]
Critical: Cv = q √(Gg T)/(834 FL P1)

Flow by Weight

Subcritical: Cv = W/[32.2 √(Gg ΔP (P1 + P2))]
Critical: Cv = W/(28 FL Gg P1)

For Saturated Steam

Subcritical: Cv = W/[21 √(ΔP(P1 + P2))]
Critical: Cv = W/(18.3 FL P1)

For Superheated Steam

Subcritical: Cv = W (1 + Tsh/1000)0.7/[21 √(ΔP(P1 + P2))]
Critical: Cv = W (1 + Tsh/1000)0.7/(18.3 FL P1)

where

  • Cv = valve coefficient
  • FL = pressure recovery factor
  • Gg = gas specific gravity (air = 1.0)
  • P1 = absolute upstream pressure, psia
  • P2 = absolute downstream pressure, psia
  • ΔP = pressure drop P1 − P2, psi
  • q = gas flow rate at 14.7 psia and 60°F, SCFH
  • T = flow temperature, °R(460 + °F)
  • Tsh = steam superheat, °F
  • W = flow rate, pounds per hour

Note: When a valve is installed between reducers, use Cv Fp instead of Cv in capacity tables (see the “Pipe Reducers” section in this chapter).

Các phương pháp khác Other Methods

Cv = Cg/(40 FL) = Cs/(2 FL); XT = 0.84 FL2/Fk; FL = Fk/FC

Note: XT is not strictly related to FL. While XT gives you the maximum gas flow (which may happen at orifice pressure ratios P1/P0 of 3:1 or more, that is, above sonic velocity), FL2 tells you the onset of sonic velocity, that is, where P1/Porifice ≈ 2, and P1/P2 sonic = P1/(P1 − 0.5 FL2 P1).

Điều kiện dòng chảy đúng là gì? What Are the Right Flow Conditions?

Using a computer and sizing the Cv number to the fifth place after the decimal point is all well and good, but only if you have the right process conditions. First, the valve should be sized to control the maximum flow rate your process is designed for. But what is this exactly? How about emergency conditions? On the other hand, should the valve fail open, would the resultant flow be more than the downstream pressure relief valve could handle? Then again, what is the correct inlet pressure? Can you use the head pressure of the pump from the manufacturer's published curve? (If you do, use the head pressure corresponding to the maximum flow the valve has to pass, and do not ignore the static head at the pump's location.) What is the head loss or pressure drop across a heat exchanger next to the valve? How about line losses? All these are questions that beg only vague responses from your process design people. They do not know the answers and have to rely on the input from other vendors. The result is usually guesswork with ample safety factors thrown in. Therefore, do not be too concerned about the accuracy of sizing equations. Ninety-nine out of one hundred times, the valve is too large for the job anyhow (see Chapter 8 on equal percentage trim). The remaining 1% that is undersized comes about because someone made a decimal point error in the Cv calculation.

If you want to be clever and save your company energy and money in the bargain, have the process people ask you for the valve pressure drop when they design the system (see Chapter 18, “The ABC Method for Gases”). Now you are in the driver's seat. Here are the scientifically derived rules of thumb you should memorize: For ΔP valve sizing, use the higher of 5% of the total system pressure (i.e., static head plus pump head at maximum flow, boiler pressure, etc.), or 5 psi for rotary control valves, or 10 psi for globe valves.

You may wonder why I picked 5 psi for rotary control valves. It just so happens that the flow rate (either liquid or gas) generated by a 5 psi pressure drop through a wide-open control valve with a Cv divided by the valve diameter squared of 172 is all that a pipe (the same size as the valve) can handle, allowing for the customary maximum line velocities of 15 ft/s for liquids or 150 ft/s for gases [5] (see Figure 5-1). Higher ΔP values mandate a valve smaller than the pipe size where reducers are required or when reduced trim is selected.3 In any case, you are throwing away dollars for pump, compressor, or boiler horsepowers if you assign too much pressure drop to the valve (see Chapter 18).

2 A butterfly valve at 60° opening, for example.
3 For example, if a 4-inch butterfly valve is installed in a 6-inch pipe, then at 17 × d2 = 272 Cv, the valve pressure drop increases from 5 psi to 25 psi with 15 ft/s fluid velocity in the 6-inch pipe.

Of course, the abovementioned trick can be used in reverse. You may be requested to replace an old steam reducing valve, vintage 1948. All the records are lost, and the only thing you know is the boiler pressure and the valve and pipe size. Assume the boiler pressure is 95 psig (110 psia) and the pipe size is 1½ in. First, look up the maximum flow rate of a 1½-inch steam pipe at, say, 100 psig pressure. You will find it to be approximately 1800 lb/h (see Table A-2 in Appendix A, “References—Tables and Figures”). Next, assume the flow to be choked, that is, P2 is less than one-half P1. (In any case, people did not care much about energy savings in 1948.) Now calculate the Cv required, which is about 10.5. Go ahead and select a 1½-inch globe valve with a rated Cv of about 22. The worst that can happen is that the outlet pressure is 80 psig instead of below 50 psig. No problem. Now the Cv required is 16, which is still OK. Therefore, when in doubt, use a full-trim size if flow conditions are unknown, except in special applications such as pH control.

Các đoạn thu nhỏ đường ống Pipe Reducers

One of the competitive strategies of some valve manufacturers is to offer a control valve that has a much higher Cv rating than somebody else's valve. These are usually oversized globe valves with reduced flange connections, for example, a 1½-inch flanged globe valve with a 2-inch orifice size. While there is nothing wrong with this concept (after all, you are getting more weight for your dollars), it nevertheless forces you into using pipe reducers, because with a large Cv your valve can be smaller than the attached pipe. One thing you must do is evaluate the extra cost and space requirement. You may also need reducers when you buy rotary control valves, which, because they have less torturous bodies, have inherently higher Cv numbers. They also have higher ΔP values, however these are cancelled out because of low FL numbers (choked flow is dependent on the product of Cv times FL).

A word of caution: Never choose a valve that is less than half the pipe size. Too much stress may be created by the movement of the pipes. The only two times in my life when I was genuinely scared were (1) witnessing a live test with steam on a newly designed steam dump valve for nuclear power and (2) seeing a 1-inch steam makeup valve welded into a 10-inch schedule 80 pipe, reducing 1800 psi steam to 10 psig!

There is a good reason I put this discussion at this point in the chapter. Namely, reducers do not affect you that much. Simply remember that the combined flow coefficient of the valve and reducer is Cv Fp, where Fp is the piping geometry factor (which I used to call R [6], a term that was used by one manufacturer). Some vendors have published tables showing the reduced Cv = (Cv Fp) due to the pressure losses caused by the pipe reducers. Make sure the Cv or Cv Fp value of the valve you select is at least 10% higher than the Cv number calculated with the previous equations. For globe valves at rated Cv values of 10 d2 or less (d is valve size in inches), Fp is practically 1, so it can be ignored. Things are more critical with butterfly or ball valves where Fp can be as low as 0.6!

Here is a quick guide to Fp factors. First, select a valve for one-half the diameter of the pipe; look up the maximum Cv of this valve in your vendor's catalog. Now, divide the valve catalog Cv by the valve diameter d (inches) squared, then read Fp:

Next, calculate the required Cv from the given process date and, finally divide this calculated Cv by Fp. This is the final Cv that should be stated on your purchase order. Note: You may have to start over if the originally selected valve was not large enough, although this is rare.

For example, you have a 4-inch pipe. Next, select a 2-inch eccentric rotary plug valve with a catalog Cv of 60. Cv/d2 is 15. This gives an Fp factor of 0.91 from Table 5-1. Now, determine the process data call for a required Cv of 45. Dividing 45 by 0.91 requires a minimum rated Cv of 49.5. This is not a problem, because the catalog Cv is 60.

Hiệu chỉnh theo độ nhớt Correcting for Viscosity

Because this procedure is not often encountered, I put it last. Ignore this unless (1) you have a liquid with a viscosity exceeding 40 centistokes (or = 40 centipoises for liquids), or (2) you need really small valves with a Cv of less than 0.1.

Similar to the reducer correction, we have to increase the Cv from that calculated with the standard sizing equations to make up for the additional friction caused by the stickiness of the matter passing through the valve. This is expressed by the valve Reynolds number factor, FR. The valve Cv you really need = Cv originally calculated/FR. More precisely,

(5-9)Cv,corrected = Cv,turbulent / FR

where Cv,turbulent = q √(Gf/ΔP) (See the valve sizing formulas on page 25.)

The FR number varies with the internal fluid resistance (K factor) of each valve style [8], that is, Cv/do2 and the FL factor (for graphical values, see Figure 5-2). If flow is completely laminar:

(5-11)FR = 0.026 (FK/Fd) Rev0.5 = 0.78 × 0.026 Cv FL Rev0.5/(Fd ν d2)

or, if flow is transitional:

(5-12)FR = (Rev/10000)n

where n = 1 + (890 d4)/(CvR2 FL2) + log(Rev).

Because you do not know if the flow is laminar or transitional, calculate FR with both equations and then use the smaller of the two numbers in Equation 5-6 (or get FR from Figure 5-2). Ignore the viscosity correction completely if Rev is above 10,000.

For control valves with full-size trim, that is, where the Cv/d2 exceeds 8, and further assuming a line-size valve and assuming the fluid flow is fully laminar, the Cv can be estimated as follows:

(5-13)Cv = 0.194 q (Gf/ΔP)0.5 [ν/(FL2 Fd K d2)]2/3

For globe valves, you may substitute 0.9 for FL, 10.0 for K, and 0.46 for Fd. This then makes Cv,Globe = 0.059 q ν2/3 √(Gf/ΔP).

Example: q = 180 gpm; ΔP = 30 psi; v = 10,000 centistokes; Gf = 0.6. Cv,Globe = 0.059 × 180 × 10,0002/3 √(0.6/30) = 64. This result means you should select a 3-inch globe valve (rated Cv about 105).

For a contoured ball-type control valve with K = 1.4; Fd = 0.8; FL = 0.6 (from Table 5-5 in the “What Size Valve to Choose” section of this chapter), Equation 5-13 simplifies to Cv,Ball = 0.077 q ν2/3 √(Gf/ΔP).

For the previous example, this would yield a Cv of 83.7. Again, a 3-inch contoured ball valve with a rated Cv of approximately 225 will be appropriate for this job. (Note the uncorrected Cv—assuming turbulent flow is only 25.5!)

If you do not know whether the flow is laminar, calculate the required Cv either by the turbulent equation Cv = q √(Gf/ΔP) or by Equation 5-9. Now, choose the larger Cv number. Admittedly, this method is not too accurate in the transitional flow area (errors of up to 20% are possible), but it is simple and easy to use for an approximation.

Định cỡ van lưu lượng nhỏ Small Flow Valve Sizing

Now for small flow valves (Cv ≤ 0.1) FL ≈ 1.0, we calculate the valve Reynolds number Rev as before, except we use different Fd factors,10 namely

  • 0.7 for splined plugs
  • 0.3 for short travel diaphragm valves with a flat seating surface
  • Fd = 0.09 √(Cv)/(plug diameter), for close clearance tapered plugs

Example: For a 1/8-inch-diameter close clearance plug with a Cv = 0.05: Fd = 0.09 √0.05/0.125 = 0.16.

For liquids:

(5-14)Rev = 17300 Fq q / [ν d (CvR FL/d2)0.5]

For gases:

(5-15)Rev = 2153 Fq q / [ν d (CvR FL/d2)0.5]

where q = flow in SCFH at 14.7 psia and 60°F. Now calculate FR:

(5-16)FR = (Rev/10000)1/n ≤ 1 (max.)
(5-17)n = 1 + K + log(Rev)

and because K for small flow valves = 1, n = 2 + log(Rev). For example:

Fluid: CO2 gas at 85°F (T = 545°R); P1 = 36.3 psia; P2 = 24.7 psia; ν = kinematic viscosity = 8.65 cSt at 85°F and 14.7 psia (Note: use ν at atmospheric pressure if you use q in SCFH); Gg = specific gravity = 1.516; Cv = turbulent valve sizing coefficient (selected) = 0.025; FL = liquid pressure recovery factor = 1 (from manufacturer's catalog); Fd = valve style modifier = 0.16 (from manufacturer's catalog); q = desired maximum flow rate = 15 SCFH at 85°F and 14.7 psi absolute; T = 460 + 85 = 545°R.

From Equation 5-15, Rev = 2153(0.16)(15)/[8.65 √(0.025)] = 3778.

and from Equation 5-16, n = 2 + log(3778) = 5.58, so FR = (3778/10000)1/5.58 = 0.84.

Now, determine if the selected valve Cv of 0.025 is sufficient to handle the flow of 15 SCFH. Measure turbulent Cv from the turbulent gas equation for subcritical flow5 and modified by FR: Cv = q √(GgT) FR √(P1/ΔP)/(963 P1 Y). With P1 − P2 = 11.6 psi, this gives Cv = 0.0193.

The selected valve Cv of 0.025 is OK.

Định cỡ van điều khiển cho chất lưu nhớt: một giải thích Sizing Control Valves for Viscous Fluids: An Explanation

I always wondered how a ball valve with hardly any obstruction can pass the same amount of tar6 for a given pressure drop and flow coefficient (Cv) as a cage valve with, for example, 100 small, drilled holes even though it has the same Cv as the ball valve. Instinct tells us this could not be so despite the fact the previous ISA standard told us so. Not believing it, I decided to do something about it and came up with the revised method presented in this section.

6 Ignoring the FL factor, which affects the Reynolds number.

The FR values for small flow valves derived from my new equations did not agree with values given in Figure 1 of the former ISA-75.01.01, Flow Equations for Sizing Control Valves (now revised) and they yield more accurate results typically increasing the required Cv for small flow valves by a factor of four or more over the current method in Reynolds numbers below 100.5 The former ISA (single) FR curve was based on a limited number of tests with full-sized globe valves, whereas the new values are based on air and water tests with small flow valves, or rotary valve types in addition to globe valves.

5 Remember, there is no sonic velocity if flow is nonturbulent; therefore, critical flow equations do not apply.

From data published in Perry's Chemical Engineers Handbook, sixth edition, Table 5-15, it appears that more streamlined valves (e.g., angle valves and gate valves) exhibit FR factors close to the Figure 5-2 curves, while disk and plug-style globe valves (due to their more abrupt flow pattern) tend to favor the former ISA FR values when corrected with the proper Fd values.

From this data, it appears that the “pseudo Cv,” that is, the “laminar flow Cv,” or CvFRFL, is identical for all valve types with full-size trim and at low Reynolds numbers (Rev < 100).

From this, the required Cv (valve capacity needed to pass viscous flow for a given Reynolds number) is Cv = q Re/(0.026 K Ap), where 0.026 is the area in square inch per 1 Cv when the head loss coefficient K = 1. Ap is the cross-sectional area of the given pipe size in inches squared (same as the valve size!).

  • K = velocity head loss coefficient of valve trim where K = (38 × A/Cv)2
  • A = flow area of trim in in2

Furthermore, from the ISA equation, Cv,Req = Ci/FR, where Ci is the Cv calculated assuming the flow pattern is completely turbulent; this makes Cv = Re/(ApFL). Because the valve Reynolds number for liquids is Re = 17300 Fq q/[ν d (CvRFL/d2)0.5], the equation labelled (5-13) above gives Cv,req = 0.194 q (Gf/ΔP)0.5 [ν/(FL2Fd K d2)]2/3.

Because FL is approximately proportional to the relative valve capacity (Cv/d2), we may, for estimating purposes only, substitute FL ≅ 0.96[1 − 0.0025(Cv/d2)]2/15 in Equation 5-13.

Furthermore, Fd is somewhat related to FL (e.g., a large valve, such as a ball valve, has a low FL but a high Fd). We can thus further estimate that FL = Fd = 0.4/0.89 = 0.42 and, finally, that √K/d is involved.

This then makes Equation 5-13: Cv = 0.00386 q √(Gf/ΔP) [0.96 − 0.0025(Cv/d2)]2/15 (ΔP/d4)2/3.

Note: The selected Cv should not be less than 10 d2 and no more than 30 d2.

I suggest you input the customer's pipe size for d. The previous equation will give you a ballpark Cv number, which can aid in selecting a valve type. Using the actual valve coefficients (rated Cv, FL, and Fd), recalculate the required Cv using Equation 5-9.

Đơn vị mét Metric Units

It might not bother you that the rest of the world thinks in metric terms. However, an occasion may arise when you will have to do a project for an overseas plant, and you will be confronted with liquid flow rates of cubic meters per hour instead of gpm. Luckily, the Cv numbers work equally as well in Europe. For example:

(5-18)Cv = 1.17 q √(Gf/ΔP)
  • q = m3/h of liquid
  • ΔP = valve pressure drop in bar
  • Gf = specific gravity (water at 60°F = 1)

In Germany, they go so far as to eliminate the 1.17 constant and then call the flow coefficient Kv; therefore, Kv = 0.85 Cv, or Cv = 1.17 Kv.

Complications arise only if you are confronted by Newton per square meter, also called pascal or Pa. To get back to the more familiar bar, divide Pa by 100,000 or one bar = 100,000 Pa, and one bar = 14.5 psi. One kilopascal (kPa) = 0.145 psi. There is also the less often used kg/cm2, which is 0.98 bar or 14.2 psi.

Remember also that degrees Celsius is used abroad where °F = (°C × 9/5) + 32, and in absolute terms, °K = 273 + °C. Converting flowing units:

  • 1 m3/h liquid = 4.4 gpm
  • 1 m3/h gas = 35.3 ft3/h
  • kg/h = 2.2 lb/h

You are now fully equipped to work abroad.

Chọn cỡ van nào What Size Valve to Choose

After you have labored hard to select the final required valve Cv suitable for maximum flow requirements, and having considered the F effects (FL, Fp, FR), you are now confronted with the final choice—the valve size.

If the pressure drop is low, say, 5 to 15 psi, it is always safe to start with a line-size valve. Keep in mind that the person in the piping design department already sized the downstream or upstream pipe to pass the design flow rate at a reasonable velocity. Incidentally, valve outlet velocity should not bother you unless there could be a noise problem with high-pressure gas or steam reduction (Chapter 20, “Methods of Aerodynamic Valve Noise Reduction,” discusses aerodynamic noise), or where you have flashing liquids (if the downstream pipe is too long, your valve will cavitate because of downstream pressure buildup caused by high mixed-phase velocity). This then leaves only the Cv rating of the valve. In case you have not yet selected a certain brand because you are partial to the color of the valve, that is, green or red, see Table 5-5 for a hint.

The Cv values are per valve diameter (inch) squared. The Cv values in parentheses are per pipe diameter (inch) squared. For example, a 6-inch eccentric rotary plug valve has a wide open Cv flow coefficient of 62 × 12 = 432. However, when installed between reducers in a 12-inch pipe, the available flow coefficient is only 62 × 11.3 = 407 (see column Cv/d2 for D/d = 2), or 122 × 2.8 = 403 using the figures in parentheses.

The above values are conservative and vary somewhat from size to size. You may notice that when high-capacity valves are used under choked flow conditions (such as steam pressure reduction), they all tend to lose their high Cv advantage, as you can see from the CvFL/d2 column! Explain this to your valve vendor.

Starting out with a line-size valve, divide your desired maximum Cv value first. This will give you a 10% safety factor, and it will guard against the ±10% tolerance in the Cv rating of the valve given by the manufacturer. Now divide this new Cv number by the pipe size squared. For example, from the flow data, you require a maximum Cv = 123. Your line size is 4 in. First divide 123 by 0.8 to obtain 154. Now divide this by (4 in)2. 154/42 = 9.6 Cv/d2.

From the data given in Table 5-4, you have the following choices:

  • A 4-inch single-seated globe valve, Cv/d2 = 10; Cv rated = 10 × 42 = 160.
  • A butterfly valve at 70° opening (if other considerations such as leakage, noise, etc., permit). Here a 3-inch butterfly valve installed in a 4-inch pipe has a flow capacity higher than 10.2 × D2 (see the figures in parentheses), that is, more than 42 × 10.2 = 163 Cv.

This is usually 5% with tetrafluoroethylene (TFE) packing to 15% for laminated graphite packing if no positioner is used.
