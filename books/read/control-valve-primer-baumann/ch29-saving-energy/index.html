Saving Energy Saving Energy

As I pointed out in Chapter 5, you should only use 5% of the total system pressure or 5 to 10 psi as the allowed pressure drop across the control valve at the maximum flow rate. It will save your company a lot of electrical energy for pumps and compressors. This is contrary to the old rule of using about 50% of the dynamic head pressure to achieve a more linear installed flow characteristic (see Chapter 8).

Because the power converted in a control valve is similar to that used in a pump to create the pressure in the first place, it can be calculated as

P = 4.2 × 10−4 × ΔP × q in kW

where

  • q = flow in US gallons per minute (gpm) of liquid
  • ΔP = pressure drop in psi

For example, in a valve application for 120 gpm oil at a Gf of 0.8, extra pump horsepower is needed (a bigger motor) if a 50 psi pressure drop is assigned to the valve:

P = 4.2 × 10−4 × 50 × 120 = 2.5 kW

To reduce the pressure, you need a valve size of only 1½ in. The Cv required is 15.1.

By reducing the valve pressure drop at maximum flow to only 5 psi and choosing a different pump, the energy required for the valve is now only 0.25 kW, a savings of 2.5 − 0.25 = 2.25 kW per hour. This amounts to 20,000 kW per year! True, the valve size now has to go up to 2 in to accommodate the higher Cv (48) based on the 5 psi pressure drop; however, the cost savings of about $2,000 per year more than pays for the cost difference between the two valve sizes. Besides, the line size for 120 gpm flow is 2 in (see Table A-1), so you also save the cost of pipe reducers.

Another benefit besides energy (cost) savings is less valve repair because there is no trim vibration that could be caused by a higher pressure drop and no cavitation to wear out the trim.

In an article, Hans O. Engel [1] pointed out that Germany’s three largest chemical producers use about 120,000 pumps, with an average power consumption of 3 kW, cooperating with about 200,000 control valves. If all valves are sized more rationally (i.e., as proposed herein), then about half the power, or 180 MW of electric energy, could be saved. This, in turn, translates to a yearly savings of about 1.5 million MWh. It is fair to assume that the total US chemical industry is about three times the size of the German representation. This means a possible savings of 4 to 5 million MWh per year in the United States.

It is enlightening to realize how much energy a control valve converts when it throttles, that is, when it does its assigned job of reducing pressure. The following equations by E. W. Singleton [2] help you do this.

For liquids For liquids

Nonchoked:

kWh = (Cv/45) (ΔP/Gf)0.5

Choked:

kWh = CvFL[(P1 − P2)(FL2)]0.5 / [45(Gf)0.5]

For gases at choked flow For gases at choked flow

kWh = 0.014 CvFL3P1

where

  • All pressures are in psia
  • Cv = flow coefficient at desired flow conditions
  • FL = pressure recovery factor at required travel
  • Gf = specific gravity at flowing conditions (water = 1.0)

This discussion should not be taken as an endorsement of speed-controlled pumps. These have their own problems; see Chapter 2, “Why Not Use a Speed-Controlled Pump?”
