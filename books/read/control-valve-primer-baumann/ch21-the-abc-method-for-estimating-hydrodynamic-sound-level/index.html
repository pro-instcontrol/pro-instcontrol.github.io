The ABC Method for Estimating Hydrodynamic Sound Level

Hans D. Baumann · Control Valve Primer, 5th ed. (ISA / Wiley)

Chapter 21 · 3 source pages · companion notes
Key terms. Disclaimer Disclaimer

Disclaimer. The proposed method is valid only for single-outlet ported control valves such as globe or line-of-sight valves with turbulent fluid (Rev > 10.000). Note: Cavitation, while most common with water, can also happen with other fluids such as crude oil.

Noise reduction trim such as multiported trims or resistance-in-series types are covered in Chapter 20.

Similar to the ABC method for gases, this method not only predicts turbulent liquid sound but it also can accurately predict additional sound sources produced by cavitating liquids (actually aerodynamic phenomena).

Pipe diameter downstream of valve D in inches (D = d / 25.4 if d is in mm), P₁ in psia (bara × 14.5), P₂ in psia (bara × 14.5).

D = d / 25.4 (d in mm)
P₁ [psia] = p [bara] × 14.5; P₂ [psia] = p [bara] × 14.5

Input Needed Input Needed

Cᵥ = flow coefficient (at operating conditions).

F_L = pressure recovery coefficient without expander at actual travel position.

X_Fz = coefficient of incipient cavitation (see Table 21-1).

P_v = liquid vapor pressure in psia.

r_w = valve type modifier (3 for globe valves).

Initial Calculations Initial Calculations

X = (P₁ − P₂) / (P₁ − P_v)
Xy = X at maximum cavitation = (1 + X_Fz) / 2
E = exponent = 45 (1/√X_Fz)

Calculation Steps Calculation Steps

A = 8 log(D) + 12 log(Cᵥ/D²) + 4 dB

where A is in dB and D is the pipe diameter in inches.

B = 35 log(P₁) − 35 for US, or 35 log(14.5 p) − 35 if p is in bara
c₁ = 30 log(10X), X maximum = F_L²

This equation gives the turbulent sound level in dBA at 1 m.

c₂ = 60 log(X / X_Fz) if X is above X_Fz but below Xy
c₂ = 60 log(Xy / X_Fz) if X is above Xy; otherwise use zero

This defines the increasing cavitation sound level in dB.

c₃ = E log(X / Xy) if X is larger than Xy; otherwise use zero

This denotes a decreasing cavitation sound level in dB.

C = c₁ + c₂ − c₃ dBA
L_pAe1m = A + B + C − r_w dBA at 1 m, plus modifiers

Modifiers Modifiers

Schedule 40 = 0; schedule 80 = −3 dB; schedule 160 = −6 dB

For liquids other than water, add:

20 log[(62.5 / ρ_liq) + 25 log(4750 / c_liq)]

where ρ_liq is the density of the chosen liquid (lb/ft³) and c_liq is the speed of sound of the chosen liquid (ft/s).

For pipe materials other than steel, add:

20 log(7800 / ρ_material)

where ρ_material is the density of the chosen material, in kg/m³.

Example Example

A 6-inch (150 mm) butterfly valve in a 6-inch pipe has the following data:

Cᵥ = 180 A = 8 log(6) + 12 log(5) + 4 = 18.6 dB
P₁ = 115 psia B = 35 log(115) − 35 = 37.1 dB
P₂ = 46 psia c₁ = 30 log(10X) because X is less than F_L² = 23.4 dB
F_L = 0.9 c₂ = 60 log(0.60 / 0.3) = 18 dB
X_Fz = 0.3 c₃ = zero because X ≤ Xy
Cᵥ/D² = 5 C = c₁ + c₂ − c₃ = 41.4 dB
P_v = 0.4 psia because X is smaller than Xy
Xy = (1 + 0.3) / 2 = 0.65
X = 0.6
L_pAe1m = A + B + C = 18.6 + 37.1 + 41.4 = 97.1 dBA

[Test data showed 94 dBA.]

Additional Test Points Additional Test Points

See Figures 22-1, 22-2, and 22-3 in Chapter 22, “Graphical Estimation of Noise Produced by Turbulence and Cavitation of Liquids.”

X = 0.3, Est. 70 dBA, test = 66 dBA
X = 0.8, Est. 92.1 dBA, test = 91 dBA

Here we have to distinguish between noise produced by turbulent flow (only important if large pipes are involved) and noise produced by cavitation. The latter is more common and can create sound levels in excess of 90 dBA even with valves as small as 1 in.
