Part 1: Sound Level Estimate for Valves Having a Standard Trim Part 1: Sound Level Estimate for Valves Having a Standard Trim

In contrast to vendor-dominated, empirical IEC standard 834-8-3, the ABC method and user-friendly ISA standard 75.17 [1] are open and based only on scientific laws. In addition, they are easily programmable [2].

This chapter consists of two parts. Part 1 has three sections: A, B, and C. When the parts are combined, and after adding a few discussed modifiers, one can determine an aerodynamic sound level for a control valve with a schedule 40 outlet pipe and a straight valve outlet port. Please consult Appendix C, “How Does the ABC Method Work?” to understand the algorithms used in this method.

Note that I did not specify a particular valve type. As a matter of fact, this method accommodates all available reciprocating or rotary valve types, or even designs not invented yet, as long as the valve has a schedule 40 outlet pipe and a straight valve outlet port. Modifiers are provided for adjusting the calculations when the pipes are thicker than schedule 40 and fluids other than air are used.

Disclaimer. The proposed method is valid only for single-ported control valves, such as globe or line-of-sight valves, and for turbulent fluids (Rev > 10,000).

Section A Section A

(18-1)A = 25 log(D) − 10 + 20 log(Cᵥ/D²) dBA

where Cᵥ = flow coefficient of the valve under actual flow and test conditions; D = outside diameter of the pipe at the valve’s outlet (inches).

Section B Section B

In the B section, we simply have:

(18-2)B = 35 log(P₁) − 35 dBA

Section C Section C

(18-3)C = 27 + 30 + 30 log(X) + 20 log(F_d) dBA

As I explained previously, F_d is a factor that essentially determines the diameter of a gas jet. Remember, the smaller the jet, the higher the fp and the transmission loss. This is why we have to subtract F_d. Note, 20 log(F_d) is negative because all F_d numbers are below 1 (for typical Fd values, see Table 5-4).

(18-4)A + B + C + G

Note: G is tabulated in Table 18-1.

where G = a correction actor for gases other than air.

Adding the Parts and Modifiers Adding the Parts and Modifiers

All we have to do now is add all the results of A + B + C and then add two modifiers: ΔF_L and the sound power resistance factor (r_w). Now we have the 1m distance sound pressure level in dBA for a schedule 40 pipe wall. Note: G is a modifier to account for gases other than air (see below). G for air = 0. This gives:

(18-5)A + B + C + ΔF_L − r_w + G plus other modifiers

The first modifier is ΔF_L, which accounts for the case where the actual F_L differs from an assumed 0.7 (average F_L). To correct for ΔF_L, use:

(18-6)ΔF_L = 30 log(actual F_L / 0.7)

Rw = 3 dB for globe valves only. A resistance factor accounting for the torturous flow path in a globe valve housing.

Example Example

An 8-inch segmented ball valve, Cv = 134, F_L = 0.6, F_d = 0.40, P₁ = 37.5 psia, P₂ = 14.5 psia. The outlet pipe (D) = 16 in. This makes X = 0.6 and Cv/D² = 2.1.

From the above equations, A = 17.5, B = 20, C = 48.7, and ΔF_L = −2. The total = 17.5 + 20 + 48.7 − 2 = 84.7 dBA. This agrees with the test data of 85 dBA in Figure 18-1 for 10X = .6.

Other Modifiers: For gases other than air, add the following: ps = 20 log(Ci gas/1126) + 15 log(M gas use/28.97) from the PLa subtotal, where Ci is the sonic speed of the gas used, in ft/sec.

For pipe thickness: Schedule 40 = 0, Schedule 80 = −4, dB Schedule 160 = −8 dB.

Part 2: Method for Estimating Sound Levels for Sound-Reducing Valve Trims Part 2: Method for Estimating Sound Levels for Sound-Reducing Valve Trims

2.1 Single-Stage, Multipassage Trim 2.1 Single-Stage, Multipassage Trim

Example: Drilled holes in valve plugs or cages.

Method: Use the equations from Part 1 with the following values for F_d: F_d for equally sized holes = 1/(n_o^0.5), where n_o is the number of equally sized passages exposed under actual flow conditions.

Note: This method is only valid if the distance between holes is no less than one hole diameter.

For rectangular passages, utilize F_d = (d_h / d_o) × (1 / n_o^0.5), where d_h = 4 a_p / 2 l_p, and d_o = (a_p × 4 / 3.14)^0.5 n_os^0.5.

where d_o = the equivalent circular orifice; d_h = the hydraulic diameter of one slot; l_p = the length of a slot; n_os = number of rectangular slots; a_p = the flow area if a single passage. Note: All dimensions are in inches.

2.2 Multistage, Single-Passage Valve Trim 2.2 Multistage, Single-Passage Valve Trim

Example: Cone-shaped plugs having equally spaced circular rims intersecting with similar concave recessions in mating elements.

Method: Use the results from Part 1 Equation 18-1 and subtract ΔAR, where ΔAR = 10 log(AR). AR is the ratio between the flow area of the exit flow passage and the flow passage at the entrance.

Example: A globe valve having a single flow page with multistage trim has a Cv of 10 and a pipe diameter of 2 in, reducing 200 psia to 14.5 psia. The trim has 8 stages, an area ratio of 7, an F_L of 0.8, and an F_d of 0.9. X = 0.93.

Using the equations from Part 1, the result is 104.9 dBA − 10 log(7) = 96.4 dBA.

2.3 Multistep and Multipath Trims 2.3 Multistep and Multipath Trims

Method: Use the procedure from Part 2.2 and subtract 20 log(n_op^0.5), where n_op = the number of equally sized multistep flow paths for given flow conditions.

Example: Using the example from Part 2.2 and assuming the trim has 12 equally sized paths (n_op), the result is 96.4 dBA (from above) −20 log(12^0.5) = 85.6 dBA.

For an explanation of how such a simple method works, see Appendix C. If this is still too complicated, consult Chapter 19, “The Graphical ABC Method for Gases.”
