High-Recovery Valves

From potential energy to inlet-pressure control Từ năng lượng thế sang điều khiển bằng áp suất thượng lưu

High-recovery valves are used to replace conventional control valves in process control across a range of industries. They are used primarily to control fuel gas to power-plant turbine generators, where pressure drop diminishes the efficiency of the turbines. All conventional valves operate on the principle of converting potential energy (upstream pressure) into kinetic energy (fluid velocity), then, depending on the desired downstream pressure, converting kinetic energy into turbulence whose magnitude corresponds to the pressure differential across the valve. Effective though this is, it is relatively inefficient because it reduces the available potential energy downstream of the valve. High-recovery valves, by contrast, operate as follows: once choked flow is reached by reducing downstream pressure below 10% of inlet pressure, effective flow control is achieved simply by modulating inlet pressure. This also simplifies the control algorithms.

Tests on a valve similar to the one in Figure 6-1 showed that choked flow could be achieved with only a 9 psi pressure drop at 100 psia inlet pressure at 10% travel, and only a 10% pressure drop out of 100 psia at 100% travel. That compares with a 36% pressure drop out of 100 psia for a fully open standard globe valve with an FL of 0.85 in order to achieve such flow control.

Venturi contours and isentropic recompression Đường biên dạng venturi và nén lại đẳng entropy

Newer valve types (see Figure 6-1) try to reduce this energy loss by using venturi-type contours downstream of the valve's throttling orifice. When the outlet pressure is reduced sufficiently, the velocity within the throttling orifice reaches sonic velocity for gaseous fluids. The gas gradually expands within the venturi section until the desired downstream pressure is reached, thus avoiding most of the energy lost to turbulence. This process is called isentropic recompression. The amount of avoided turbulence depends on how streamlined the fluid path is from the inlet port to the outlet of the valve. This requires that the cross-sectional flow areas evenly decrease toward the throttling area at the seat and gradually expand thereafter, thus avoiding sudden velocity changes.

Valve types currently used for this service include segmented ball valves and streamlined angle valves. Although ball valves perform well at high flow rates with FL numbers of about 0.4, they lose efficiency at low flow rates because of higher FL numbers at low travel. Angle valves suffer from head losses due to the curved inlet port. The author is surprised that this concept is little used in other industrial applications; such valves could, in his view, be used to fill tanks with gas or steam, with the filling rate modulated by varying the inlet pressure to the valve. The question arises: because high-recovery valves work well for gases, why not employ them for liquids as well?

Water tests at Utah State University Thử nghiệm với nước tại Đại học Bang Utah

Indeed, they can offer equal benefits. Flow tests conducted at the Utah State University Water Research Laboratory found that high-recovery valves have liquid pressure recovery factors FL as low as 0.4 and, even better, that such low numbers extend through all travel ranges. This means that such valves exhibit choked flow at pressure drops as low as 19 psi out of 100 psia. This makes them ideal for applications where either a constant flow or a constant downstream pressure is demanded, as listed in Tables 6-1A and B.

Comparison with eccentric rotary plug valves So sánh với van cắm quay lệch

These tables show significant benefits of using high-recovery valves compared with conventional control valves such as an eccentric rotary plug valve. The obvious benefit of constant flow control is that one can vary the travel of the high-recovery valve to change the downstream pressure while keeping the inlet pressure constant. This means a constant pump head. A choked eccentric plug valve, in contrast, needs variations in inlet pressure to maintain a constant flow when the outlet pressure varies.

Similar differences are shown in Table 6-1B when trying to maintain a constant downstream pressure (a typical pressure reduction application) with variations in flow rates. Here too, the inlet pressure of the conventional valve had to be varied when changing travel to accommodate changing flow rates.

Cavitation and noise Xâm thực và tiếng ồn

One might wonder whether the high-recovery valve will cavitate excessively with an average low incipient cavitation number fxz of 0.15. Surprisingly not, due to two factors. First, because of the low FL number, incipient cavitation generated terminates at a benign level of 3 to 5 dBA above turbulence. More significant cavitation noise can be expected when the pressure drop exceeds about 30% of P1, even though it does not reach damage level. Keep in mind that high pressure drops are not needed for flow control when the pressure ratio equals FL2 (see Chapter 15). In this case, the maximum turbulent noise only terminates at:

30 log(10 × FL² / fxz) = 3.3 dB

Second, Xy, the point of maximum cavitation in this case, is a low 0.55. At this pressure ratio, there is practically no turbulence sound used to base the cavitation sound on.

The graph in Figure 6-2 shows that a maximum cavitation sound level is reached at X = 0.55, as predicted. Still, the overall sound level is about 5 to 8 dB below that of a conventional valve using basically the same input data except for a 0.75 FL factor.

Energy savings Tiết kiệm năng lượng

Finally, also important is the energy savings offered by the high-recovery valve. As shown in Table 6-1B, the standard valve needs between 76 and 120 psi pressure drop, compared with only 20 psi for the high-recovery valve. This translates directly into pump horsepower savings.
