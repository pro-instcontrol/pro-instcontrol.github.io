Methods of Aerodynamic Valve Noise Reduction Methods of Aerodynamic Valve Noise Reduction

Now to the more common occurrence of “noise pollution,” that is, aerodynamic noise. From the preceding equations for estimating throttling noise levels it becomes apparent that two basic variables are mainly responsible for the sound level and therefore can be varied to achieve a desired noise reduction: the jet velocity (Uvc) and the internal peak noise frequency (fp).

Jet velocity is the most important element in noise generation, yet the most difficult one to deal with. Because the velocity is a fraction of the internal orifice pressure ratio P₁/P₀, where P₀ is the static pressure inside the orifice, manipulating this ratio is the only way to reduce velocity. The printed relation is:

P₁ × F_L²

Subsonic Regime I Subsonic Regime I

In subsonic Regime I, that is, P₁/P₀ ≤ 1.9, selecting a valve trim that is less streamlined — one with a high F_L number (0.9 to 1.0) — gives a lower jet velocity and therefore a lower sound level. At higher pressure ratios the only remedy is to use orifices or resistance paths in series so that, ideally, each sub-jet operates in the subsonic regime. Figure 20-1 shows such a valve with a multistep resistance trim (the zig-zag path).

P₁/P₀ ≤ 1.9

Regimes II and III Regimes II and III

For Regimes II and III, that is, P₁/P₂ > P₁/P₂ critical, flow is sonic up to Mach 1.4:

(20-1)ηm = −40 + 37 log[(P₁/P₂)/(P₁/P₂ critical)] + 10 log(F_L²) dB

Worked example: natural gas Worked example: natural gas

Assume an application in which natural gas at an inlet pressure of 1500 psia is throttled down to 750 psia, and take the valve pressure recovery factor as 0.9. The internal pressure ratio P₁/P₀ for a single-stage reduction would have to be P₁/P₂ = 2.35:1, yielding a modified acoustical efficiency of −34.8 dB from Equation 20-1. Using instead the ten-step trim of Figure 20-1, the pressure ratio across the orifice at each step is only 1.089:1, with a modified acoustical efficiency of −63.1 dB — a 28.3 dB noise saving (ignoring other factors). Ignoring the partial addition of ten separate sound power sources, this represents about 29 dB of noise reduction; the actual saving once added sources and the change in frequency are accounted for would be about 20 dB.

Multi-orifice trim and frequency shift Multi-orifice trim and frequency shift

A less costly way to reduce “audible” throttling noise is to increase the peak frequency using a multi-orifice trim as in Figure 20-2. Throttling is still single-stage, but the jets are divided into several parallel ports. This lowers the valve style modifier Fd because:

Fd = 1/(n₀)^0.5

where n₀ is the number of equally sized parallel ports. Because Fd is proportional to the jet diameter Dj, the frequency can be shifted upward, which increases pipe transmission loss. With n₀ = 16, Fd is 0.25 and the resultant peak frequency is 1/0.25, or four times that of a single orifice (Fd = 1) with the same total flow area:

n₀ = 16 → Fd = 0.25 → f_peak = 1/0.25 = 4×

If the peak frequency falls in the mass-controlled region of the pipe, which is most likely, an overall reduction in the external (audible) sound level is achieved:

ΔT_Lfp = 20 log(f_pl / f^0.25) = 12 dB

(unless a large pipe is involved). This approach is limited to about a 3:1 pressure ratio across the cage wall; at higher ratios the jets may combine downstream and negate the benefits of the high frequencies. One caution: Equation 20-1 works only where the peak internal sound frequency fp is no more than two octaves (4×) above the pipe ring frequency fr:

f_p ≤ 4 f_r

Below this ratio the internal sound shifts from 20 dB per octave to only 10 dB per octave. This typically occurs when a valve with low-noise trim (high fp) is installed in a large pipe, 12 in and larger.

Labyrinth cage trim Labyrinth cage trim

A more elaborate arrangement is shown in Figure 20-3: the multistep is combined with the multipath approach in layers of washer-like disks with individually cast or etched channels. The design brings both the beneficial effect of reduced velocity and the benefit of higher transmission loss from the increased frequency. Typical noise reductions are up to 30 dB.

Two-stage multiarea trim Two-stage multiarea trim

Figure 20-4 shows a recent improvement over the labyrinth trim: multiple identical layers of laser-cut plates that between them create a three-dimensional flow path, typically from a single streamlined passage on the inside of each disk to multiple nonstreamlined rectangular outlet passages at the outer circumference. The ratio of outlet to inlet passages is dictated by the overall pressure ratio across the valve. For maximum noise reduction the outlet stage is kept subsonic (where a less streamlined path has lower acoustic efficiency) while the first stage sees supersonic velocities. The streamlined passages act as supersonic diffusers, converting most of the pressure-reduction energy into shock waves rather than into turbulence, which is generally more sound-producing. A good portion of the sound energy from the first stage is also retained in the “settling space,” the vertical passage between the two stages. With an outlet-to-inlet area ratio of 6:1, the first-stage pressure ratio is typically 5:1, while the overall ratio across both stages can be as high as 8:1 with optimized low-noise performance. Because there is no long, tortuous path, this trim is much narrower than a typical labyrinth trim and therefore has more flow capacity for a given trim size. It is also less likely that the passages will plug if dust particles are present in the fluid — a problem typically associated with low gas velocities and fine flow passages.

Fluted butterfly valve with a downstream restrictor Fluted butterfly valve with a downstream restrictor

A more economical solution couples a static downstream pressure-reducing device, such as a multiple-hole restrictor, with a throttling valve such as the fluted-disk butterfly valve of Figure 20-5. The fluted disk generates multiple parallel jets with increased noise frequencies, much like a multiport cage valve, but most of the pressure energy is converted by the static plate, giving a noise reduction at maximum flow of about 20 dB. At maximum design flow the valve is sized for a low pressure ratio of 1.1 or 1.15, with the rest of the pressure reduction across the static plate, whose multistage, multihole design is inherently low-noise. Placing a silencer downstream of a valve is not cost-effective because its reduction is limited to about 10 dB: a good portion of the sound power travels upstream (particularly in line-of-sight valves such as butterfly and ball valves) or radiates through the valve body and actuator. Similar concerns limit acoustic pipe-wall insulation to about 15 dB.

Summary of valve noise abatement procedures Summary of valve noise abatement procedures

The following is a summary of valve noise abatement procedures:

  • If Cv × F_L × P₁ is less than 1000, then no abatement is needed (SL ≤ 90 dBA).
  • Use 1-inch-thick pipe insulation for a 5 to 10 dB reduction.
  • Double the pipe wall thickness for a 6 dB reduction.
  • Place a silencer downstream for a 10 dB reduction.
  • Place silencers both upstream and downstream for a 20 dB reduction.
  • Place a multiport resistance plate downstream of the valve for a 15 to 20 dB reduction (use only 5% to 10% of valve inlet pressure as valve ΔP at maximum flow).
  • Use a special low-noise valve for a 15 to 30 dB reduction.
  • Combine the above methods where appropriate; a combination can be more cost-effective.

A note of caution: low-noise trims operating at high frequencies, typically between 5000 Hz and 20,000 Hz, can interfere with ultrasonic flow meters placed downstream of such valves. The high trim-produced frequencies can reduce the accuracy, or even cause failure, of such flow meters [2].
