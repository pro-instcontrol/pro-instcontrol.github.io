This chapter defines telemetry and surveys the ways measurement signals travel from a remote location to an observer: hydraulic and pneumatic signalling, copper cable, optical fibre, analog and pulse modulation, PCM, RF, and applications in power grids.

4.1 Introduction

Telemetry consists of two parts: tele and metering. Tele means distance transmission, that is, a remote location, and metering means measurement. In this way, telemetry can be defined as remote measurement techniques and transmission.

The measurements, which are remote, are of little importance unless they are transmitted to the observer for analysis located at some central place. This means there is a considerable gap between the remotely located measurement instruments and the centrally located analysis instruments. This gap can be as low as a few metres or as high as thousands of kilometres. This discussion points towards the existence of transmission methods as well, which is hidden inside the definition of telemetry. The methods of transmission shall be simpler for low distances and complex for higher distances.

While the measurement techniques happen to be the same, that is, different types of transducers, transmitters and so on, the suitable transmission methods applied need to be examined thoroughly.

Logically it seems that the concept of telemetry has evolved from telepathy or telegraphic messaging mechanism, which were in earlier use in post offices and other similar places. The method was very much popular and useful also as an information sending system for military purposes as well as domestic affairs. Later on process control also utililized these techniques for optimum performance of the process. This trend of adopting different communication techniques, e.g. satellite communication techniques for spacecraft control or military equipment controls for the purpose of process control improvement, are still being followed.

Thus, the scope of definition of telemetry can be modified to include the method of transmission of the measurement signal (also control signals or any other data) from one end to the other end of an instrumentation system. Transmission of status of process variables at the oilfields to the central control station, transmission of condition-monitoring signals from one end to the other on a long distance supply system of crude oil, and transmission of information signals containing status of fault conditions or changes in voltage level or frequency or load variations or insufficient supply for an electrical power transmission system through grid interconnections are some of the examples of most reliable process control telemetry system.

Methods adopted for signal transmission depend upon the type of measurement or control media selected. In general, we are concerned with three types of measurement or control media, e.g. pneumatic, hydraulic and electrical. Nowadays a fourth type of signalling medium has also come into existence, which is called optical type. In this system, the signal transmission part is only optical and therefore, for this system to work properly, the original signal has to be transformed into optical form. In the same way, after transmission, this optical signal has to be transformed back to the original form. The transmission of optical signal is carried out through optical fibre cables.

Pneumatic remote measurement

The pneumatic remote measurement is performed by pneumatically operated transducers or transmitters. The working of these transmitters is based upon the most popular flapper-nozzle system which has already been described in earlier discussion. The flapper is connected to the sensor part of the transmitter system. The movement due to the variation in process variable is taken up by the flapper. The movement of the flapper against the nozzle develops pneumatic output proportional to the sensor response. This pneumatic output signal, which is representative of primary variable, is to be transmitted to the centrally located control room where other instruments are also installed for carrying out further processing of the pneumatic signals.

Pneumatic signalling system corresponds to a standard dry air supply system of 1.4 kg/cm² and a generated measurement or control signal in the form of 0.2 to 1.0 kg/cm². All forms of pneumatic indicators, recorders, controllers, etc. are operated by this form of signal only. The pneumatic system have been very popular, in general, till 2000 A.D., and in some cases have been the only reliable and the time-proven instrumentation system. Nowadays this system has been replaced by electronic system, but is still in operation in some of the older process plants where the replacement would have proved uneconomical otherwise. Moreover, the final control elements which are conventional pneumatically operated control valves or dampers are still in operation and continue to be like that in years to come. Therefore, the requirement of pneumatic system, though comparatively less, shall continue.

Hydraulic signalling

The hydraulic signalling system is entirely a local phenomenon. For instance, the system of transmission for hydraulic control of turbine and other similar high-speed prime movers are related to short distance transmission. The short distance hydraulic transmission system is characterized by negligible pressure less, less maintenance involved in lines, easy visual monitoring by field operators, etc.

In the hydraulic system, the standard supply is the control oil pressure of 6 kg/cm² in general and the output signal corresponds to a range from 1.2 to 4.2 kg/cm² standard. This hydraulic output signal operates a number of hydraulic equipment connected to turbine control systems, e.g. governor, steam inlet valve, condensate outlet valve, quick closing trip valve, etc. Since the hydraulic system is not at all a long transmission system and involves less telemetry, the discussion shall not continue any more.

Electrical signalling

The last transmission system is the electrical type, which is the most important nowadays. Almost all measurement and control signalling are now is electrical. In this case, the standard uninterrupted instrumentation supply system are of three types: 110 volts D.C., 110 volts A.C. and 24 volts D.C.

Out of these three systems, 110 volts D.C. is invariably used for electro-mechanical relay based interlocking circuit which also are being phased out slowly and are being replaced by logic gate interlocking system or programmable logic control (PLC) system (these shall be described in appropriate chapter). The 110 volt A.C. supply is employed to all the controllers, recorders, indicators or other receiver instruments, which are located inside the central control room. The 24 volts D.C. supply is supplied to all the remote transmitters located in the outdoor field of a process plant. As has been described earlier, the low voltage supply system is used to minimize safety related hazards due to the presence of explosive mixture or gases in the field. Low voltage makes the transmitter an intrinsically safe system.

An intrinsically safe system is that system which has insufficient amount of energy available to ignite an explosive mixture or gases in the field. Output of these field mounted transmitters which is in the form of 4 to 20 mAmps has to be transmitted to the central control room for further processing.

In pneumatic telemetry system, pneumatic signal output of all the transmitters located in one particular area are brought to a junction box by means of individual copper or AISI 304 stainless steel tubing of normally 6 mm O.D. Metal tubes are used in place of PVC tube to avoid damage of PVC tube due to mechanical causes.

Figure 4.1 shows the individual field signal tubes terminated inside the pneumatic junction box.

The termination of these pneumatic signal tubings into the junction box (made of aluminium casting) are done by means of compression fittings of appropriate sizes (made of brass or AISI 304 stainless steel). Now from the field junction box, a multicored, PVC pneumatic tube bunch is run underground all the way up-to the central control room. This multicored tube bundle is PVC sheathed, armoured and finally PVC tape wound from the outside to protect against mechanical and other damages. This tube bundle can have 7-core, 14-core or 19-core (this means there are 7, 14 or 19 independent tubes respectively which have been put together to make the complete bundle). Therefore, a 19-cored tube bundle is capable of handling 19 different pneumatic signals between the central control room and the field.

In the control room end, the multicored tube bundle carrying all different signals is terminated at common tube Terminal Board (TB) located near the back of the control panel as shown in Figure 4.2. This termination is again accomplished by means of compression fittings of same size and type that have been used on the junction box. From the TB, these signal-carrying connections are brought to individual instrument (back side) by means of copper tubings and brass fittings.

Pneumatic telemetry is limited to within a particular industrial establishment i.e. between a plant field area and its central control room (within one kilometre maximum). But for a telemetry system to cover long distance beyond one kilometre, signal transmission in electrical form is advisable. As far as optimization is concerned, electrical signalling is economical even in the cases where pneumatic signalling has been selected. Selection of pneumatic signalling is primarily due to the presence of pneumatic instrumentation system and therefore, where pneumatic instrumentation systems have been replaced by electronic systems, all signalling systems are of electrical type only.

Telemetry for electrical signalling in broad sense are of two types: Cable telemetry and R.F telemetry. These two altogether can be of analog as well as digital varieties.

4.2 Cable Telemetry (Landline System)

As we can understand the simplest form of telemetry in the electrical signalling is of direct cable transmission type. In this system different types of signal cable or control cable are used. The cables used are of the following types:

  1. Multicore copper conductor ( ≤ 1.5 mm² cross-section)
  2. Twisted pair signal cable
  3. Coaxial cable
  4. Fibre optic cable

(i) Multicore copper conductor

Multicore copper cable can have a number of cores starting from 2 to 19 or even more put together in systematic manner. When one variable signalling is concerned, two-core cable is sufficient and for more than one variable, more pairs become essential. Ordinarily the cable is PVC coated having smaller value insulation because the signal voltage level is very low. Sometimes it can be armoured as well for mechanical reasons only. Armouring is required when the routing of cable is through damaging areas inside the plant. Cables used for underground routing are required to have thick outer PVC coating for longer life in addition to armouring.

Selection of a particular type of cable telemetry depends upon the type of signal selected as well. Basically, the signals for transmission can be of A.C. as well as D.C. type in either voltage form or current form. Design considerations change accordingly whereas transducers/transmitters are located in the plant field (in contact with the real process), the receiver instruments are located inside a central control room, which happens to be invariably centrally air-conditioned. The transmiter output in the form of electrical signal is transmitted to the control room, which can be in the range of a few hundreds of metres away to even a few hundreds of kilometres away. For such distances, transmission cannot be done on low level A.C. base because low-level A.C. signals are almost always affected by stray magnetic fields. In case of low-level D.C., the interfering noise signal can be removed by suitable filters. The high-level D.C. signal is still the better choice.

In A.C. signalling system, phase shift is a common problem. Compensation for phase shift in instrumentation system requires complicated circuitry making the entire system altogether more expensive whereas in D.C. signalling system, there is no such consideration at all.

In D.C. signalling system, D.C. amplification is quite complicated and not so satisfactory result is obtained. A.C. signal amplification, on the other hand, is simple and quite satisfactory result is obtained.

The signals, which are used for operation of electro-pneumatic valve positioner in the process control loops, the signals are required to be converted into force, which can be done by directly passing a D.C. through a coil suspended in a magnetic field. In case of A.C. signal system, the A.C. has to be rectified first.

In process industries, almost all locations of instrument installations are hazardous. Therefore, low energy level D.C. signals are selected for use in such environments. Selection of this signal minimizes the stored energy in the explosive atmosphere and thus making the system intrinsically safe. An intrinsically safe system is that system where the energy level for igniting the explosive mixture is very less than is required to initiate the explosions. Hence, the possibility of explosion is completely removed.

But since the field instruments are connected to control room through copper cable and the normal power supply system (110 volt D.C. or 110 volt A.C.) is of higher order, an accidental connection of these supply systems to field instruments can send the higher voltage to the field instruments and thus triggering explosion all of a sudden. Such accidental situation is successfully prevented by putting electronic safety barriers in between the field and the control room wiring. In this arrangement, all the cables going to cater the field instruments from the control room are made to pass through individual barrier. In the same way, all the signal carrying cables coming from the field side are made to pass through barrier before entering the control room instruments.

The function of these barriers are to isolate one system from the other by physical disconnection so that no higher voltages can go to field instruments during any eventuality.

An electronic safety barrier called zener barrier also sometimes has been shown in Figure 4.3. This is a passive device and allows the signal to pass from one end to the other without any loss of signal strength. The barrier consist of three resistances and two zener diodes connected as shown in the diagram. The resistances limit the current and the zeners limit the voltage. Use of only one zener is sufficient for operation of the barrier but one more zener is used for obtaining 100% redundancy, i.e. the accidental failure of one can be safeguarded by the other one. The use of a fuse of accurate specification (to be calculated appropriately) makes the barrier economical. The current capacity of the fuse is lower than barrier so that before zener is damaged and becomes out of service the fuse should blow. By making the arrangement like this, the life of zener barrier is also increased and frequent replacement is avoided. A fuse (glass fuse) is much cheaper than a barrier.

Further in cable telemetry system, as far as choice of voltage or current are concerned, a voltage telemetry system needs more special quality circuit than do a current telemetry system. The requirement of signal-to-noise ratio is stringent. Since the power level of a voltage system in almost all the cases are low, telemetry circuit is affected by interfering noise which are of normally same magnitude as the signal.

The current telemetry systems, on the other hand, are capable of developing higher level voltages than do the voltage system. Due to this characteristic, there is no effect of thermal or inductive or other type of noise voltage on the signal. However, the leakage current, inherent to such system can introduce some error. The current signal telemetry systems are more expensive than voltage signal telemetry system because of high-level amplifier requirement. However, a cheap receiver instrument can be used with current signal telemetry system. The receiver instruments in this case can be connected in series without any interaction amongst them. This is an additional advantage of a current telemetry system. However, in case of voltage telemetry system, parallel connections of receiver instruments necessitate consideration of source impe-dance as well.

Proper functioning of electronic controllers depend upon proper operation of operational amplifiers which require a signal in voltage form. This voltage is developed from the current signal by passing the current signal across a precision resistor of 62.5 ohms. A 4 to 20 mAmps. D.C. signal develops voltage in the range of 0.25 volt to 1.25 volt, which is applied to the electronic controllers.

For operation of electro-pneumatic valve positioner, as discussed earlier, signal in current form is most suitable because it develops the required force when simply passed through a coil placed in a magnetic field. For operation with signal in voltage form, the coil resistance needs to be kept constant however, for this a temperature compensation arrangement is needed.

If at all the resistance of the cable conductor during signal transmission varies, voltage signal also gets affected. On the other hand, if the signal is available in current form there is absolutely no effect.

Modern telemetry systems handle low-level signals and possess high input impedance. In such situation signal transfer becomes more sensitive to outside interferences i.e. noise.

If the signal cable is passing through an area where varying magnetic field (due to power cable or high voltage transformer) is present, an induced voltage can be superimposed upon the original signal. This is called electromagnetic noise. A similar effect is expected due to the potential difference created through the coupling capacitor. In this case also an induced voltage of 50 Hz is superimposed on the signal and is called electrostatic noise. Another form of electrostatic noise is generated due to the presence of multicore cable wherein an alternating current in one pair induces noise voltage on adjacent pair because of capacitive coupling. This form of noise is also known as cross talk. These three forms of noise signals together called normal mode noise, this noise voltage adds to the original signal and thus contributes significant error. Such noise voltage can go upto 10 millivolt and therefore, suitable arrangement is to be made for removal of this. Filters are used for this purpose. Careful selection of filter is necessary because while rejecting noise voltage, filters have negative effect also in the form of reduced response level. Thus, a compromise is called for.

Another typical noise popularly known as common mode noise is produced, when one terminal of a signal system is grounded and the other one is coupled to the measurement circuit capacitively. An instrumentation loop grounded at two points having a potential difference also is capable of developing common mode noise. This form of noise can reach upto over 5 volt (at 50 Hz) or so. Receiver instruments having differential input arrangement eliminates this type of noise. When millivolt signals from thermocouples are sent through extension wires having different impedances, the common mode noise is converted to normal mode. This is, however, eliminated easily by grounding the thermocouples.

Majority of the noise problem in a cable telemetry system is due to poor cable design. A twisted pair signal cable (to be discussed next) is an excellent choice for high degree of noise rejection automatically. Another important point is that A.C. power and D.C. signal cable should not pass through the same route. Separate route for these two different cables shall avoid generation of noise to the maximum extent possible.

Since inductance and capacitance are energy storing characteristics of a cable, their actual values constitute important consideration in cable design decision for telemetry purposes. The values of inductance and capacitance increase as the length of the cable increases. At the same time resistance of the cable also increases in the same way and therefore, can compensate to some extent the effect of energy storing behaviour of the inductance. In similar way, the insulation resistance between two conductors decrease as length is increased and thus controls the energy storing behaviour of the capacitance. A normally designed cable can have 0.1 microfarad of capacitance per kilometre of cable length. Similary a normally designed coaxial cable (to be discussed later) can have about 50 picofarad of capacitance per kilometre of cable length. Inductance, on the other hand, can vary in between 0.6 and 1.4 millihenry per kilometre length for ordinary control cable. For screened cable, i.e. coaxial cable the value can be anything between 0.17 and 0.61 millihenry per kilometre of cable length.

In cable telemetry for instrumentation signal, the size of individual strand in a multistrand construction is also more or less, fixed and that comes to be approximately 0.2 millimetre (in diameter). The fusing current rating of such a selected cable is not more that 3.5 ampere.

(ii) Twisted pair signal cable

When two independently insulated copper wires are twisted together in the shape of a helix, it is known as a twisted pair. When many pairs of such cable are bunched together, a protective sheath is used for putting them together for the ease of routing. Twisting prevented from interfering them with each other and signals thus stay noiseless. Both (analog as well as digital) types of transmission is possible with twisted pair. The bandwidth, however, shall depend upon cross-section of the wire and the distance to be transmitted. Bandwidth can be defined as range of frequencies (carrying signal) which can pass comfortably without loss through a channel (in this case, the channel is control cable).

Twisted pair signal cable can be of two varieties, in general, i.e. Unshielded Twisted Pair (UTP) and Shielded Twisting Pair (STP). Use of UTP cable is restricted to areas where there is no possibility of electromagnetic or other interferences at all such as office environment and other similar locations. The STP cable, on the other hand, is the most suitable one for the industrial enviroment where the outside interferences are the deciding factor for selection. Figures 4.4 and 4.5 show these two cables respectively.

Provision of shielding arrangement in STP and their connection arrangement through shielded-style LAN (Local Area Network) connector make the STP more expensive than the UTP.

(iii) Coaxial cable

While the twisted pair provides sufficient shielding from outside interference due to noise, the coaxial cable possesses high quality of shielding property. The extremely low level signals (television signal, computer signal, pH or other quality measurement signals), which are very much susceptible to even smaller intensity of noise signal, use coaxial cable almost invariably for signal transmission. A diagram showing complete structure of coaxial cable has been shown in Figure 4.6: a single copper conductor, Teflon insulation, braiding made of copper mesh and a thick PVC cover.

The constructional feature explains that this variety of cable can be used for higher bandwidth and can achieve high signal-to-noise ratio factor. There are altogether four different varieties of coaxial cable:

  1. 50 ohms cable (Ethernet cable)
  2. 75 ohms cable
  3. 93 ohms cable
  4. Twin axial cable

The 50 ohms coaxial cables (called Ethernet cable) also are used for real-time networks. In one design of 50 ohms cable, thick wire with double shield are provided. These are very successfully used in process industries instrumentation system. In another design where a thin wire with single shield is selected is susceptible to noise interference but is easier to work upon for termination and others. The thick wire cable on the other hand is not easy to work upon but provides excellent noise rejection capability.

75 ohms coaxial cables are normally not in use for instrumentation system. These are used almost invariably for CATV (Cable Television) network. The fact is that the 75 ohms cable provides degradation in signal which goes normally, unnoticed by human eyes (in case of video signal). But degradation in instrumentation signal can bring measurable error and failure of process control.

The 93 ohms coaxial cable is a special class cable and is used in proprietary networks communication for IBM mainframe computers and also in ARC net networks.

The twin-axial cable is also a special cable, which has two copper conductors with overall shielding as shown in Figure 4.7. In addition to its use in proprietary network applications, these are used in programmable logic controller (PLC) for connecting remote I/O module (input/output module). The speeds, which are typically encountered with remote I/O modules are excellently matched with the performance of the twin-axial cable. Performance in noise rejection is also superior.

Whenever there is a requirement of joining two coaxial cables, both the components of the cable have to be joined together separately. The copper conductor is to be joined to the other copper conductor and the braiding (made of copper mesh) has to be joined to each other by twisting properly. Finally, the combination should be wrapped separately by domestic insulation tapes.

(iv) Fibre optic cable

Fibre optic cable constitutes the intermediate part, i.e. the medium for transmission of an entire optical transmission system. This is consisting of, in general, a light-generating source for signal, the medium and finally a suitable detection system. The basic principles behind the fibre optic system lie in the property of total internal reflection described in optical physics. The ray of light incident at (and/or above) critical angle gets totally reflected back and is trapped inside the fibre enclosure. In this way the trapped light can be propagated uninterruptedly for many miles altogether with minimum loss. Figure 4.8 indicates one such ray of light trapped inside the fibre enclosure.

If the dimension of the fibre enclosure is reduced and made equivalent to the wavelength (in microns) of light, the fibre enclosure works like a waveguide through which the ray of light propagates in perfect straight line without any bounce. This arrangement is known as single-mode fibre. If, on the other hand, the core of the fibre enclosure is increased, more than one ray of light bounces around at different angles (up to the maximum angle of total internal reflection), a multimode fibre arrangement results. Obviously the single-mode fibre shall be more expensive in effect than the multimode. Single-mode fibre is applicable to higher speed and longer distance transmission system whereas a multimode fibre is applied to medium range of speed and distance. Transmission upto 100 kilometre can be realized without the need for amplification.

A single fibre has been shown in Figure 4.9. The entire design refinement is centred at manufacturing of glass core through which the ray of light shall propagate. The diameter of the core and cladding (expressed as core/clad) determines the size of the optical fibre.

In general, three different dimensions are in practical use. They are type 9/125 type 62.5/125, and type 200/230. The first digit indicates the size of core and the other one shows size of the cladding in micron units (1 micron = 10⁻⁶ metres). Type 9/125 where core is 9 micron and cladding is 125 micron are used for telephony applications. The next one, i.e. 62.5/125 type are used for enterprise data transmission system. The large-core fibre, i.e. type 200/230 are used for process control and instrumentation system. These three types have been shown in Figure 4.10.

The core of an optical fibre is generally considered to be homogeneous but at the junction of the core and the cladding, the theory of homogeneity ceases to exist. The refractive index thus gets changed at the boundary of the two. This is called step index fibre. The speciality of this type of fibre is that it does not get affected by either constant refractive index or step change in it at the junction. But with multimode fibre there is a difference between the ray at the critical angle and the ray along the axis. Even then the speed of light remains constant, the length of the path of these two rays not being the same, the time to reach the other end does not remain same. This phenomenon is called broadening of pulse or modal dispersion. The dispersion can be minimized by arranging the core of the fibre of highest refractive index along the axis and decreasing the refractive index gradually upto the boundary. This way a gradation of refractive index is created and this type of fibre is known as graded index fibre. These have been shown in Figures 4.11 and 4.12.

Handling of fibre glass is an important factor in whole of the affairs. Risk of breakage is due to four reasons:

  1. Tensile strain
  2. Heat
  3. Water/moisture
  4. Time factor

Proper manufacturing process minimizes the tensile strain, which ultimately develops cracks. Plastic coating on the fibre creates an artificial barrier for water or humidity. The cladding also provides same protection. Cladding is generally made of hard fluoro-polymer which is an excellent water repellent. In fact, glass is not easily broken because its breaking strength is nearly five times more than the steel but the glass becomes weak due to microscopic defect found on the glass surface by nature. These microscopic defects develop cracks rapidly in the presence of heat and moisture. Therefore, the strength of the fibre is an important factor and great care has to be taken for installation and other handling activities.

As far as connection of the two fibre glasses is concerned, great care is observed during the process. Two fibres are connected in many ways. In one way of connection, the two fibres can be terminated in a special connector and can be plugged securely to an especially designed fibre socket. In this arrangement, nearly 20% of the transmitted light signal is lost but is very easy to handle. In another arrangement, the two pieces are put in a special sleeve facing each other and clamped properly. In this particular arrangement, provision for alignment checking is thereby sending a ray of light and adjusting the position slightly this way or that way. With this connection also almost 10% of the light signal is lost. In the third arrangement, the two pieces can be fused by melting under controlled condition and a one-piece fibre can be obtained. This is the best possible method of connection, which has minimum loss of light.

Discussions till now indicate that it is very difficult and expensive to handle, install, connect and maintain an optical fibre system. Even then the optical fibre system is gaining popularity in the world of telemetry and other signal transmission environments. The reason lies in its tremendous bandwidth availability. Bandwidth indicates signal-carrying capacity in an optical fibre system or in any channel. Requirement of bandwidth for voice is 3 kilohertz (KHz) and for television is 6 megahertz (MHz), but the available bandwidth with an optical fibre system is in gigahertz (GHz) which is much higher than the above two practical requirements.

Chromatic dispersion is the variation in the velocity of light over the entire range of wavelength produced by the light source. It is the only limitation on bandwidth in an optical fibre system; during manufacturing the fibre is developed in such a way that minimum chromatic dispersion should result while in use.

Single-mode fibre has more bandwidth than the step index multimode fibre. Graded index multimode fibre has the bandwidth in between these two. For transmission distance upto 10 kilometer and 10 Mbps (Megabits per second) speed, step index multimode fibre is used. For 500 MHz-km (product of bandwidth and distance) graded index multimode fibre is used. And finally for 10 GHz-km single-mode fibre is used.

Another consideration in this regard is the overall structure of the optical fibre. The cable is the overall structure placed on top of the fibre glass in order to provide physical protection to the delicate fibre. Structural provision is designed properly to meet the specific need of the fibre. Since the fibre is not at all ductile like copper, any undue stretching can damage the fibre completely. Important requirements of a perfect fibre optic cable are:

  1. Resistance to crushing effect
  2. Resistance to all forms of tensile forces
  3. Minimum expansion due to heat
  4. Sustenance to bending during installation
  5. Minimum microbending loss (Small amount of light can be lost due to natural microbending which goes normally unnoticed physically, resulting in loss in the main signal itself)

Figure 4.13 shows the cross-section of a complete fibre optic cable that has been used in one of the fertilizer plants control as well as networking systems.

The fibre core and the cladding serve the usual purpose. Next layer is the protection tube, which in fact protects the cladding from damage. Kelvar rod has been provided next to the protection tube for mechanical strength making the handling safer and easier. The Kelvar rod bunch is covered with resin and finally two-layer jacketing (one of Kelvar and the other one of PVC or Teflon) is done to protect the interior parts of the assembly from physical as well as environmental abuses.

Figure 4.14 shows an entire telemetry system of opto-electric type.

The advent of newly designed opto-electric telemetry system has brought about new wiring techniques as well. This is known as composite cabling system. This cabling system contains UTP/STP wiring, co-axial cabling and also fibre cables all put together in a unified jacket. The connections are made as per the requirement for specific application at appropriate place. This cable is selected when the transmission system is complicated and many forms of signals are encountered with. If the system does not call for, use of composite cable is uneconomical. Normally these cables are custom-built and vendor-supplied for turn-key projects in process instrumentation system and other networking systems.

4.3 Analog Telemetry

Analog telemetry can be considered a part of both cable telemetry and RF telemetry, but a separate discussion, though brief, is needed because an AC transmission system encounters many errors for which excellent electronic solutions are available.

This is very unfortunate that analog transmission through land lines as well as through RF signalling suffers loss of signal strength due to the natural characteristic of attenuation along the path, delay distortion of the received signal and poor signal-to-noise ratio due to interference. The worst part of the problem is that these effects are largely frequency dependent. The analysis can be studied easily through the well-known spectrum of Fourier components.

The problem of loss of signal can be dealt with by applying modulation techniques. Modulation in fact modifies the analog signal completely while leaving the quality of the signal intact, so that it can be converted into its original form by electronic means at the receiving end. The introduction of a standard sinusoidal waveform called the carrier wave does the job. The function of the carrier wave can be compared with the function of a passenger railway train. A passenger train runs from one destination to the other carrying a number of persons and luggage. Here, the persons and the luggage do not actually travel; it is the passenger train that travels all along. When the persons and the luggage are put on the train, they also reach the destination. In modulation techniques similarly, the signal is put on the carrier wave and at the receiving end the signal is stripped off the carrier, which is known as demodulation (the opposite of modulation). In this way the receiving end gets the signal in nearly original form.

Since there are three variables in a carrier sinusoidal waveform, e.g., amplitude, frequency and phase, modulation techniques can be classified as amplitude modulation (AM), frequency modulation (FM) and phase modulation (PM). Modulation is in fact a vast topic, which requires a detailed description for its complete treatment. But here the description shall be restricted to the basic principles applicable to land-line and RF telemetry only.

Amplitude modulation (AM)

In the amplitude modulation technique, the amplitude of the carrier waveform is modified with respect to the actual instantaneous value of the signal to be transmitted. Let us suppose the carrier waveform at frequency f₁ to be sinusoidal (the most usual case), as shown in Figure 4.15, and the wave-shape of the analog signal of frequency f₂, which is also usually in sinusoidal form, as shown in Figure 4.16. When the amplitude of the carrier is added to the signal wave, the resulting waveshape of the amplitude modulated signal is shown in Figure 4.17.

As is seen, the resulting waveform has another two effective frequency patterns in addition to the original frequency f₁ of the carrier waveform. The combined frequencies consisting of (f₁ + f₂) and (f₁ − f₂) make the overall frequency band of the signal 2f₂. These are called sidebands.

The quality of amplitude modulation is affected by high cable impedance and the stray field interferences and therefore may not be advisable to be used for landline telemetry of substantial distances.

Frequency modulation (FM)

In case of frequency modulation, the modulation is affected by frequency change only, the amplitude and phase necessarily remaining the same. Here also it is assumed that the analog signal wave is of sinusoidal type. When frequency is modulated, the resulting modulated waveshape is seen in Figure 4.20 along with the carrier waveform (Figure 4.18) and the signal waveform (Figure 4.19).

The magnitude of variation in frequency is determined by the amplitude of the carrier wave whereas rate of variation is found by frequency of the carrier wave. Unlike AM technique, in case of FM, a number of sidebands are formed, but their intensity goes on decreasing for one sideband to the other. This decrease is quite effective after a few sidebands itself and converge also very rapidly.

Also the frequency modulation technique is bandwidth selective because some of the range of bandwidth creates distortion in the output signal. The range of bandwidth BW is given by the expression:

(4.1)BW = 2(dₘ + f₀)

where dₘ is the maximum deviation of the carrier with respect to output signal frequency and f₀ is the maximum frequency of the output signal.

Phase modulation (PM)

Lastly the phase modulation is effected by changing the phase angle of the carrier wave with respect to the instantaneous value of the carrier wave. The shift in phase is characterized by the value of amplitude of the carrier wave only. The frequency of the carrier wave does not play any role in phase modulation. The PM technique is not at all popular in telemetry work and therefore, is of academic interest only. Figures 4.21, 4.22 and 4.23 show the carrier, the signal and the modulated waveshape respectively.

4.4 Pulse Telemetry

Till now we have considered the signal waveform to be of the same type as the carrier wave, i.e. sinusoidal in form. The treatment will be entirely different if we consider the signal waveform to consist of a train of pulses (of rectangular shape). The modulation technique will also change accordingly and is described below.

Pulse Amplitude Modulation (PAM)

In this modulation technique, the trains of pulses (the actual signal waveform) have constant frequency. The carrier waveform is modulated in such a way that the heights, i.e. the amplitude of the resultant wave, carry the actual signal value, as shown in Figure 4.24.

Pulse Duration Modulation (PDM)

In this variation of the modulation technique, the amplitude of the pulse is kept constant and a change in the duration of a pulse signifies the value of the signal wave. This is also known as pulse width modulation (PWM). The width or duration of the pulse represents the value of the signal strength, as shown in Figure 4.25.

t₁, t₂ and t₃ are durations; w₁, w₂ and w₃ are widths.

The technique of PDM or PWM can be modified by replacing the existing pulse with a pulse of short duration, as shown in Figure 4.26.

This is called pulse position modulation (PPM). Its advantage is that PPM uses less power with respect to PDM or PWM, but the disadvantage is that it needs a larger bandwidth for transmission.

4.5 Digital Telemetry

In this form of telemetry, a digital signal consisting of a combination of 0s and 1s is transmitted via a cable link or by other means. Here too the well-established modulation techniques are applied to the signal to be transmitted, which is now available in digital form. The basic techniques are similar to those described for analog systems. All three modulation techniques have been applied and are shown in Figures 4.27, 4.28 and 4.29 for three different digital signals, 01100101, 01101001 and 1010011010 respectively. The carrier wave here is also the sinusoidal wave itself, and accordingly the modulated digital signals have been shown in the same figures.

The bit strings in Figures 4.27, 4.28 and 4.29 are 01100101, 01101001 and 1010011010 respectively.

The most popular modulation technique for the digital form of telemetry, however, is that of pulse code modulation (PCM). It is very easy to obtain a PCM wave: it is produced simply by quantizing the pulse amplitude modulated wave. The PCM system selects, as usual, the nearest approximation with respect to a set of discrete numbers, i.e. binary numbers. For instance, if an analog signal system assumes values between 0 and 5 volts (a standard value used in most equivalent systems) and the instantaneous value is 4.4 volts, then its digital value is 0100 over a 4-digit level of representation. The error here comes to 0.4 volt and is called the quantization error. If the number of digits is increased at the digital code level, more details can be obtained from the PCM. The representation level is given by R_l = 2^N, where N is the number of digits in the PCM signal.

In the PCM technique, the bandwidth of the actual signal directs the selection of bandwidth of the PCM. Nyquist theorem defines the way sampling is decided with the PCM system. The sampling rate is optimum when it is twice the bandwidth of the original signal wave. The bandwidth of a particular channel is given by:

(4.2)BW = Nf₀

where f₀ is the maximum frequency of the output signal.

Rate of transmission Trp through PCM can be expressed in terms of the signal-to-noise ratio and is given by:

(4.3)Trp = f_b log₂[1 + P₁/P₂]

or

(4.4)Trp = 2f_b log₂[1 + V₁/V₂]

where P₁ and V₁ are signal strengths in the form of power and voltage and similarly P₂ and V₂ are the noise strength in terms of power and voltage.

4.6 RF Telemetry

RF telemetry or radio frequency telemetry is a kind of wireless telemetry, which uses radio frequency waves for its transmission. Frequency range for transmission is allocated as per the standards selected for the purpose by the appropriate regulating authority.

The basic characteristic of a radio wave at low frequency is that it penetrates through obstacles very easily and travels through very long distances uninterruptedly with equal intensity. But at high frequency it becomes directional and therefore, gets reflected back wherever it strikes an obstacle. The range of frequency for practical use is to be selected accordingly otherwise repeaters have to be used.

All forms of modulation techniques described in earlier discussion shall be applied to the RF telemetry as well. However, it should be understood clearly that the RF telemetry is applied only in the cases where it is known that the use of land line telemetry is going to be difficult because of unfavorable geographical conditions of the route, e.g. ocean, desert, vast jungle, large plateau, etc.

Nowadays, the latest advancement in low power sensor technology has created tiny sensors, which are called sensor nodes. Several such nodes when connected together systematically, a sensor network is formed, which is capable of measuring many independent variables in a closed or open environment that will completely define the system prevailing in that particular environment. These measurement systems comprises of other sensor network elements as well and are capable of communicating with the central control station through RF telemetry. In this system, the communication satellite positioned at geostationary orbit also takes part in the communication. This system can be used for military purposes to monitor the presence of troops, lethal equipment, ammunition and other emergency services in difficult locations, also for environmental findings, e.g. tracking of rare animals and birds, forest fire and sudden flood alarming situation, soil and atmospheric pollutants research work and also for human physiological study. This system is slowly becoming so popular that more and more difficult situations are being monitored so easily through this system. Even if some of the sensor nodes have become defective or have gone otherwise out of order, the overall systems do not get affected and the required measurement signals continue to reach the central control station for analysis.

4.7 Telemetry in Electrical Power System

Electrical power system network comprise generating units (GU) and in-house step-up substation (SUS), high voltage transmission system (HVTS), grid stations (GS) and lastly utility substation consisting of distribution step-down system (SDS) as shown in the block diagram (Figure 4.30).

Grid network is the backbone of electrical power transmission and supply system of any country. Generating units are interconnected to one another through grid system interconnect. In this way any interruption in one of the generating unit in any part of the country can be well taken care of by another running unit having surplus power through existing grid network. Even if there is no surplus power readily available, it can be generated by any hot standby generating unit if instructed properly. For carrying such typical function a carefully designed and thoroughly planned instrumentation system is required. Telemetry in power system connects one instrumentation system of the entire network to the other for effective control of generation, transmission and distribution.

It is fortunate that sudden load changes, any fault condition in the transmission and distribution network or even problem in generating units can be reflected immediately in terms of changes in voltage and frequency. This is a typical characteristic of electrical power system. However Telemetry is also used , for finding the actual fault and the location of fault and for implementing the required automatic corrective action. These monitorings can be done manually also by suitable telephone message passing from one station to the other or to the generating unit directly. The required telephone lines can be leased lines sometimes called hot lines. By telephone messaging through hot lines monitoring and load sharing can be controlled properly and immediately.

Whether the correction is implemented automatically or manually all the methods of transmission systems described in this chapter or earlier are utilized. Here the measurement signals are essentially voltage, current and frequency. Sudden drop or rise in voltage due to downstream changes or decrease or heavy rush of current or changes in frequency are measured by suitable instruments through current and potential transformers installed for the purpose. These measurement signals become initiating signals for manual as well as automatic control. These signals are transmitted to the control station from where the necessary correction signal can be initiated manually or automatically.

Most of these types of controls applicable for power system networks are in fact manual. There is a specific reason for selecting manual control. The manual control does not interfere with the generating unit control. Therefore, the transmission system control through substation instrumentation is kept under one boundary whereas the control instrumentation of generating unit (consisting of boiler control and turbine control) is kept under separate boundary. Interaction between these two boundaries is purely manual. The solution of control problem (arising out of merging the automatic control of two boundaries) are the subject of many papers worldwide. But these have never been tried to in practice, at least in India.

Examples

Example 4.1

In an optical fibre transmission system the input optical power is −12 dBm and the output power is −18 dBm. If the fibre length is 600 metres, find the attenuation?

Solution

Attenuation per kilometre length

(18 − 12)/(600/1000) = 6/0.6 = 10 dB/km. Ans.

Example 4.2

If the signal transmission frequency for an optical signal is 10 Megahertz and distance to be transmitted is 600 metres only, what is the minimum bandwidth required for the fibre?

Solution

The required bandwidth,

= 10 × 600/1000 = 6 MHz-km.

Example 4.3

Through pulse code modulation (PCM) technique, a 7-digit level code has been transmitted. If the signal bandwidth is 2 KHz, find the minimum carrier channel bandwidth required?

Solution

Bandwidth requirement for such a situation is given by:

BW = N f₀
= 7 × 2 KHz = 14 KHz. Ans.

Example 4.4

For PCM technique, find the quantization level for an 8-digit encoder. Find also the range of the encoder if each level represents 1 volt. If the actual signal is of the value of 134.2 volts, find quantization error in modulation.

Solution

Quantization level = N⁸ = 2⁸ = 256
Range of the encoder = (N⁸ − 1) × value of each level
= (2⁸ − 1) × 1 = 255 volt.

The value of voltage in binary form shall be,

01000110 = 134

Hence, the error encountered

= (134.2 − 134)/134.2 × 100 = 0.15% (app.) Ans.
