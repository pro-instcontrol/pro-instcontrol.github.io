This chapter shows how measurement signals are conditioned before digitisation: power packs, amplification and filtering, multiplexing, sample-and-hold, ADC and DAC conversion, digital-meter resolution and accuracy, aperture time, PC-based acquisition, industrial communication standards, plus eleven worked examples and an 18-question exercise.

5.1 Introduction

A data acquisition system collects field data available directly from the measurement transducers in analogue form, that is, in current, voltage, resistance, capacitance, frequency, and so on. Since the standard representation signal for all the process variables is electrical, at the level of 4 to 20 mA D.C., the inputs available from the transducers have to pass through a signal conditioning unit. The signal conditioning unit transforms these input signals into the standard form by linearization, filtering, etc. (non-linear operations) and by amplification, algebraic operations, conversion, etc. (linear operations). Since data acquisition is essentially a computer-based operation, the input signals available in standard analogue form are transformed into digital form to make them compatible with the computer. These data are stored by the computer for further analysis as and when required.

The system under discussion is essentially restricted to acquisition only. This means there is no further action to control the process variables from the computer peripherals. But nowadays system design has improved to include the supervisory jobs also to be initiated by the computer. The new system is named SCADA (supervisory control and data acquisition). However, we shall restrict our description up to data acquisition only. Figure 5.1 shows the components of a data acquisition system in assembled block form.

Before we go into the individual detailing of the different components of the existing data acquisition system, we must examine the system available earlier for data acquisition. In fact, the system available earlier was called a data logger. The data logger unit incorporated electrical transducer signals in standard form, thermocouple signals and pneumatic transducer signals in the form of 0.2 to 1.0 kg/cm². Since the majority of the field transducers were of the pneumatic type, the pneumatic signals received thus had to be transformed into the standard form, that is, into 4 to 20 mA D.C. For this, these pneumatic signals in their original form were passed through a mechanical scanner (called a scani valve) moving in circular motion in steps at a fixed interval as per the design. All the inputs available in pneumatic form, after passing through the scani valve one by one, were first converted into electrical signals (4 to 20 mA D.C.) by means of a pneumatic-to-electrical transducer. The signal was then converted into digital form and logged into the processor memory. In this system, the fixed interval for the step movement of the scani valve was calculated by taking into account the time of selection of one particular pneumatic signal, its conversion to an equivalent electrical form and then its conversion into digital form. Unless the entire operation described above was completed, the next step movement of the scani valve was restricted. In a particular event, if a pneumatic signal was not available owing to a fault in a field-mounted transmitter, the scani valve moved to the next step, initiated by a timer circuit. Other inputs such as normal electrical signals, thermocouple signals, RTD signals and analyzer signals all came to the multiplexing unit after proper signal conditioning and were similarly logged into the processor memory.

Though it seems a rather typical design and difficult to operate and maintain, this equipment was very popular in process industries up to 1980. Now, when electronic transmitters came into the picture and were installed in place of pneumatic ones, the scani-valve design became obsolete and the new system became easier to be maintained. A data logging system employing electronic transmitters (Tx) is presented in the block diagram Figure 5.2. In this scheme, the transmitters installed in the field incorporate the signal conditioning unit as an integral part and the analog signals received by the multiplexing unit are all in standard form. These are then converted into digital form by an A/D converter and then processed by the computer for auxiliary storage, for the generation of various alarm conditions and also for obtaining hard copies of different records.

The efficient operation of any process industry or any manufacturing establishment involves a number of process variables affecting the process unit one way or the other. This is because the process units need a number of control loops to be implemented carefully so that the direct interaction amongst variables can be minimized. This type of interaction amongst different variables ultimately affects the loop stability and sometimes some of the control loops can go completely out of control.

In a modern system, which incorporates a data acquisition system along with various independent control loops, interaction is minimized and the overall performance of the operation is improved. This is achieved by the use of high-speed digital computers. Nowadays almost all data acquisition systems are PC based.

One more important point in the process industry is the presence of a hazardous atmosphere. During abnormal conditions the area inside the process plants becomes hazardous owing to leakage of explosive gases or mixtures. For such an area, installation practices dictate provision of intrinsically safe interfacing. This necessitates the use of electronic safety barriers as well between the hazardous area and the safe area. Physically, these barriers can be installed between the transducer terminals, located inside the cabinet installed in the field near the process, and the signal conditioning unit cabinet located inside the control room. If the signal conditioning units are integral to the transducer cabinets, then the barriers should be installed near the multiplexing unit. In all the cases the location of the barriers shall be inside the control room, that is, inside the safe area.

Based on the facts discussed so far, the data acquisition system includes essentially the following:

  • (a) Transducers
  • (b) Signal conditioners
  • (c) Multiplexers
  • (d) Data conversion (ADC and DAC)
  • (e) Local or remote display devices, storage, alarm logging, etc.

Since the description of transducers has been presented in an earlier chapter, we shall not discuss these any further. We shall start our description from signal conditioning units.

5.2 Signal Conditioning

As we have discussed earlier, the complete measurement input signal comes out of two stages in general: the primary transducer and the secondary transducer. The primary transducer essentially consists of primary elements that are in physical contact with the process; these elements may also be called sensors. Usually the sensor signals are not available in standard form (our standard form of electrical signal is 4 to 20 mA D.C.). Sensors or primary elements may give the result of measurement in the form of circuit elements, that is, a change in resistance, inductance or capacitance, or in the form of extremely low voltages, that is, millivolts or microvolts. Such signals cannot be used for further work, namely for indicating, recording or controlling. It is the job of the secondary part of the transducer to condition these signals, in their original form, and convert them into standard form, that is, into 4 to 20 mA D.C. or 0.25 to 1.25 V.

Sometimes conditioning means more than this. For instance, in analysis instruments the sample is drawn from the process in raw form for the analysis of its different contents. This raw sample may be cooled (if it is in heated form), filtered, cleaned and so on. It has to pass through a closed chamber for carrying out cooling, filtering, cleaning, etc. In the case of analysis instruments these operations also come under conditioning. NOx and SOx analyzers have such signal conditioner units.

Next, the power supply system, that is, excitation in the case of a passive transducer system, and then the amplification of the low-level signal are also considered part of the signal conditioning unit. Sometimes a few main components of the data acquisition system, for example the sample-and-hold circuit, multiplexer, converter, etc., are also studied under the signal conditioning unit. We therefore observe that the scope of study of the signal conditioner is much larger.

As far as the conditioning unit in the form of a secondary transducer is concerned, we have already discussed it at great length in the previous chapter, so no further discussion is needed here. Similarly, we shall not discuss the details of the signal conditioning unit applicable to analysis instruments, because this is beyond the scope of the present discussion. We shall therefore start our discussion of the topic with the excitation unit, or power pack unit.

5.2.1 Power Pack Unit

A power pack unit or excitation unit is essentially a power supply system designed to supply a particular level of voltage or a combination of more than one voltage level, as required by the measurement circuit as well as by the amplifier unit. Since the combination of the measurement and amplification systems may be of A.C. type or D.C. type, the power pack unit can also be designed to supply A.C. or D.C. Both types of supply unit essentially have a transformer in common. A general block diagram showing the application of the power pack unit is given in Figure 5.3.

Figure 5.4 shows a typical but practical circuit diagram of a power pack unit supplying a voltage from +Vx to −Vx for the amplifier unit that needs such a supply. In this circuit three types of diode, one type of capacitor, one type of resistor and one type of transistor have been used. Similarity of nomenclature for a circuit element indicates similarity of characteristic. The circuit, in its original or an improved version, is used in many instrumentation applications.

5.2.2 Amplifier Unit

The low-level input signal obtained from the sensor has to be amplified, that is, its level has to be enhanced, so that further processing becomes possible for obtaining signals to drive indicators, recorders or controllers. Instrumentation amplifiers are almost invariably used to carry out the amplification. In earlier instrumentation systems, when A.C. amplification was popular, the instrumentation amplifier was called a chopper-modulated instrument amplifier. The input signal received in D.C. form was first converted into A.C. by means of a mechanically operated chopper in combination with low-power transformers, as shown in Figure 5.5. The output of the chopper in A.C. form is amplified and then rectified to recover the original input signal in D.C. form. There are instrumentation operations where a signal in D.C. form may not be required, so the amplifier output may be allowed to remain as it is. For example, in temperature recorder applications an A.C. servomotor is employed to operate the recording-cum-printing operations.

In situations where the design of the A.C. amplifier is of the common type (as is the case in most instances), the chopper can be of many designs. Some of these are the oscillator-driven electromechanical type (a popular design in earlier temperature recorders), the transistor-operated chopper, the photo-optical chopper, and so on.

In the modern design of the instrumentation amplifier, however, the advent of the operational amplifier and its possible applications has changed the complete basic design concept and has thus improved the performance of the instrumentation amplifier. The operational amplifier in its basic form is shown in Figure 5.6.

The operational amplifier circuit can be viewed as two different amplifiers cascaded together as a constant current source. The open-loop voltage gain of such an amplifier is more than 100,000. When it is connected as an inverter, the non-inverting, that is, positive input is grounded. The input signal voltage is applied to the inverting input, marked negative. Since the gain is very high, an analogue input signal voltage (e_i) of the order of a microvolt can also deliver a considerable output voltage (e_o) to operate display instruments. In this way, zero input voltage together with infinite input resistance make the inverting input a virtual ground (ground for voltage, not for current). Though it has zero voltage, it cannot sink any current. The inverting input of an operational amplifier being virtually grounded means zero voltage and zero current. Because of the very high gain and high input resistance, the inverting input is virtually grounded, so all the input voltage appears across the input resistor R_i. Since no input current enters the virtual ground, it must pass through the output resistor R_0, and therefore the output current equals the input current. The minus sign at the output indicates phase inversion. The output resistance of the amplifier is very low and the common-mode noise rejection is also high. The operational amplifier can perform many mathematical and other operations efficiently. Some of these operations are presented here in the shortest possible way.

Virtual ground: zero voltage and zero current; all the input voltage appears across R_i and the output current equals the input current.

Inverter

The input/output equation is:

(e_i − e_g)/R = (e_g − e_o)/R

Since −e_o/e_g = A (gain), e_g = −e_o/A.

Hence,

e_i + e_o/A = −e_o/A − e_o

or e_i = −e_o (since e_o/A is negligible).

Adder

The input/output equation is:

(e_1 − e_g)/R + (e_2 − e_g)/R = (e_g − e_o)/R
e_1 + e_o/A + e_2 + e_o/A = −e_o/A − e_o

or e_1 + e_g = −e_o (since e_o/A is negligible).

Hence e_o = −(e_1 + e_2).

Subtractor

The input/output equation is:

e_o = (e_2 − e_1)

Integrator

The input/output equation is:

(e_i − e_g)/R = C·d(e_g − e_o)/dt
(e_i + e_o/A)/R = C·d(e_o/A − e_o)/dt
e_i/R = −C·de_o/dt
de_o/dt = −e_i/(RC)
e_o = −(1/RC)·∫₀ᵗ e_i·dt

If for a particular condition R = 1 MΩ and C = 1 μF, then:

e_o = −∫₀ᵗ e_i·dt

Differentiator

The input/output equation is:

C·d(e_i − e_o)/dt = (e_g − e_o)/R
C·d(e_i + e_o/A)/dt = (−e_o/A − e_o)/R
C·de_i/dt = −e_o/R
e_o = −RC·de_i/dt

Again, if for a particular condition R = 1 MΩ and C = 1 μF, then:

e_o = −de_i/dt

Similarly, other operations can be designed by employing the operational amplifier.

Instrumentation amplifier

The instrumentation amplifier is designed to have such a stable resistive feedback unit that the desired gain can be obtained with the highest precision. The characteristic of high common-mode rejection of the operational amplifier is fully utilized in designing the instrumentation amplifier. Figure 5.12 shows a basic form of instrumentation amplifier. Instrument manufacturers modify the system slightly here and there to suit their particular measurement circuit and add their own trademark (TM). In this design, the operational amplifiers OA1 and OA2 have unity gain for the common-mode noise signal e_n. The calculation of the gain for the differential signals e_1 and e_2 can be made according to the following equations, with reference to the diagram.

e_g1 = (1 + R_1/R_x)·e_1 − (R_1/R_x)·(e_n + e_2)
e_g2 = (1 + R_2/R_x)·e_2 − (R_2/R_x)·(e_n + e_1)
e_o = (e_g2 − e_g1)

If for a particular condition R_1 can be made equal to R_2, then:

e_o = (1 + 2R_1/R_x)·(e_2 − e_1)

The drift problem in an instrumentation amplifier is the really troublesome area, where design refinements in amplifier units are required. The gain of the instrumentation amplifier can be changed by changing the variable resistance R_x.

5.3 Multiplexing

When a number of process variables are required to be transmitted on one channel only, the time shared method of measurement and transmission is employed and is known as multiplexing. For a PC (Personal Computer) based data acquisition system to operate properly, the analog signals available from the field mounted transducers are scanned through a scanner and transmitted through a multiplexing unit. In this arrangement, a sample and hold circuit is added before the multiplexer unit and an analog-to-digital converter (ADC) is installed after the multiplexer unit, as shown in Figure 5.13. In this way, the analog inputs are multiplexed channel-wise with the communication system for further transmission and processing.

The computer generated address lines select an analog channel through the decoder one by one for sampling. A simple two-to-four decoder is presented in Figure 5.14. A higher order decoder can be manipulated easily on the basis of this one. As is observed, there are four AND gates used for a two-to-four decoder. In a similar way, for a four-to-sixteen decoder, sixteen AND gates will be required.

On a similar pattern, a simple four-channel analog multiplexing unit is presented in Figure 5.15. Depending upon the status of the address lines, the decoder selects a particular FET switch and the appropriate analog input from the transducer is connected to the analog output line. A higher order multiplexing unit can be designed on a similar pattern.

Figure 5.16 shows a simple four-to-one digital type of multiplexer unit.

5.3.1 Sample and Hold Unit

As indicated by Figure 5.13, the sample and hold unit feeds the transducer signals systematically to the analog multiplexer unit. The operation of a sample and hold unit is simple and is shown in Figure 5.17. It is in fact a combination of a switch (operated by a relay) and a holding capacitor Ch, as shown. The voltage across the holding capacitor Ch follows Vin (the input signal) during the sampling period, when the switching pulse closes the switch contact, and holds the charge at the capacitor during the hold period. When the switch contact closes, the holding capacitor is charged to a voltage equal to the input voltage level. When the switch contact opens, the capacitor continues to hold the voltage acquired during sampling. The period for which the capacitor holds the voltage is called the holding time.

In the modern design shown in Figure 5.18, a pulse driven FET switch operates upon the input signal and the holding capacitor Ch just follows the input signals. The holding capacitor Ch is connected to the output line through a voltage follower element having very high input impedance, which ultimately restricts the discharge of capacitor Ch to a controlled amount only.

5.3.2 Data Conversion (ADC and DAC)

Most of the measurement process inputs are basically formed in analog form and do not need any data conversion if the system of instrumentation is of analog type, e.g. indication, record or control. But if the system of instrumentation and control is of digital nature then the analog measurement signals have to be converted into digital form for facilitating digital indication, record and control in the conventional system. Also the signals so obtained can be processed effectively through computer system for advanced control functions.

An analog-to-digital conversion (ADC) system employs four different techniques:

  1. Ramp type
  2. Integrating type
  3. Potentiometer type or continuous balance type
  4. Successive approximation type

1. Ramp type ADC conversion

In this type, the time interval that a ramp type standard voltage will take to change from an input voltage level to its zero level is measured by an electronic time interval counter.

As shown in Figure 5.19, an active ramp voltage when applied to the measurement circuit is continuously compared with respect to the unknown voltage to be measured. When the ramp voltage first coincides with the input voltage, an input comparator initiates a pulse to open the gate as shown in Figure 5.20.

When the ramp voltage (while decreasing) reaches zero level of the input voltage, the ground comparator initiates a pulse and closes the gate. The time between opening and closing of the gate is t and this is the interval during which whatever pulses, generated by clock oscillator, pass through the gate are counted and displayed as voltage in digital form. The sample rate multivibrator determines the rate of initiation of measurement cycle. The sample rate circuit simultaneously initiates starting of the ramp generator as well as resetting of counters.

2. Integrating type ADC conversion

As we know that the ADC conversion employing ramp type circuit samples the input value at the end of measurement period, the integrating type circuit, on the other hand, measures the average value of the input over a fixed measurement period. The technique employed here is the voltage to frequency conversion which works as a feedback control system governing rate of pulse generation with respect to magnitude of input signal voltage. In effect the frequency of the generated pulses depends upon the input voltage Ei to be measured. Therefore, number of pulses appearing during a definite interval is counted and is the indication of input voltage in digital form. Operational amplifier used in this technique acts as an integrator and the output Eo of the integrator is given by the equation:

Eo = −Ei × (1/RC) × t

This equation describes that for a constant voltage input the output of the integrator is a ramp voltage of opposite polarity. The same characteristics have been shown on graphical presentation as well on Figures 5.21 and 5.22 from where it is also clear that for higher values at input voltage, the slope of output voltage is sharper.

Figure 5.23 indicates implementation of the above description. The input Ei is applied to integrator and the output Eo is fed to a level detection circuit which sends a pulse to a pulse generator gate when the level of Eo reaches a fixed reference level. The level detector in effect is a voltage comparator circuit where Eo is compared with respect to a reference. Greater the value of Ei, sharper shall be the slope of Eo and faster the Eo will reach the reference level in the level detector circuit. The pulse generator gate when enabled by level detector signal, sends clock oscillator pulses to pulse generator. Output of pulse generator are pulses of constant but, higher amplitude and width whose polarity is opposite to Ei. This is fed back to the integrator and due to reversal of input to the integrator, net input is changed and also the output Eo drops back to original level. Since Eo drops below the reference of level detector, there is no output from level detector circuit to pulse generator gate and so this gate is closed and no more pulse is fed back to integrator. So Ei again gets restored to original value and Eo again starts rising and whole process is once again restarted. Thus, the wave form of Eo will be seen as sawtooth wave whose risetime will depend upon the input Ei and fall time will be determined by the width of output pulse of the pulse generator. Hence the frequency of Eo (sawtooth wave) is a function of magnitude of Ei.

Since for each cycle of Eo in the form of sawtooth wave, one pulse is obtained from the pulse generator, number of pulses produced in this way in a given interval, i.e. frequency of sawtooth wave is a measure of unknown voltage. For measurement of this frequency, the clock oscillator pulse is also applied to time base selector, which opens start-stop gate and in turn opens the main gate and the pulse generator output enters the main gate in addition to the integrator. Next pulse from time base selector closes start-stop gate and also the main gate. Hence, the counter will indicate the number of pulses that have passed during a known interval of time. This is the value of voltage to be measured. The amplitude and pulse width of the pulse generator can be adjusted to read the counter directly in voltage.

3. Potentiometer type ADC conversion

The potentiometer circuit employed for this type of ADC conversion is basically self-balancing type. The unknown input voltage to be measured is applied to the precision potentiometer unit and is compared with respect to reference voltage supplied by zener diode voltage source as shown in Figure 5.24. The resulting error voltage is converted to A.C. (error voltage being of D.C. type) by converter and then fed to servo-amplifier through pre-amplifier. Servo-amplification is needed to increase the signal level necessary for operation of servo-balancing system. The servo-balancing system, consisting of servo-motor along with its control and reference windings, balances the potentiometer and brings back the slide wire to equilibrium state.

Other systems of digital display can also be used. As is evident, here the principle of balancing, and not sampling, has been used.

4. Successive approximation type ADC conversion

The principle of successive approximation compares an input voltage in analog form to the reference voltage of a DAC (Decode Assembly Counter), which is divided repeatedly into half. As seen in Figure 5.25, a four-digit binary number 1000, representing Er (full reference voltage), is divided into half (the equivalent binary number is 100) to give the corresponding voltage 1/2 Er. This reference voltage 1/2 Er is compared to the analog voltage. If the comparison indicates that the first approximation is too small (that is, 1/2 Er < analog input), then the next comparison is made with respect to 3/4 Er (the equivalent binary number is 110). If this comparison indicates that the first approximation was too large (that is, 1/2 Er > analog input), then the next comparison is made with respect to 1/4 Er (the equivalent binary number is 010). After four successive approximations, the required digital number is resolved. An eight-digit number will be resolved in eight successive approximations.

Figure 5.26 shows an ADC conversion system employing the successive approximation method. The analog input voltage is applied to the comparison circuit; the other signal to the comparator is a reference voltage applied through a digital-to-analog (D/A) converter (DAC). As indicated by the block diagram, at start the control register and the distribution register are set with a 1 in the MSB (most significant bit) and a 0 in all the remaining LSB (least significant bit) positions. So the distribution register indicates that the conversion cycle of the analog input signal has started and is at its first stage. The control register indicates 1000 and this brings an output voltage from the D/A converter circuit of the order of 1/2 of the reference voltage. At the same instant, a pulse enters the time delay circuit.

By the time the D/A converter and comparator circuit have settled, the delayed pulse, after the time delay, is gated through the comparator output. When the next MSB is set in the control register circuit by the timing circuit, the present MSB remains at 1 or is reset to 0, based on the output of the comparator circuit. The state 1 of the distribution register is shifted to the next nearby position and keeps track of the next comparison. The procedure is repeated until the end of the last approximation operation, and finally the end of conversion is reported by the distribution register.

In order to avoid any change in the analog voltage input taking place during the conversion period (from analog to digital form), the analog voltage input is taken through a sample and hold circuit. The circuit takes a sample of the analog voltage input in a very short time and holds it at the same value until the conversion from analog to digital is completed.

5.3.3 Resolution of Digital Meters

The number of digits used in a digital meter decides the resolution. This means a three-digit digital meter of range 0 to 1 volt shall be capable of indicating 0 to 999 millivolts with a resolution of 1 millivolt. However, for industrial use, a fourth digit, if placed on the left of the active digit, is capable of indicating either a zero or a one. Thus, the value 999 as described above will go above 999 up to 1999, which gives overloading between ranges. This is called over-ranging. This type of display shall be called a 3½-digit display. However, the resolution of a digital panel meter is determined by the number of full active digits only.

If n is the number of full active digits, then the resolution is defined as:

Resolution = 1/10ⁿ

Thus, a four-digit display will have a resolution of:

1/10⁴ = 0.0001 = 0.01 %

This is also called resolution at 1 in 10,000.

Sensitivity of digital meter

Sensitivity is defined as the smallest change in signal input which a digital meter is capable of detecting. Mathematically, sensitivity S is given by the relation:

S = lowest full scale value × resolution

5.4 Accuracy of Digital Meter

Accuracy of a digital meter is expressed in terms of percentage of the reading plus a percentage of the LSD (least significant digit). For instance, accuracy of ± 0.2 per cent of reading ± 2-digit indicates that 0.2 per cent multiplied by the indicated value is one part of the error and the percentage of LSD, that is 2-digit, which comes to 0.02 per cent, is to be added to the first part of the error.

The acceptance level of error during the quantization process is known as quantization error and is dependent on the number of quantization levels. The quantization level is an index of the resolution of the instrument. The quantization error is shown in Figure 5.27. The peak-to-peak value is C. The empirical relation for the rms value of the quantization error Ce is given by:

Ce = C / (2√3)

5.5 Aperture Time for Sample and Hold Mechanism

Sample and hold is a general system used in data processing and transmission circuits, and therefore our discussion is limited to the duration for which an analog signal must be sampled and the sampled value held at that particular level so that an acceptable level of quantization and coding is obtained. This duration is known as the aperture time. The empirical relation for the aperture time Ta is given by,

Ta = ΔE / (2π Em f)

where ΔE is the change in amplitude level at voltage E, Em is the maximum value of the sinusoidal voltage and f is the frequency of the voltage. For successive-approximation conversion, the aperture time equals the conversion time.

In general, sampling must be carried out in such a way that no part of the information carried by the signal is lost, while keeping the number of samples as small as possible. The theoretical sampling rate proposed by Nyquist must be at least twice the frequency of the signal to be sampled. If fs is the frequency of the signal to be sampled, the sampling rate will be 2fs. In practice, however, it is taken as 2.5fs to compensate for noise and other limitations of the circuit components.

Digital-to-Analog Conversion (DAC)

The digital-to-analog conversion system employs resistor networks. Additional circuits are combined for the temporary storage of the digital signal as well as for shifting the voltage level. There are two types of DAC technique:

  • Weighted resistor network DAC system.
  • Ladder network DAC system.

(a) Weighted resistor network DAC system

In this system there are n input resistors combined with an equal number of switches weighted in binary progression, as indicated in Figure 5.28. Here n represents the number of bits selected for conversion. Vref is a stable reference voltage and the resistors used are of precision type, chosen for the highest possible accuracy, because the accuracy of conversion also depends on the components used. The operational amplifier OA acts as a current-to-voltage converter and sums the flowing currents. This in turn develops the output voltage V0 as the analog output signal, exactly proportional to the digital input supplied.

For a particular condition, when all the switches are closed, Dn−1 = Dn−2 = … = D2 = D1 = D0 = 1, and the currents are:

In−1 = Vref / R
In−2 = Vref / (2 · R)
I2 = Vref / (2^(n−3) · R)
I1 = Vref / (2^(n−2) · R)
I0 = Vref / (2^(n−1) · R)

Hence, the output in the form of current is:

Iout = In−1 + In−2 + … + I2 + I1 + I0
Iout = (Vref / R) [1 + 1/2 + … + 1/2^(n−3) + 1/2^(n−2) + 1/2^(n−1)]

The general expression for the output current is given by:

Iout = (Vref / R) [Dn−1/1 + Dn−2/2 + Dn−3/2² + … + D1/2^(n−2) + D0/2^(n−1)]

The limitation of this method of conversion lies in the fact that resistors of exactly identical value are difficult to find. Each step of the conversion needs to be weighted properly, which is impossible to realize because no two resistors are identical. This results in an error in the output. Also, the range of resistance values keeps increasing as a multiple of two of the preceding one, and this makes the system more complicated as the number of bits is increased.

(b) Ladder network DAC system

In this system, resistors of only two different values are used and it is therefore more attractive than the weighted-resistor type. This system eliminates all the problems encountered with the weighted resistor system.

If we design a ladder resistor network as shown in Figure 5.29 and calculate the equivalent resistance looking from points A, B, C and D towards the left, the final resistance value turns out to be only R. This has been shown successively in Figures 5.30, 5.31, 5.32 and 5.33.

This reduction process of the resistor network indicates that all the nodes are positive and binary weighted. Therefore, if a binary word is applied at the nodes A, B, C and D, the ladder can divide a reference current into binary-weighted levels. Figure 5.34 shows such an arrangement, where a reference current of 2 milliamperes has been applied, which results in the distribution of current indicated in the diagram itself.

The nodes of the R-2R ladder can divide a reference current into weighted binary levels. Figure 5.34 shows such an arrangement, in which a reference current of 2 milliamperes is applied, resulting in the current distribution indicated in the diagram. For the four-bit conversion system, the current output Iout is given by:

Iout = (Iref/2)[D₃ + 2⁻¹D₂ + 2⁻²D₁ + 2⁻³D₀]

The general expression for the output current Iout can be extended as:

Iout = (Iref/2)[Dₙ₋₁ + 2⁻¹Dₙ₋₂ + … + 2⁻⁽ⁿ⁻¹⁾D₀]

The basic circuit can be improved by adding an operational amplifier, in which the switches connect to the reference voltage for a binary input of 1 and to ground for a binary input of 0. The amplifier output is in analog form.

5.5.1 PC-based Practical Data Acquisition System

A practical PC-based data acquisition system (DAS) performs real-time data logging operations via a suitable communication protocol such as Ethernet, RS-232C, RS-422, RS-485 or GP-IB. The universal input module completes its functions of scanning/multiplexing and analog-to-digital conversion in every 500 milliseconds for a 300-channel system. The input signals include all types of signals present in any process industry. In addition, an alarm module is an essential feature, which outputs contact alarm signals according to a preset point condition. Monitoring of the power supply system to the data acquisition system is done by measuring the root mean square (rms) value of the AC supply, active power, apparent power, reactive power, frequency, power factor and phase angle. The data update cycle for the power supply system can be up to 2 seconds. Apart from the others, the three most important parts of a PC-based data acquisition system are: (a) hardware, (b) software, and (c) communication link.

(a) Hardware

Nowadays data acquisition systems use ASICs (application specific integrated circuits) developed by the particular manufacturer and becoming their trademark. ASICs bring a high degree of system integration and make the system smaller and lighter. This altogether results in low power consumption, low heat generation and thereby improves the reliability of the overall system. Another improvement in design is the planar transformer used in the data acquisition system. A planar transformer differs from a conventional wire-wound transformer. This small and thin transformer consists of multi-layer precision thin-film coils that enhance insulation strength while also reducing heat and noise emission. Still another design improvement is the SSR switches (solid state relay switches), which have a high breakdown voltage (nearly 1500 volts DC). This voltage matches industrial safety norms. Their low leakage current, of the order of 1 nA (one nanoampere), enables very low level voltage signals from thermocouples, pH, conductivity and other analysis equipment to be measured with higher accuracy. Power consumption is low owing to their quiet operation compared with other forms of relays.

(b) Software

The data acquisition system software runs on all Windows-based platforms (95 onwards) and on the 32-bit Windows operating system. The main software, common to all data acquisition functions, includes hardware set-up, simplified data logging, simplified data viewing, data conversion through MS Excel or ASCII format, preference setting, system diagnosis and calibration functions. Another software, called parametric setting software, allows the exchange of all set-up related data items, for example the range of measurement, duration of measurement, start time of measurement, alarm generation, event/action expression and computation functions by the PC.

The third software, known as system set-up, diagnosis and calibration software, mainly defines the communication method with the main unit and also sets up a directory for this purpose. It also allows viewing the calibration data contained in the ROM (read only memory) and initializing all the settings/configuration of the main unit.

The fourth software is the data logger and viewing (through tag setting) software, which is important to the user. It shows all the measured and computed data on the display unit. These data can be saved on an external storage unit by the data acquisition functions. Various graphic tools, such as analog trending waves, digital display windows and alarm overview windows, enable optimum screen configuration for every application. Tag numbers for every channel can be shown by means of the tag setting software in 8 or 16 characters.

(c) Communication link

Communication links are necessary for interconnecting digital systems together to share as well as consolidate the information contained in the process input signals. The communication link is yet another important component of the data acquisition system under discussion. The physical links, which may be a twisted pair of wires, a coaxial cable or a fiber optic cable, have been discussed at length in the previous chapter. Here we confine our discussion to the types of communication interface standards, which include the following common varieties for data acquisition functions: (i) RS-232 (Recommended Standard), (ii) RS-422, (iii) RS-485, (iv) Ethernet, (v) GPIB (General Purpose Interface Bus), and (vi) CAN (Controller Area Network).

(i) RS-232 Standard

The recommended standard RS-232 was developed by the EIA (Electronic Industries Association); it is a serial communication interface and the most popular interface in industry. It is installed on all personal computers (COM1 and COM2) and modems. The standard specifies a 25-pin connector for DCE (data communication equipment) and DTE (data terminal equipment). The detailed drawing of the RS-232D link is shown in Figure 5.36. The voltage level in the RS-232 standard ranges from ±3 V DC to ±25 V DC. A voltage level between +3 V and +25 V represents a 0 bit, and a level between −3 V and −25 V represents a 1 bit (both levels are with respect to the signal ground pin). The RS-232 standard is not used beyond 50 feet or at data rates above 20 kbps.

(ii) RS-422 Standard

The RS-422 standard requires altogether four wires (two each for the transmit and receive terminals), whereas RS-232 requires only three wires. Both connection details, that is the three-wire system for RS-232 and the four-wire system for RS-422, are shown in Figure 5.37. As can be seen, RS-422 is balanced with respect to ground, so it can be used over large distances and can operate at the much higher data rate of 10 Mbps. The twisted wire pairs used in RS-422 enhance the noise immunity of the whole communication system, which is an added advantage and also an economy. The signalling voltage on an RS-422 link ranges between ±200 millivolts and ±6 volts DC. A voltage level between +200 mV and +6 V DC represents a 0 bit, and a level between −200 mV and −6 V DC represents a 1 bit.

(iii) RS-485 Standard

The RS-485 standard is constructional equivalent to RS-422, with the only difference being the data rate (56 kbps for RS-485). But the major technical difference between them is that RS-485 has been designed for network communications, which is completely different from point-to-point communications. In a point-to-point communication link only two digital attachments are interconnected, and therefore they do not require any network protocol at all. In a network communication system, however, more than two digital attachments are always present, and therefore the problem of sharing the link with other devices arises. Coordinating data transfer among the various digital attachments needs network topology in addition to network protocol, and therefore the communication link standards differ. Since instrumentation systems involve only a medium-performance network, for example multidrop smart transmitters, RS-485 has been designed accordingly and is the most suitable standard link for the purpose. The simple and most inexpensive RS-485 link is installed in combination with bus topology, as shown in Figure 5.38.

(iv) Ethernet

Although Ethernet is commonly used in office networking, interconnectivity of instrumentation operations and administration in the form of CIM (computer integrated manufacturing) has now begun. Ethernet has become an industry standard. Since Ethernet is required to have connectivity with different systems, it defines two layers: one is the physical link and the other is access through contention. The physical layer requires a 10 Mbit link and the network access method is CSMA/CD, that is Carrier Sense Multiple Access with Collision Detection. Messages sent through Ethernet are in the form of packets, whose format is shown in Figure 5.39.

Ethernet is a random access control technique in which a station wishing to transmit waits to check whether the system is free. If the system is free, it begins to transmit, which is called carrier sense multiple access. It also monitors the network to avoid multiple transmissions. Multiple transmissions resulting in collisions may be detected by observing the garbled transmission appearing on the network; this is collision detection. If a collision does occur, the colliding stations stop transmission for a brief period and then attempt to retransmit. During periods of higher network load, a number of collisions can be expected. Therefore the CSMA/CD protocol may pose problems for real-time process data acquisition, since a station may lose some critical data that may be absolutely necessary for a particular process condition. For this reason, when Ethernet is used for a data acquisition system, the load on the network is kept balanced so that collisions can be avoided.

(v) GPIB System

Interconnection of different instruments for common visualization has always been a problem because of the various control and other unique features associated with every instrument. GPIB solved this problem very economically, making possible low-cost interconnection of up to 15 instruments even with different data rates. GPIB also makes direct communication between instruments possible. GPIB normally consists of sixteen different lines. Lines 1 to 8 form the multiplexed data/command signal lines, lines 9 to 11 are kept for handshaking, and lines 12 to 16 are reserved for management functions of the different equipment connected in the system. The bus length is restricted to 20 metres and the data transfer rate is up to 3 MB/second (design figure; the practical figure may be less). The GPIB cable has identical connectors at both ends and is costly. In a GPIB system, at one time only one piece of equipment is in transmission mode while the remaining interconnected equipment are in receiving mode. The receiver receives data from the 8 lines (DI01 to DI08). Data transmission operations are accomplished by means of three signal lines: (i) DAV (data valid on the data lines), (ii) NRFD (no receiver for new data), and (iii) NDAC (data not accepted by all the receivers).

The on-line transmitter controls the DAV line, whereas the receivers control the NRFD and NDAC lines. The system controller (IFC), which is line number 12, takes over control of the system. The active controller (ATN), which is line number 13, controls the multiplexing functions of the data/command signals. Line number 14 (SRQ) is the service request and acts as a request signal for service to the active controller. Line number 15 (REN) performs remote enabling control functions, and line number 16 (EOI) indicates the end of a block.

(vi) CAN (Controller Area Network)

CAN communication protocol was originally designed for automobile applications. A few special points have to be taken care of when a communication protocol is used for this purpose. The protocol should be able to provide the required timely response with predictable and minimum latency to the dynamic and asynchronous behaviour of automobiles. CAN is a modified version of the traditional CSMA/CD access method. The protocol utilizes bit-to-bit arbitration of a non-destructive type to transmit messages simultaneously and arbitrate them priority-wise. Since the node controlling the bus also monitors to detect whether it has lost arbitration, in that case it returns to receiving mode until the higher-priority transmission ceases. The message acknowledgement scheme of CAN is typical in that nodes are aware of the exact status of the message, resulting in better consistency and a reduction in error recovery time to less than 25 microseconds at a bus speed of 1 megabit per second.

The excellent features of the CAN protocol described above have also attracted process automation industries, and it has been utilized in some distributed data acquisition schemes. CAN employs NRZ formatting along with a bit stuffing scheme for data transmission. Bit stuffing maintains network synchronization that would otherwise be lost due to superimposition of noise voltage. The standard format of the message consists of eight fields, as shown in Figure 5.40.

SOF and EOF are the start of the message frame and the end of the message frame, respectively. ARB and RTR are technically combined fields, where ARB is an arbitration field and RTR is a code message indicating whether the message shall have a reply or not. CTRL is a 6-bit control field and DATA is the information field consisting of up to 8 bytes only. CRC (Cyclic Redundancy Code) is a 16-bit error checking field. ACK is a 2-bit acknowledge field consisting of an ACK slot (1 bit) and an ACK delimiter (1 bit).

The TransNet design

In another design of a PC-based data acquisition system, the Host PC incorporates two additional microprocessors. One is called the input/output processor (IOP), for regulating input/output (I/O) data, and the other is called the TransNet Processor, for regulating the TransNet communication system. The IOP continuously updates the I/O data with respect to the data of the TransNet Processor. This operation is not controlled by the Host PC at all, but the Host PC can access this information. Data validation and maintenance of system status are also performed by the IOP. The TransNet Processor in fact controls the exchange of data between the Host PC and the universal I/O modules. These modules scan process data from the transducers and final control elements installed in the process plants, and also perform self-diagnostic checks for possible errors and unexpected faults. The process data information is transferred to the TransNet processor, the IOP processes the data and updates the RAM. The entire system works on the principle of distributed and parallel processing for better system performance.

The communication system of TransNet comprises multiple microprocessors connected through a high-speed serial bus and interfaced with the Host PC through RAM as well. Information is transferred to the Host PC through RAM. The system scans the I/O data and either transfers new information to the Host PC through an interrupt, or continues scanning until interrupted by the Host PC itself. Another way is that while the system continues to scan the I/O data without interrupting the Host PC, a bit flag is set to let the Host PC know that new updated data have arrived and are available for access on the RAM. The TransNet communication system operates in dual mode: the first mode is called normal scanning mode and the second is called Interrupt Mode. During normal scanning mode, universal I/O modules are scanned in sequence by the IOP. The modules in turn respond according to their existing status, starting from module number 1 (as configured in the table) and continuing one by one. Every module is allotted a time slot and has to complete its response within that slot; if it fails to do so, the processor generates an error code for that particular module. The output module in particular expects a response on command from the Host PC. If the modules do not get a valid response, they revert to default conditions. During Interrupt Mode, the Host PC interrupts the normal scanning operations to inquire about system variables, module status or anything else required to be known at that moment.

Distributed data acquisition and control system

The data acquisition system under discussion is shown in a block diagram view in Figure 5.41. A twin axial cable has been used as the physical link for the system. A twin axial cable has a pair of centrally located conductors with an overall shielding. It offers superior noise immunity and higher speed.

As is obvious, we are going through different types of modern data acquisition systems based on networking technology. Along this line, another system is in operation which may be called a distributed data acquisition and control system, in which industry-popular field buses such as RS-485 and CAN (Controller Area Network) have been used, and the systems communicate with the Host PC over a multidrop fieldbus network as shown in Figure 5.42. Another peculiarity of this system is that it supports a composite cable system. A composite cable system is a custom-made physical link that encapsulates all types of communication cables, for example twisted pair cable, coaxial cable and fibre optic cables, in a single overall jacket.

Yet another peculiarity of this system is that it works on any unregulated power supply system (between +10 V and +30 V D.C.). The system has adopted 3-level isolation, i.e. I/O isolation through local bus, power isolation through isolation transformer and communication controller isolation through opto-coupling isolation, as shown in Figure 5.43. The isolation is of the order of 3000 volt D.C. for I/O modules and power, whereas it is 2500 volt D.C. for the communication system. The system also provides self-test for the hardware and diagnostic feature for the software in addition to a watchdog supervisory scheme.

Watchdog supervision is implemented through a watchdog timer in which there can be a specified number of normally closed contacts referenced to itself. This is ON for one scan, OFF for the next scan as long as the system is in operation. Thus, it takes two scans for the watchdog timer to complete its full cycle. The hardware in the processor monitors the watchdog timer in such a way that if it does not change its status every specified time, say 200 millisecond or so, the system gets shutdown indicating system failure.

EXAMPLES

Example 5.1

(a) What is the resolution of a 3½ digit display? (b) If the range of a 3½ digit digital meter is (i) 1 volt and (ii) 10 volt, what are the resolutions in the two cases?

Solution

(a) Number of full active digits = 3.

∴ Resolution = 1/10³ = 0.001 = 0.1%

(b)(i) Here full scale reading = 1.000;

Resolution on 1 volt range = 1 × 0.001 = 0.001 volt

(ii) In this case full scale reading = 10.00;

Resolution on 10 volt range = 10 × 0.001 = 0.01 volt

Example 5.2

A 4½ digit digital voltmeter is used for voltage measurement. (a) Find its resolution. (b) How would 12.75 V be displayed on the 10 V range? (c) How would 0.7525 V be displayed on the 1 V range? (d) How would 0.7525 V be displayed on the 10 V range?

Solution

(a) Here number of full active digit = 4.

∴ Resolution = 1/10⁴ = 0.0001 = 0.01%

(b) There are five digits on a 4½ digit display, so 12.75 V will be displayed as 12.750 V on the 10 V scale.

(c) In this case, resolution on the 1 V range:

1 × 0.0001 = 0.0001 V

So on the 1 V range, any reading is displayed up to the 4th decimal place, and 0.7525 is displayed as 0.7525 volt on the 1 volt range.

(d) In this particular case, resolution on the 10 V range:

10 × 0.0001 = 0.001 V

So on the 10 V range, readings are displayed up to the 3rd place only, and 0.7525 is displayed as 0.752 volt on the 10 volts range.

Example 5.3

A 3½ digit voltmeter has accuracy of ± 0.2 per cent of reading ± 2 digits. (a) What shall be the error when the meter reads 3 volts on the 10 volts range? (b) What shall be the error when the reading is 0.10 volt on the 10 volts range? (c) What percentage of indicated reading shall be the error in (b)?

Solution

(a) 0.2 per cent of indicated value is

0.002 × 3.00 = 0.006 volt

Display for a 3 volts reading on the 10 volts scale of a 3½ digit meter shall be 03.00. Now the percentage of digit on LSD (Least Significant Digit) is 0.02 volt.

Total error = 0.006 + 0.02 = 0.026 volt

(b) When the indicated value is 0.10 volt on the 10 volts range,

± 0.2 per cent of reading = 0.002 × 0.10 = 0.0002 volt

± 2 digits on LSD gives an error of ± 0.02 volt.

Total error = 0.0002 + 0.02 = 0.0202 volt

(c) Percentage error in (b):

0.0202/0.100 = 0.0202/0.1 × 100 = 20.2%

Example 5.4

A 3½ digit voltmeter is used for measuring voltage. (a) Find the resolution of the indicator. (b) How would a voltage of 15.43 V be displayed on the 10 V scale? (c) How would a reading of 15.43 V be displayed on the 100 V scale?

Solution

(a) Number of full active digit on a 3½ digit digital display = 3.

∴ Resolution = 1/10³ = 0.001 = 0.1%

(b) There are four digits on a 3½ digit digital display, so 15.43 V shall be displayed as 15.43 V on the 10 V scale.

(c) Resolution on the 100 V range:

0.001 × 100 = 0.1 V

Hence, 15.43 shall be displayed as 15.4 V.

Example 5.5

The lower range on a 4½ digit digital voltmeter is 10 millivolt full scale. Find sensitivity.

Solution

Number of full active digit on a 4½ digit digital display = 4.

∴ Resolution = 1/10⁴ = 0.0001

Sensitivity = Lowest full scale value × Resolution.

10 mV × 0.0001 = 1 × 10⁻⁶ = 1 μV

Example 5.6

A 3½ digit digital voltmeter has accuracy ± 0.5% of reading ± 2 digits. (a) What is the error when the instrument reads 5.00 volt on its 10 volts range? (b) What is the error when the instrument reads 0.10 volt on its 10 volts range? (c) What is the percentage error in (b)?

Solution

(a) 0.5% of indicated value:

0.005 × 5 = 0.025 volt

Display for a 5.00 volt reading on the 10 volt scale shall be 05.00. Now percentage on LSD is 0.02.

Total error = 0.025 + 0.02 = 0.045 volt

(b) When the reading is 0.10 volt on the 10 volt range:

0.005 × 0.10 = 0.0005 volt; ± 2 digits = 0.02 volt
Total error = 0.0005 + 0.02 = 0.0205 volt

(c) % error in (b):

0.0205/0.1 × 100 = 20.5%

Example 5.7

An analog voltage 2.732 is to be measured by successive approximation digital voltmeter of 4 bits. Find the digital output when reference voltage is 5 volts.

Solution

(a) First set d₃ = 1;

output = 5/2¹ = 2.5 volt

Since 2.732 > 2.5, d₃ will be set at 1.

(b) Next set d₂ = 1;

output = 2.5 + 5/2² = 3.75 volt

Since 2.732 < 3.75, d₂ will be set at O.

(c) Next set d₁ = 1;

output = 2.5 + 5/2³ = 3.125 volt

Since 3.125 > 2.732, d₁ will be set at 1.

(d) Next set d₀ = 1;

output = 3.125 + 5/2⁴ = 3.4375 volt

Since 3.4375 > 2.732, d₀ will be set at 1. Thus, digital output = 1011.

Example 5.8

An analog signal of 0 to 10 volt range is able to distinguish a minimum change of 10 millivolt. Find (a) its resolution; (b) to have the same resolution in digital form, what number of bits can be selected for the digital converter of digital voltmeter; (c) quantization error.

Solution

(a) Resolution in analog form:

10 mV/10 V = 1/1000 = 0.001

(b) If the required number of bits is n, then for the same resolution:

1/1000 = 1/2ⁿ, so n = 10 (∴ 2¹⁰ = 1024 ≈ 1000)

(c) Here

C = Signal range/2ⁿ = 10/2¹⁰ = 10/1024 volt ≈ 1 millivolt
Quantization error Cₑ = C/(2√3) = (1 × 10⁻³)/(2 × √3) = 0.29 millivolt

Example 5.9

A digital voltmeter of resolution 0.01% is required to measure an analog signal of 0 to 10 volt at 1 KHz. Find: (a) Minimum practical sampling rate; (b) Number of bits selected for the digital voltmeter; (c) Analog value of LSB (last significant bit); (d) Dynamic range of digital conversion in digital voltmeter; (e) Quantization error; (f) Aperture time.

Solution

(a) As we know, practical sampling frequency must be

2.5 × fₓ = 2.5 × 1 = 2.5 KHz

(b) Since resolution = 0.01% = 0.0001 =

1/10000 = 1/2ⁿ

Hence n = 14 (Since 2¹⁴ = 16,384 i.e. 5 decimal number and we take 2¹³ this will become 8,192 which is less than 10,000).

(c) Analog equivalent of LSB:

(1/2ⁿ) × scale range = (1/2¹⁴) × 10 = 10/16384 = 610.35 μV

(d) Dynamic range of digital conversion of digital voltmeter is given by:

6 × number of bits selected = 6 × 14 = 84

(e) Here

C = Signal range/2ⁿ = 10/2¹⁴ = 610.35 μV

and hence

Cₑ = C/(2√3) = 610.35/(2√3) = 176.2 μV
(Note: we see that C is equivalent to analog value of LSB)

(f) Aperture time:

Tₐ = ΔE/(2π fₘₐₓ E) = 1/(2 × 3.14 × 1000 × 16384) = 9.71 × 10⁻⁹ second
Here ΔE/Eₘₐₓ = 1/2¹⁴ = 1/16384

Example 5.10

An analog voltage of 10 volts is to be measured digitally. Find the minimum number of bits and actual resolution to have an accuracy better than 0.01% and conversion time for a clock frequency of 10 KHz, assuming successive approximation type of DVM.

Solution

(i) Here required accuracy is better than 0.01% = 0.0001. It means if a 10 volt signal is to be measured, a minimum value expected shall be 9.9999 volts.

According to equation of exact range of conversion:

E₀ = E_R[dₙ₋₁ × 2⁻¹ + dₙ₋₂ × 2⁻² + … + d₁ × 2⁻⁽ⁿ⁻¹⁾ + d₀ × 2⁻ⁿ]

where E_R is the analog voltage = 10 in this case and E₀ is the expected output in digital form = 9.9999 in this case. A quick look at the above equation reveals that a value of 9.9999 can be obtained by selecting minimum 16 bits. Hence, n = 16.

(ii) Resolution:

1/2ⁿ = 1/2¹⁶ = 1/65536 ≈ 0.0001

(iii) In case of successive approximation type conversion, aperture time Tₐ is equivalent to conversion time T꜀.

Tₐ = T꜀ = ΔE/(2π fₘₐₓ E) = 1/(2 × 3.14 × 10,000 × 2¹⁶) = 4.1 × 10⁻⁹ second
Since ΔE/Eₘₐₓ = 1/2¹⁶

Example 5.11

An integrating type digital voltmeter has an integrating capacitor of 0.1 μF and a resistance of 100 K. If Eᵢ is 2 volts, and E₀ is not to exceed 10 volts, what is the maximum time up to which Eᵢ can be integrated?

Solution

As we know in case of integrating type digital voltmeter:

E₀ = −Eᵢ × (1/RC) × t

Here Eᵢ = 2 V; E₀ = 10 V; R = 100 KΩ; C = 0.1 μF = 0.1 × 10⁻⁶ F. Putting these values in the above equation:

10 = (2 × t)/(100 × 1000 × 0.1 × 10⁻⁶)

or t = 0.05 second.
