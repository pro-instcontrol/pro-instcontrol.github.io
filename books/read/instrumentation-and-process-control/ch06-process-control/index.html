This chapter presents the modes of process control: open and closed loops, ON-OFF, proportional, integral, derivative, PI and PID actions, integral windup, electronic and pneumatic controller designs, the selection of a control technique, three-element feed-forward control, plus thirteen worked examples and a fourteen-question exercise.

6.1 Introduction

A process consists of several systematic operations, functioning within co-ordinated boundaries, which go step by step to produce a final output. Since a process has to work systematically and in a coherent manner, some sort of control over its different activities is needed. It is needless to mention that an uncontrolled process activity can never deliver fruitful output; rather, it may sometimes result in severe damage to the connected equipment.

The appearance of process control can be visualized very clearly in day-to-day human activity. The various actions of a human being, from rising in the morning even to dreaming, are the greatest examples of a control system. When a person walks with eyes closed, it is understood to be an example of an open-loop control system, because the legs do not move in a co-ordinated way. There is no control over the direction in which they move, and the person may even fall. When the person walks with eyes open, the situation is entirely different: the eyes, together with the brain, keep a constant and co-ordinated watch on the legs. The eyes see obstacles lying ahead on the path well in advance and accordingly send an input signal to the brain. The brain performs a great deal of internal manipulation, in a way that is still little known to us, and finally directs the legs towards the right direction. This type of control mechanism is known as a closed-loop control system.

Feedback, as we call it in control terminology, appears naturally in a closed-loop system. In fact, it is the appearance of feedback that turns an open-loop control system into a closed-loop one. In the example above, the eyes together with the brain create the necessary feedback operation. The eye sends the input signal to the brain, which compares the previous direction of walking and redirects the legs to change direction if required.

Negative feedback is the basic control mechanism for the whole system. It adjusts the direction of walking in such a way that the gap between the earlier direction of walking and the newly assigned direction is minimized. Although other control mechanisms also exist, for example positive feedback and feed forward, these cannot be applied in this example. Those systems have different utilities and will be described at the appropriate places.

In process industries, different processes require different control systems. The need for control is based on obtaining the right product specifications. The required specification can be met by suitably controlling the four basic process variables, namely pressure, temperature, flow and level, in addition to others. These process variables are always in a dynamic state, and any substantial change in them indicates an energy or mass unbalance. Thus the most important function of a process control system is to manipulate the energy or mass balance in such a way that these indicative variables are maintained within specified limits.

An automatic controller is a device that takes the process variable as input and manipulates this process input with respect to set values so as to minimize the deviation automatically. The process variable so controlled is called the controlled variable. The controller operates through another medium, a pneumatic controller through air and an electronic controller through an electrical signal, in order to make the required correction in the process variable; this medium is called the manipulated variable.

The manner in which an automatic controller changes its output with respect to the process input is known as control action. Basically there are four control actions, sometimes called modes of control:

  • (i) On-off control;
  • (ii) Proportional control;
  • (iii) Integral control;
  • (iv) Derivative control.

Each mode of control has its own advantages as well as limitations. An automatic controller can be designed to produce only one of the four actions, or a combination of two or three actions, for the desired process control. Before describing the different controller actions in detail, we shall study the various process characteristics that ultimately govern the choice of a particular type of control action.

The choice of a suitable controller action for a particular process condition is based on various process characteristics. In general, processes exhibit two effects: (i) variations in the controlled variable owing to load changes, and (ii) a delay in the time taken by the process variable to reach a new value when load changes occur, called process lag. Process lag is generally contributed by one or more process behaviours: capacitance, dead time and resistance.

Load in any process is the total requirement of the control agent, fluid or any other material, for the process at any instant of time. Since the final control element regulates the flow of the control agent in order to keep the controlled variable at its set value, load is directly related to the position of the final control element. Therefore, every time load changes, a new position of the final control element is called for, to minimize the deviation of the controlled variable from the set value. Thus the magnitude as well as the rate of change of load are both important factors for a particular control action.

Another problem is that sometimes load changes cannot be recognized easily. For instance:

  • (i) change in ambient conditions;
  • (ii) changes in the process reaction rate;
  • (iii) changes in the composition of the control agent.

In addition to these three, the main form of load change is, however, the change in the requirement for the control agent according to the demand for the final product.

The capacitance of a process is its holding capacity with respect to the flow of the control agent. It may be considered proportional to the retention time of the process. Processes with large capacitance have a greater tendency to maintain the controlled variable at its set value in spite of severe load changes. But the limitation of a high-capacity process, with its long retention time, is that the controlled variable cannot easily achieve a new value. This is because higher capacitance introduces a time lag between the moment a change is initiated in the control agent and the moment the controlled variable reflects that change. A new term now appears: the process reaction rate. The effect of the corrective action brought about by a control action to keep the controlled variable at its set value depends on the process reaction rate.

Resistance, defined as the natural opposition to flow, is the second basic characteristic of a process. If a material is being heated in a process with higher thermal resistance, more control agent is required to bring about a substantial temperature change in the material. On the other hand, this requirement falls drastically in a process with lower thermal resistance.

Some processes have a multi-capacitance system, for example a temperature measurement system in which there is a thermo-nozzle, then a thermo-well and finally the temperature element. Heat energy flows from one capacitance to another, forming a multi-capacity measurement system. The time lag in the response created by such a multi-capacitance measurement system is called transfer lag. The area most affected by transfer lag is the temperature control system. In fact this limits the very rate at which heat is transferred to the control agent to effect control of the controlled variable. This generates an overshoot around the set value, because the transfer of heat to the control agent is not sensed immediately and consequently no change can be initiated by the control action.

There is another kind of lag, called dead time, transport lag or distance-velocity lag. It is created by an instrument-installation problem. If a final control element has been installed at an appreciable distance from the measurement system, or the measurement system has been installed away from the actual process, this type of lag results. It creates a delay in the response of the measurement system, which ultimately results in a delay in the action of the final control element.

From the above discussion it is clear that dead time, or transport lag, is the difficult element as far as control is concerned. It is really troublesome for a control loop to anticipate the delay and decide on corrective action. Capacitance, on the other hand, is not a difficult element; it creates self-regulation, which ultimately acts towards restoring the equilibrium condition.

6.1.1 ON-OFF Control Action

An ON-OFF control action is necessarily a two-position control. This means the controller output is at a maximum for one set of conditions and at a minimum for another set of conditions, with no intermediate position of control at all. Suppose the set value for this kind of control action is 60%, the maximum control point for one set of conditions is 65% and the minimum control point is 55%. Under these conditions the controller output takes the two positions mentioned above and continues to move between them. The control band is therefore 10%, or ± 5% with respect to the 60% set value.

The defect of this kind of control action is that the cycle of variation between 65% and 55% continues indefinitely, because this control action cannot balance supply against load in the control system. Even so, this mode of control is very popular for control services where ON-OFF action is sufficient, merely to keep the process system under control. For example:

  • control of a domestic heating system;
  • temperature control of a refrigeration system;
  • level control of an overhead water tank.

All three of these systems, as well as other similar systems, are required to have an ON-OFF operating switch initiated by a thermostatic system or by a field-mounted level switch of the mercury type.

In normal practical operation, the controller output signals ON when the measurement input is below the set value and triggers OFF when the input is at its highest with respect to the set value. But a narrow gap, which appears as an error, always exists between these two changes that start the actual operation. This characteristic is advantageous because it minimizes the cycling effect. Cycling causes further upsets in process operation and also contributes to wear in the internals of control valves. The cycle time of controller action depends on process dead time. Process dead time determines the reversal time of the measurement input signal as it crosses the set value. Large-capacity processes have a long time constant, resulting in a slow change in the measurement input signal. This ultimately makes a narrow band around the set value; if the cycle is slow and systematic, the control action is satisfactory. The control action is indicated in Figure 6.1.

ON-OFF control is at its best in performance when the proportional band is less than 1%, that is, at maximum gain, with a slow process reaction rate, low dead time and low transfer lag. However, if the change in the process, reflected in the measurement input signal, is more sensitive to fluctuation in supply, the amplitude as well as the frequency of the cycle start to increase. This ultimately calls for a comparatively more improved version of control action, which will be discussed a little later.

The electrical implementation of ON-OFF control action is shown in Figure 6.2.

As shown in the diagram, when the thermostatic switch TS closes at a particular temperature, set according to the requirement of the process, relay R1 is energized. The normally open (NO) contact 1R1 of relay R1 closes and energizes another relay R2, whose normally open contact 1R2 closes and sends supply for the ON-OFF action of the process operation, cutting off the heating system. When the system cools to a specified temperature, heating starts once again.

The pneumatic implementation of ON-OFF control action is shown in Figure 6.3.

Remember. The 60% set value with control points at 65% and 55% gives a 10% control band, that is ± 5%. ON-OFF control performs best with a proportional band below 1%; the pneumatic supply in Figure 6.3 is 1.4 kg/cm².

6.2 Proportional control

Proportional control is also known as throttling control or modulating control. In this mode a continuous linear relationship exists between the value of the controlled variable and the output of the controller: the output changes in the same way as the indicated deviation between the set value and the measured value. The action is proportional to the amount of deviation only and has no effect due to the rate of change of deviation. Hence no change in controller output can be expected without a substantial amount of deviation.

The necessary change in the measured or controlled variable needed to effect a full change in controller output is known as the proportional band (PB); it is also termed the modulating range or throttling range. PB is expressed as a percentage of the full range and can vary from less than one per cent to more than 200 per cent.

Another way of representing the throttling range is controller gain (CG) or sensitivity, which are reciprocals of the proportional band. Gain indicates the ratio of controller output to its input; for instance, a 50 per cent proportional band corresponds to a gain of 2. The adjustment inside the controller can be designed and calibrated in terms of gain or proportional band.

Any change in the controlled variable requires a change in controller output to maintain the variable at the set value. But a controller with proportional action needs a change in deviation to generate output, so it produces corrective action for a particular load only; for all other loads some deviation must remain, which is called offset. The presence of offset is characteristic of proportional action and can be eliminated only by manually readjusting the set value to bring the controlled variable back to its actual value. That readjustment can be done only by the operator, and only where load changes are not very frequent; if load changes are frequent it is not practically possible to readjust manually.

The criteria used to evaluate a control action are:

  1. Minimum deviation following a disturbance
  2. Minimum time interval before returning to the set value
  3. Minimum offset due to changes in load

These criteria accord with the requirement of a controller response with quarter amplitude decay, as shown in Figure 6.4. After quarter amplitude decay the controlled variable should reach the set value (steady-state value) without fail.

In this respect, however, proportional control action with a higher gain produces over-correction of the controlled variable around the set value. The resulting movement of the process pointer (which indicates the controlled variable) around the set pointer (which indicates the set value) is an oscillation around the set value, as shown in Figure 6.5.

If, on the other hand, the gain is reduced to minimize the cyclic movement, the response becomes sluggish, which is not at all desirable because fast response is very important in any process control system. Considering both extremes, a compromise in the selection of gain for optimum control is called for. A gain setting that results in a few fast subsiding cycles is the best choice. Obviously, such a gain setting depends on the magnitude of load change and also on the time taken by the control system to correct the deviation. In a case where the response of the process is faster, a small gain is the best choice.

Proportional control is at its best when the capacitance of the process is large, the process reaction rate is slow and the lag (process lag and transport lag) is small. These characteristics bring stability faster and need higher gain, ultimately leading to faster corrective action. When the process characteristics are different, a lower gain is selected even if it creates a substantial amount of offset on load changes. High gain, however, results in cycling if the response of the process is faster. This condition worsens if a dead-time element is present, because the correction for overshoot takes more time. Therefore it can be concluded that proportional control action should be used in processes having a large time constant and small dead time, the magnitude of load changes being small even if the speed of load changes is fast.

Output and input of a proportional controller are related according to the expression:

(6.1)E₀ = (100/P_b)·E_s + B

where P_b is the proportional band in percentage, E_s is the amount of deviation or error, B is the bias output setting and E₀ is the output.

If the deviation or error signal happens to be zero, the output of the controller equals the value of the bias setting. Here it is worth mentioning that controller gain consists of two parts, that is, steady-state gain (say K_g) and dynamic gain (say g_d). Steady-state gain is scalar whereas dynamic gain is a vector. Since a proportional controller does not have a dynamic element, its dynamic gain can be taken as 1.0 with zero degree phase angle. The phase shift of 180° which takes place in a negative feedback system comes up in the dead-time element only in the case of a proportional controller. Therefore a process having a dead time of t shall cycle with a period of 2t.

The deviation that appears due to a load change and continues to sustain is given by:

(6.2)E_s = ((E₀ − B)·P_b)/100

This in fact appears as an offset, which will go on increasing as the proportional band is increased.

Implementation of proportional action is shown electronically in Figure 6.6.

In this implementation, the proportional band is determined by the ratio of C_in to C_fb. This ratio is affected by the operational amplifier (OA) gain as well. When the effective gain ratio is 1, then the proportional band becomes 100 per cent. The adjustment in proportional band is effected by the proportional band adjustment potentiometer.

The equivalent implementation in its pneumatic form has been shown in Figure 6.7.

As is observed in Figure 6.7, the flapper is not fixed (as was the case in ON-OFF control action) but is in a floating state between the spring (having K_p as the spring constant) and the proportional bellows. The proportional bellows, which is also called the feedback bellows, and the spring together are acted upon by the output pressure for restoration of balance between the nozzle and the flapper system. In this way the force exerted by the expansion of the bellows due to the application of output pressure is equal to the force exerted by the spring. The ratio of flapper length above the nozzle position to that below the nozzle position is important in this arrangement; this ratio in fact decides the amount of proportional gain and can be adjusted by varying that ratio.

Proportional action is the basic controller action in the field of continuous process control.

6.3 Integral control

Integral control action is also known as proportional speed floating control. In this control action the controller output changes its value at a rate proportional to the deviation. The higher the deviation, the faster the change in controller output, and hence as long as the deviation persists the change in output continues. The action of the controller responds to the amount as well as the duration of the deviation; in this way the control action is unique. The application of correction continues until it produces the exact amount of correction for any load changes. Since the rate of correction increases according to the amount of load change, the action is able to cope with faster load changes even when self-regulation is considerably less. Naturally it is suitable for a process with a small time constant and small dead time, and it can also handle large load changes.

Implementation of integral action can be done electronically as indicated in Figure 6.8. A potential divider has been set across the error input signal Ein and a variable resistance unit Rv connected in parallel with Cin works as the integral adjustment unit. Whenever an error input signal Ein is present, the potential divider along with Rv provides a path for the flow of current. The continuity of this current depends upon the differential voltage available due to the difference between the process variable and the set value. The feedback current, in this way, continues to discharge through Cfb as long as an error input signal Ein is present. The amplifier provides a continuous variation in the output as long as the signal input is present, and this provides integral action. If at any time the error signal input Ein becomes zero, the input current stops flowing. The input current, and hence the integral action, can be varied by changing the value of Rv; the output current is required to increase at a faster rate so that a matching change can be observed across Cfb, resulting in faster integral action. To produce a slow integral action, the potential divider has been included in the input circuit. It will become clear later, when integral action is used in conjunction with proportional action, that the value of the integral resistance determines the number of times the proportional action is repeated per minute. The integral action has been indicated on the graphical diagram Figure 6.9.

Output of this control action is expressed by the expression:

(6.3)E_o = (1/T_i) ∫ E_s dt + B

where Ti is the integral time.

Implementation of integral control in its pneumatic form can be done by adding an integral restriction and bellows combination unit against the proportional bellows (refer to Figure 6.7), as shown in Figure 6.10.

The most important characteristic of the integral action is that it eliminates the offset caused by applying proportional action alone.

6.4 Derivative control

This is also known as differential control or rate control. In this control action, the output of the control action is proportional to the rate of change of process variable. This control action tries to oppose the change in process variable and therefore, have good stabilizing effect on the control system but have no effect on the error caused by the offset.

The derivative action can be expressed by derivative time (in minutes) being the time in minutes by which a derivative action advances the effect of proportional action. It is a type of anticipatory control action working as preventive action (booster action). Speed of correction shall be dependent upon how fast the process variable drifts from its set value. It measures the rate of change of deviation between process variable and the set value and predicts the large overshoot if any before it can actually occur and apply correction to limit it. This reduces the unnecessary oscillations that may otherwise arise out of process disturbances.

To make the derivative action physically effective, either the process variable input signal or the output signal going to the final control element is enhanced in proportion to the rate of change of actual signal. The effect is equivalent to temporary increase in gain, which in effect improves the control in such a way that a sluggish control action also becomes sufficiently rapid. The inherent effect of acceleration in the signal is important for long time constant process having low response initially. The dead time effect is similarly counteracted by the derivative action, which brings further reduction in the period of the cycling. Therefore, on a sudden load change, the process variable, i.e. controlled variable is stabilized around the set value quickly. Adjustment of derivative time is dependent upon the amount of process lag, which ultimately will change the level of response.

Initially when the process start-up takes place, large correction brought about by the characteristic derivative action brings the process variable near the set value quickly and thus reduces overshoot as well as cycling. The derivative response has been shown in Figure 6.11.

Response of derivative control action is governed by the following expression:

(6.4)E₀ = T_d · dE_s/dt + B

where T_d is the derivative time.

Electronic implementation of derivative action has been shown in Figure 6.12.

Here proportional action has also been included because integral as well as derivative actions do not exist independently. These are always combined with proportional action separately for making them useful in a control loop.

As is observed from the diagram, the output is passing through derivative capacitor Cd and only a portion of the total output is fed through feedback capacitor due to presence of potential divider in the output circuit. The effect is a decrease in proportional band, which ultimately results in greater change in output for a given error signal input. This status continues till the derivative capacitor charges fully. The time elapsed to charge the Cd is related to its time constant, which can be varied by the help of variable resistance Rd, which is used for adjustment of derivative time. The effect of potential divider ceases when the Cd gets fully charged. The proportional band is then restored to its original value.

Pneumatic implementation of derivative action has been shown in Figure 6.13. As it can be observed from the figure, a small restriction in the output line going to proportional bellows creates derivative action pneumatically. Here also the derivative action has been shown along with proportional action, which makes the system complete. The air supply is shown as 1.4 kg/cm² in the diagram.

The most important characteristic of the derivative action is that it changes the gain temporarily, which stabilizes the control loop quickly when load changes occur.

6.5 Proportional-cum-Integral Control (PI Control)

It has already been shown that integral control can eliminate the offset created by proportional control acting alone. A combination of proportional and integral control action can therefore provide exact correction in practical control loops for all load conditions. Here the integral rate, expressed in repeats per minute, is actually the number of times per minute that the output produced by proportional action is repeated by the integral action. Its effect is to go on adding correction to the process variable for as long as the deviation exists. In effect this is the same as gradually shifting the set-value adjustment, forcing the control action to follow until the previous set value is reached. On a load change the immediate response comes from proportional action, which is then increased at a fixed rate by the integral action. This can sometimes cause prolonged cycling if the load change is rapid.

PI control action is suitable for processes having a small time constant and small to medium dead times. For the control of process variables such as flow, level or pressure, PI control is applied almost invariably. When the process variable is returned to the original set value, the integral action ceases immediately.

The PI mode of control is the most versatile mode of control action, capable of handling most combinations of process characteristics quite satisfactorily. The process characteristics together with the most required practical gain settings for flow, level and pressure control loops are given below:

  • Flow control loop: capacitance = negligible; transfer lag = small; dead time = small; gain setting = 0.2 to 10.
  • Level control loop: capacitance = small or medium single capacitance; transfer lag = small or medium; dead time = small; gain setting = 2 to 10.
  • Pressure control loop: capacitance = small or medium single capacitance; transfer = very small; dead time = small; gain setting = 5 to 50 for liquid pressure control; gain setting = 20 to 50 for gas pressure control.

As far as the integral rate setting is concerned, a small capacitance allows a higher integral rate, while transfer lag and dead time require a lower integral rate. A higher integral rate counteracts the offset more quickly, whereas a lower integral rate avoids excessive cycling of the process variable. Figure 6.14 shows a detailed graph of PI control action.

The limitation of PI control is a large period of cycling and hence a slow response with appreciable dead time. If the measuring device is sensitive, transfer lag is not a limitation; if, on the other hand, the measuring device is sluggish, transfer lag is converted into dead time.

Figure 6.15 shows the electronic implementation of PI control, which is actually a slight modification of the diagram given in Figure 6.8. Rp and Ri are the proportional band and integral rate adjustments respectively. The proportional band introduces negative feedback to stabilize the system, while the integral rate provides a delayed positive feedback to compensate the effect of the negative feedback and reduces the proportional band in order to remove the offset.

The pneumatic implementation of PI action was shown in Figure 6.10 during the earlier discussion. In practice the restriction for integral action shown there is provided by a small needle valve, whose stem is used for rate adjustment in the pneumatic implementation.

6.6 Proportional-cum-Integral-cum-Derivative (PID) Control

As the name (PID) suggests, the output of a PID controller depends on the magnitude, the duration and the rate of change of the deviation between the process variable (controlled variable) and the set value. This combination of control actions carries the compensation effect needed for a large time constant with an initially slow response, such as is found in temperature control systems. The combination brings stability in addition to removing the offset caused by proportional action alone. It can handle slow and large load changes very effectively, and also applications in which the time constant is small, the dead time is large, and the load changes are large and fast. In short, this combination can handle any difficult load.

In fact, derivative action first provides a large correction as soon as a deviation occurs, which moves the final control element further than is actually required and applies more correction than is needed. The controller therefore starts working to remove the effect of that large initial correction, leaving only the correction provided by the PI response to determine the final position of the final control element. The early and extra change of the process input tends to counteract the unwanted effect of process lags; it is a temporary extra correction proportional to the amount of deviation.

Figure 6.16 shows the schematic of the electronic implementation of the PID controller. The gain of the controller is regulated by means of the proportional-band adjustment potentiometer, which changes the amount of negative feedback according to the requirements of the process characteristics. Integral-rate adjustment provides positive feedback for the elimination of offset in the event of load changes. Derivative adjustment provides negative feedback for perfect system stability.

The diagram in Figure 6.17 shows the pneumatic implementation of the PID controller. As mentioned earlier, the restrictions — both derivative and integral — are created by installing specially designed needle valves. These needle valves have small adjustable stems, which are throttled in the lines to adjust the derivative and integral actions.

In the normal case, a load change deviates the process variable from its set value, producing an error signal. The controller actions then attempt to return the process variable to the original set value. In an ideal situation a system disturbance should trigger an immediate response to produce tight control, but in practice the situation is quite different. Because of typical process characteristics such as dead time, time constant and damping, tight control is impossible. The error developed initially works to reduce the deviation of the process variable from its set value. The correction applied by the final control element, however, causes the process variable to overshoot the set value, thereby reversing the error. The system then starts correcting this new error, and the process variable overshoots the set value once again, reversing the error mechanism again. This continues to create oscillations in the process variable about the set value. If the amplitude of these oscillations dies down with time, a stable condition is eventually achieved; if, on the other hand, the amplitude is somehow maintained, no stable condition is reached. These two extreme situations of the stability criterion are known as the steady-state response condition and the transient response condition respectively.

The type of response that a particular system generates to load changes forms the criterion of quality of control. The control problems encountered in the process industries are generally those that require complete stability against process upsets followed by a rapid response under specified operating conditions. This controller action (PID) is most suitable for temperature control problems. The characteristics of the process and the gain setting are described below:

  • Capacitance = two or more (or large)
  • Transfer lag = medium or large
  • Dead time = small or large
  • Gain setting = 1 to 10

6.7 Integral Windup

Integral windup is a typical characteristic found in any controller unit that has integral mode in action in addition to another mode. The basic behaviour of integral mode is to continue changing its output as long as there is a deviation between the process variable and the set value. The controller applies the correction in its own way and goes on opening the final control element. But if there is no supply of process material in sufficient quantity, or the supply is completely cut off because an isolation valve has been closed or a pump has failed, the corrective action generated by the control action may not be effective. The controller output, however, continues to increase until it reaches a state of saturation. Such a state is called integral windup.

The difficulty caused by integral windup is experienced when the closed isolation valve in the line suddenly opens, or the failed pump suddenly starts. Under this condition, when the supply to the process resumes, the normal requirement is that controller operation should also resume. But because of the integral windup condition prevailing in the controller, it will not return to normal operation in time even if the deviation between the process variable and the set value has returned to zero. This situation continues until the saturation state vanishes.

To eliminate this situation in practice, the controller is equipped with a limiter circuit that prevents the output from crossing the saturation limit. The limiter circuit consists of low and high limit selector switches combined with a high-gain amplifier. The high selector switch, in combination with the amplifier, prevents the output from entering the control loop when the output tends to increase towards windup. In that condition a bias signal, given by the high selector switch, is allowed to enter the loop. In normal operation the high limit switch is not in action; rather the lower limit switch is in operation and allows the normal output to enter the control loop. The arrangement is very simple and quite effective.

6.8 Electronic controller

An electronic controller is an electronic instrument consisting of the following components: (i) an error detector unit, (ii) an amplifier unit and (iii) an RC network circuit.

It takes the process input signal as a 4 to 20 mA DC signal from field-mounted transmitters and provides a control output, also a 4 to 20 mA DC signal, which ultimately operates the final control element located in the field. The output range is capable of driving a final control element having an impedance of up to 1500 ohms.

The signal that initiates controller operation is the error signal (the difference between the process input variable signal and the set value), which is generated in an error detector unit as shown in Figure 6.19. The error detector consists of a precision input resistor (62.5 ohms ± 0.1%) and a precision potentiometer used as the set-value adjustment unit. The power supply unit supplies a standard voltage of 1.25 V DC to the set-value adjusting potentiometer, as shown. The input signal current is converted into a voltage signal (0.25 V to 1.25 V DC) by the precision resistor. The set-value voltage and the process-variable voltage generate the error voltage. The set value is varied by adjusting the potentiometer. The error voltage (−1 V to +1 V) is applied to the amplifier.

The error voltage is negative or positive depending on whether the process-variable input signal is above or below the set-value signal. Typically, when the two signals are equal, the two pointers coincide and there is no error signal. The amplifier unit raises this signal to a level sufficient to operate the final control element. The RC network circuit enables the amplifier unit to create the various forms of control response required by the loop. Having variable time constants, the RC network produces different control actions by changing the feedback. Negative feedback reduces the gain of the system and linearises the system; this helps to increase stability but at the same time decreases the sensitivity of the system.

The operational amplifier circuit shown in Figure 6.20 is the basic circuit for electronic controller actions. It consists of an input network and a feedback network. The input-network current charges the input capacitor Cin, while the feedback current charges the feedback capacitor Cfb. When both capacitors are equally charged, no current flows. A slight input floating current, insignificant in value, keeps the amplifier alive. In this condition, even a slight increase in the input signal current charges Cin and produces a feedback current that begins to charge Cfb. This means the feedback current constantly tracks the error signal input current, so the reaction of the amplifier circuit is almost instantaneous. Since the two currents remain more or less equal, the charges on the capacitors stay the same and therefore the junction point of the two capacitors almost always remains at ground potential.

6.9 Pneumatic controller

Looking back historically at controllers, it is surprising to conclude that a diaphragm-actuated valve, dating from the nineteenth century, was the first device in this line to be regarded as the closest analogue of a controller (in effect an open-loop controller). Later called the 'blind controller', it was essentially a field-mounted, compact object that performed both the measurement (in one way) and the control of the process. In the twentieth century, pneumatic transmitters appeared, making it possible to move the entire controller physically from the field mounting station to a centrally located control station for remote operation. This century saw many facets of development in the pneumatic controller field, finally settling on a high level of miniature controller (front size 6″ × 3″), which is still effective in some older processing plants. Nowadays, however, the electronic controller is taking the place of the pneumatic controller, which is being slowly phased out; a day may come when it becomes a matter of history only.

Figure 6.21 shows the very basic design of an old model of pneumatic controller. In this model there are four bellows arranged as shown. The field-mounted transmitter sends the process-variable signal input (in the form of 0.2 to 1.0 kg/cm²) to the process-variable (PV) bellows. A set-value regulation unit sends the set-value signal to the set-value (SV) bellows. These two signals are compared, and a corrective action in the form of a pneumatic output signal is generated by the flapper and the booster relay-cum-nozzle assembly. On its way to operate the final control element, the output signal is also sent to the reset-action (RA) bellows through the reset restrictor unit, as shown in the diagram. This output signal is sent to the feedback (FB) bellows as well, through the derivative unit. In later controllers of comparatively improved design, the derivative units were placed in the measurement input line; that form is discussed a little later.

Achieving derivative action through the output system presents some obvious limitations. In this approach, the derivative action interacts with the proportional and reset responses and actually follows the proportional response. Hence it is not capable of preventing overshoot during start-up or during large disturbances.

The block diagram in Figure 6.22 shows the design of a miniature pneumatic controller, the most modern design of its time in the pneumatic controller line. In general, this design has altogether six functional components assembled in one housing, arranged for flush mounting on a central control panel. The main unit with the twin-indicator arrangement (one indicator for the set value and the other for the process variable) in fact houses all six components, which are shown scattered in the diagram.

The set-value mechanism fixes the set value (indicated on the twin-indicator system) by means of an adjusting knob located outside the controller unit, for ease of operation by the operator. The process-variable input comes directly from the field-mounted transmitter installed in the process. The derivative unit adds to the process-variable signal before it enters the control module. As can be seen, in this design the derivative units have been added in the process-variable input line and not in the control output line.

The control module is the heart of this controller, with a combination of four working bellows (essential for any automatic pneumatic controller design), as shown in Figure 6.23. Four bellows dynamically bear upon a floating disc, which keeps them in equilibrium with respect to the fulcrum and also acts as the flapper of the system. Adjustment of the proportional band is carried out through the fulcrum bar. The combined controller action generates the output, which is fed to the final control element installed near the process.

  • FB – feedback bellows.
  • PV – process variable bellows.
  • SV – set value bellows.
  • RA – reset action bellows.

The manual loading station regulates and provides a manual output signal for operating the final control element if the automatic signal from the control module fails because of a fault in the module itself, or when the controller is intentionally operated manually to carry out maintenance on the field-mounted transmitter. The manual loading station also performs change-over by switching between manual and automatic control. The auto-balance unit is a floating controller that tracks the available output signal (from either the control module or the manual loading station) and generates the other one (the one not available), so that a bumpless transfer can take place between the manual and automatic options. If the controller is on automatic, the auto-balance unit positions the manual output so that when the controller is switched to manual, the output goes straight to the final control element. The reverse case is similar.

Finally there is a small output indicator, which in fact indicates the output signal going to the final control element but can be interpreted as the actual opening position of the final control element. All the components of the pneumatic controllers described above are easily replaceable for maintenance work; this is their greatest advantage.

6.9.1 Comparison between electronic and pneumatic controllers

Nowadays, the demand for better and more improved control techniques has grown strongly, aiming at higher productivity, lower production cost, improved and more uniform product quality, and greater security and safety for operating personnel. These goals have become easily attainable thanks to the introduction of the electronic controller, which provides better control than its pneumatic counterpart. The superiority of the electronic controller over the pneumatic one can be compared on the following characteristics:

  1. Reliability. Electronic controllers are made of semiconductor devices whose life is longer even than the equipment they control. The absence of mechanical parts means there is no failure due to mechanical causes such as fatigue, inertia of moving parts and similar problems. Based on reliability studies and on working experience with semiconductor equipment, such devices are liable to fail only during the initial operating period of test loading; once that critical period is over, extremely high reliability is guaranteed. The pneumatic controller, on the other hand, needs more maintenance and is therefore relatively inefficient. The possibility of leakage in the air system creates a larger problem in the control action during transient conditions, so serious that the control may not be able to reach a steady-state condition.
  2. Speed of response. Since there are no moving parts, the delays caused by inertia and other mechanical characteristics between the command signal and the corrective action are usually insignificant. By careful design and proper selection of components, the total delay has been reduced to only a few microseconds. In the best-designed pneumatic controllers such delays are in milliseconds.
  3. Cost. With ever-improving electronics technology, the reduction in cost is considerable and will go further down in the near future. For larger process plants there is a further reduction in the installation cost of electronic systems. Pneumatic controllers are cheaper than electronic controllers, but in the long run electronic controllers may prove cheaper as more and more replacements are needed.
  4. Power consumption. The power consumption of an electronic controller is considerably lower and uniform, requiring no special or extra arrangement; maintenance of the power supply system likewise needs no extra provision. Pneumatic controllers, however, require a complete instrument air supply system comprising an instrument air compressor (preferably a reciprocating type with capacity control), a silica gel drying system (with automatic regeneration of the silica gel) and other connected equipment and controls, in addition to an extensive piping system to make instrument air available at every corner. Extra power is therefore needed to run the compressor continuously and for the other equipment.
  5. Accuracy. Electronic controllers have an accuracy of ± 0.25%, whereas pneumatic controllers of the best design have an accuracy of only ± 0.5%.
  6. Maintainability. This characteristic is more or less equally available in both controller types. With the modular concept of construction, plug-in assemblies and sub-assemblies are fixed in easily and taken out easily for maintenance. In the modular design concept, even replacement is possible without disturbing process operation.
  7. Packaging. The design criteria for equipment packaging have improved considerably in recent years. Large instrument cases have been reduced and sizes have become miniature or sub-miniature to house modular boards. The density of instruments on the control panel has likewise been reduced, so that more space becomes available for other equipment that can provide still more information about the process condition. This characteristic is also common to both controller types.
  8. Safety consideration. Pneumatic controllers are normally safer in the potentially explosive atmosphere prevailing in most process industries. An electronic controller may not be suitable for an explosive area; nowadays, however, this problem has been completely solved by providing an electronic safety barrier that isolates the explosive area from the safe area.
  9. Computer compatibility. Electronic controllers have excellent computer compatibility. Only an analog-to-digital conversion system is needed for the analog signal, and the converter output can be fed directly to the computer for further processing, for example supervisory control and data acquisition. A pneumatic controller first needs a pneumatic-to-electric converter that converts the 0.2 to 1.0 kg/cm² pneumatic signal into the equivalent electrical signal of 4 to 20 mA DC. This electrical signal is then converted into digital form to make it computer compatible. Computer compatibility leads to more advanced forms of control system, such as adaptive control or optimal control.

6.10 Selection of control technique

Selecting a suitable yet economical control technique is the most difficult decision to be made for an automatic process control system. A compromise is called for between the expected control quality and the economy of the selected control system. The control system chosen should meet the process design tolerance, and the control refinement should not be more than just required, otherwise the cost becomes unnecessarily excessive.

In general, the expenditure incurred for a control system is evaluated in terms of what it is required to accomplish. Sometimes the quality of control needed is sacrificed to minimise the initial cost; this is not at all desirable because in the long run it often leads to a more costly proposition owing to increased maintenance and loss of production time. In selecting a particular control technique, two prime factors must be considered without bias: loss of production time and maintenance cost. These are more critical than the initial cost.

In general, the expected life of a control system is more than ten years, so the significance of the initial cost is very small. Another point is that if we analyse the situation and the working environment inside a process plant, a sophisticated or complex control technique may not increase the efficiency at all. Such techniques are usually advantageous only when high-grade skilled operators and high-grade maintenance personnel are available in the plant. Therefore the experience and sincerity level of such personnel must be examined before selecting a higher-order control technique for the plant.

Each mode of control can be made suitable for processes having certain typical combinations of basic characteristics. It should be kept in mind that the simplest mode of control that can serve the purpose is the best choice without any prejudice. As far as economy and the best possible results are concerned, applying a comparatively complicated control ultimately results in higher cost without delivering a satisfactory result, and may sometimes even contribute to undesirable characteristics of the process and to product rejection.

In careful process control design, when a narrow proportional band is selected, integral action is generally not required at all. Under this condition the control system applies large corrections for deviations and offset does not exist even with load changes. As far as derivative action is concerned, its application reduces long lags. Therefore, for controlling rapidly changing process variables such as flow or pressure, derivative action may actually result in increased error instead of making a correction. When flow is measured by differential-pressure instruments, its response to rapidly changing flow (for example pulsating flow from reciprocating pumps or compressors) is too fast and this response is quickly transmitted to the controller. Derivative action, if present, accelerates the corrective action in response to the flow pulsations, resulting in rapid opening and closing of the final control element (fluctuation of the control valve) and its eventual failure.

Considerable industrial and design experience in the field can be very helpful when selecting a particular mode of control for a process variable. Proportional control is selected for a process having a fast or medium reaction rate and small or moderate dead time or transfer lag, where load changes in particular are small and slow. Pressure and liquid level, being single-capacitance processes, generally fall under this control mode. Where load changes are considerably slow and the process has negligible capacitance, small transfer lag and dead time, proportional-plus-integral control is the best solution. Flow control loops come under this category. Finally, proportional-plus-integral-plus-derivative (PID) control is best suited to a process having two or more capacitances with large transfer lag as well as dead time, when load changes are quite large and very frequent. The well-known temperature control system falls under this category.

As seen in the discussion, pressure and level control loops use proportional control where load changes are both small and slow, but many processes are going through comparatively large load changes at a faster rate. Such loops essentially need proportional-plus-integral control and thus eliminate the offset caused.

6.10.1 Automatic process control system

The required function of an automatic process control system is to channelise the driving forces available in the process itself so that stable operation of the unit can be assured (the end result being essentially the process variable maintained at the desired level). As we understand it, an automatic control system is essentially a feedback closed-loop system in which the input energy to the system is required to be some function of the output. The required elements in a typical feedback control system are shown in Figure 6.24.

In this block diagram, the error-detecting element together with the control amplifier is in fact the controller unit. Similarly, the primary sensing element together with the transmitter can be called the measurement means. The process is the most important part of the entire system because it is required to perform certain desired and well-defined functions. In fact, it is the addition of energy that causes the process to react the way it does, and as a result the desired operation takes place silently.

The measurement unit consists of a primary sensor element along with a signal transmitter unit. The combined unit of sensor and transmitter detects the change in the process variable to be controlled, for example temperature, pressure, level, flow, etc., and produces a proportional electrical output in the range of 4 to 20 mA D.C. The sensor and transmitter unit is installed close to the process in a harsh atmosphere and the controller is installed inside the central control room. The process variable signal is transmitted all the way to the control room by suitable cabling. The controller has an in-built process indication unit, which indicates the value of the process variable.

As observed in the diagram, the controller unit consists of an error detector and an amplifier unit. The error detector compares the value of the process variable with the set value set by the operator and an error signal is produced, proportional to the difference between the process value and the set value. The amplifier unit amplifies this error signal up to a value where it is capable of actuating a final control element. The controller output after amplification is in the range of 4 to 20 mA D.C., which operates an electro-pneumatic valve positioner, which in turn operates the final control element.

The final control element, again installed near the process in the field, manipulates its opening to bring the process variable back to its original set value.

To understand the operation of a typical feedback control system, let us assume that at a particular instant the process system is under normal condition and the value of the process variable equals exactly the set value. Now, if a load change occurs for any reason, the normal control condition gets disturbed and the process variable changes its value, resulting in an error signal. The error, after detection by the error detector, is fed to the amplifier unit, which produces the controller output. This output operates the final control element to bring the process variable back to its set value. This operation continues uninterruptedly till the error exists and the process variable comes back to the set value. It is to be noted that the controller comes into action only when there is an error. It is incapable of predicting the future action of the control system. The purpose of this control system is to minimise the error between the process variable and the set value as quickly as possible with a minimum amount of cycling, that is fluctuation.

6.11 Three-element control (concept of feed forward)

A three-element control system is a special case of a multi-element control system where more than one process variable influences the control strategy. This can also be called a cascaded feed-forward-feedback control system. The system, in fact, incorporates two independent controllers or controller files connected together in a cascade fashion. One of the two controllers is known as the primary or master controller and the other as the secondary or slave controller. The system has a feed-forward loop where the load change is fed forward as the primary index to change the main process variable so as to control the process. It also has two feedback loops, which monitor the accuracy of the feed-forward loop, because the effectiveness of a feed-forward loop depends completely on the accuracy of measurement of the load change as well as of the affected process variable.

A pure feedback loop also has its own limitations, which are as follows:

  1. A measurable amount of error between the process variable and the set value must be present at all times to initiate a restoring action, hence a perfect control cannot be achieved. This is the nature of a feedback system.
  2. A feedback loop solves its control problem by trial and error because it goes on changing the output until the value of the process variable and the set value are in agreement. It is not possible for a feedback controller to know its output value for any given set of process conditions.
  3. All feedback control loops have a characteristic natural period. Should a disturbance start at an interval less than about three periods, no steady-state condition can ever be obtained.

In a cascaded control system, the final control element is operated through an intermediate controlled variable, the actual value of which depends upon the value of the primary process variable. The output of the primary controller is used to manipulate the set value of the secondary controller. The primary controller has an independent set value. Both controllers have measurement or process variable inputs, but it is only the secondary controller which provides the output for the operation of the final control element.

The cascaded control system enjoys many advantages, which are as follows:

  1. Disturbances created within the secondary loop, operated by the secondary controller, are very well corrected by the secondary controller itself before they tend to influence the primary process variable.
  2. The secondary loop efficiently reduces the phase lag that exists in the secondary part of the process, resulting in a marked improvement in the speed of response of the primary control loop.
  3. Variations in gain existing in the secondary part of the process are overcome within its own loop.
  4. The secondary control loop allows almost always an exact manipulation of the secondary variable by the primary controller.

In feed-forward control the main factors influencing the process conditions are measured and, with respect to the set value, are used to calculate the exact output to meet the exact condition. Whenever a disturbance is initiated, corrective action starts almost immediately in order to cancel it before it affects the primary process variable. In fact, feed forward is capable of perfect control; notwithstanding the difficulty of the process, its performance can be greatly affected by the accuracy of measurement variables. While in operation, a feed-forward control system continuously makes the balance between the raw inputs to the process and the load variations. Therefore, the measurements of process variables required by the control system are necessarily related to material and energy balance equations, and the main process variables are regulated accordingly.

It is an inherent characteristic of any process system that some material and energy are inevitably stored within the process, and the content varies in passing from one phase to another. This variation means a momentary release or absorption of material or energy, which ultimately produces a transient effect in the main process variable unless it is suitably accounted for in the material and energy balance calculation affecting the control loop. Normally, a load change is followed by a period of dynamic imbalance and appears as transient error. Before increasing the load on the process, which ultimately requires an increase in the rate of material and energy transfer, the existing level of material and energy must first be raised.

If no attempt is initiated to add an extra amount of material and energy to the system, it will itself raise its level temporarily, which will be reflected as a temporary reduction in the material and energy withdrawal from the system. The technique of correction of this transient effect is known as dynamic compensation, which can be accomplished quite successfully by use of a lead-lag unit. The lead-lag unit is capable of delivering more as well as less material or energy to the process to raise and lower its potential during a load change. The most stringent specification imposed upon a lead-lag unit is its steady-state accuracy. If it does not repeat its behaviour accurately during the steady-state condition immediately after a transient response, the lead-lag unit will degrade the performance of the feed-forward loop; as a result, linearity, repeatability and freedom from hysteresis may not remain achievable.

As far as the limitation on feed-forward control due to error in measurement or computation is concerned, the role of feedback control loops becomes deterministic in the sense that it is capable of removing completely the offset caused by the feed-forward control. In practice, however, most of the control load is carried by the feed-forward system itself, but feedback is still very much required by the system in its execution.

We can consider a practical example of three-element control for controlling boiler drum level in a power generating station. In a power station, boiling water to raise steam constitutes an important process. Control of level is a major function in the operation of a boiler or any steam generation plant. Steam is used to drive the prime movers, for example steam turbines. Steam is produced at subcritical pressure and temperature and thereby a distinct water-steam interface is defined. This interface is realised in the boiler drum located near the top of the boiler housing. The main function of the drum is to provide the surface area as well as the volume inside for carrying out separation of steam from water.

The secondary functions of the drum are:

  1. It purifies the steam by scrubbing the entrained water droplets as well as dissolved solids.
  2. It provides space for internal water treatment and a blowdown connection for removal of impurity concentration in the boiler feed water.
  3. It provides a place for addition of boiler feed water and also a connection for recirculation of water to different parts of the water tubes.

Due to these functions carried out by the boiler drum, its level is a critical process variable. If the level is very high, this results in flooding of water into the steam purification equipment; water along with impurities is carried over to the steam, which ultimately affects the turbine blades in addition to other erosion problems. If the level is very low, this results in deficient water treatment and insufficient water recirculation functions. In the long run these are the reasons for boiler tube failure due to overheating in the absence of water on the boiling surfaces. Normally the drum level is held within 50 mm of the set value with some tolerance for temporary upsets.

Although the level control function is understood to be simple, it becomes difficult when the liquid reaches its saturation. Under boiling conditions of water, steam bubbles exist below the steam-water interface and these bubbles, having volume, displace water. The process becomes dynamic because there is movement of bubbles up the tubes all the way up to the drum. In steady-state boiling conditions the location of the steam-water interface represents a reasonable inferential index of the quantity of water inside the tube. Under transient conditions, that is a change in pressure inside the drum and its effect on the interface, or a change in heat input, the situation becomes completely different. On a pressure change, bubbles will collapse or will be generated since the water mass reaches temperature equilibrium at the new operating pressure; there is also a change in the volume of existing bubbles. A change in heat input changes the rate of bubble formation, which changes the bubble traffic up the tubes and also the quantity of water supported and pushed up the tubes. All these effects shift the steam-water interface level, and therefore level measurement in this condition is meaningless until the heat is properly distributed throughout.

As far as load changes are concerned, there can be only two forms of load change in this process: a change in steam demand and a change in heat input. Other load changes in terms of change in feed-water temperature and change in the rate of blowdown are of less importance and are not considered. If we examine the effect of a load change on drum level with respect to a change in steam demand, we immediately find that the pressure over the interface decreases. As a result of the decrease in pressure, a certain amount of water flashes into steam bubbles with a consequent increase in the volume of existing bubbles. Simultaneously the pressure control system starts adding more fuel, resulting in increased heat input and increased steaming rate. These effects altogether tend to produce a surge condition on the interface, which is known as SWELL. Similarly, on a decrease in demand, pressure increases and bubbles collapse; the volume of existing bubbles decreases. As the pressure control system decreases the fuel supply, the heat input and consequently the steaming rate decrease. These effects tend to produce a fall in level, which is known as SHRINK. Swell and shrink are negative effects which are not logical according to load changes, but they are temporary conditions and disappear at once as the normal heat distribution through water and vapour is established and a more steady boiling condition returns. However, they can result in severe over-heating and under-heating.

The main objective of the drum level control system is to maintain the water-system interface at or near the specified location inside the drum in all conditions. However, level is an operational variable only and is not a direct measure of a load change. But since a level change is caused by mass flow unbalance, the design objective for the control system is to provide control of mass balance, that is the steam flowing out should be supplemented by feed-water flow into the boiler. The exact mass balance is indicated by the drum level. This control is accomplished by changing the feed-water flow on the basis of the steam flow change.

A proper balance between the steam load and the boiler feed-water flow is required because fluctuation in feed-water flow affects the steam temperature control as well as the feed-water heaters and pumps. It is to be noted that an inherent static error and a phasing relationship exist during transients between steam flow and feed-water flow. The proposed drum level control system is required to compensate for this error and also for any other unaccounted flow such as blowdown and leakages.

The three-element control for boiler drum level takes into account three variables: steam flow, feed-water flow and the drum level. The final control element, that is the feed-water valve, controls the flow of feed-water and is influenced by all three variables. The primary controller is the drum level controller, whose signal input is the drum level variable. The independent set variable is set in this controller itself. The secondary controller is connected in cascade with the primary controller. Its set value is set remotely, that is the output of the primary controller in combination with the steam flow/feed-water flow computer output becomes the set value for the secondary controller. The flow of feed-water becomes the process variable for the secondary controller. The feed-forward loop in the form of steam flow-feed-water flow is used to change the flow of feed-water to the system. For this, the load change in the form of a steam flow change is fed forward as the primary index to affect the feed-water flow. The two feedback control loops here are the drum level controller and the feed-water flow controller. The feedback loops monitor the accuracy of the feed-forward loop and provide final control to the water-steam interface inside the drum by actuating the feed-water control valve, as shown in Figure 6.25.

The steam flow transmitter measures the load change in the form of steam flow and, after getting it square-rooted, sends it to the feed-water flow computer. Square-root extraction is important since flow signals are non-linear (Flow ∝ √Differential Pressure). The drum level is similarly measured and the signal is transmitted to the primary controller as the process variable, where it is compared with respect to the set value. The correction generated by the primary controller is sent to the feed-water flow computer. The feed-water flow computer combines the steam flow signal, as the load signal, and the primary controller output signal and generates the necessary remote set point signal for the secondary controller.

Due to the low time constant of the boiler drum, control of level is subject to rapid load changes. Also, due to the turbulence prevailing inside the system, a narrow proportional band is not advisable to select, since this causes unnecessary variations in the feed-water flow, which are unacceptable in the industries as well. The feed-water system simply manipulates feed-water flow to equalise the amount of steam produced and withdrawn from the drum. The control system solves the following equation:

WF = WS + MC − 0.5

where WF and WS are the mass flow of feed-water and steam taken out respectively. MC is the output of the primary controller, which has a normal value of 0.5, that is a 50% bias. Since the drum level system is not self-regulating, the process variable can drift; therefore the primary controller is selected for two-mode control, proportional and integral. During the steady-state period the feed-water flow is just equivalent to the steam flow out and therefore the output of the primary controller shrinks the bias, the set value of the primary controller being at 50%.

Load changes are normally followed by a period of dynamic imbalance which looks like transient error. The technique of correction for dynamic imbalance is called dynamic compensation and is accomplished by a lead-lag unit. The most important requirement for a lead-lag unit is its steady-state accuracy. The relation between an actual change in feed-water flow brought about by a given change in steam flow out is perfect neither in the transient nor in the steady-state condition. Integral action in the secondary controller is used to adjust the phase relation between steam and feed-water flows.

With the advent of microprocessor-based, programmable, multifunction, dual controllers, entire control loop management can be accomplished by one instrument only. Only three PC-based remote-configurable smart transmitters, communicating on the HART protocol, are required, one each for drum level, feed-water flow and steam flow out. The functions of square rooting as well as flow computation are programmed inside the multifunction controller itself. An electro-pneumatic valve positioner is used for the control valve installed on the feed-water line. The control loop with these instruments is indicated in Figure 6.26.

Example 6.1

A pressure indicator controller (PIC) of proportional only type has a range of control between 0 and 160 kg/cm². If the process variable pointer indicates 50 kg/cm², then the controller output is 0.2 kg/cm². Calculate the set value of the controller and also percentage error. Assume gain of the controller to be 2.

Solution

E₀ = controller output = 0.2 kg/cm² = 0%

[Since the range of output is from 0.2 to 1.0 kg/cm²] Normal controller output Eₙ = 50% ≈ 0.6 kg/cm². Gain Pg = 2.

Eₛ = (E₀ − Eₙ)/Pg = (0 − 50)/2 = −25%
Eₛ = (Cpv − Csv)/(Crmax − Crmin) × 100 ...(i)

where Cpv and Csv are the controller process pointer value and set value respectively, and Crmax and Crmin are the controller maximum and minimum range respectively. Here Cpv = 50; Crmax = 160; Crmin = 0; Eₛ = −25. Putting these values in equation (i), we get

−25 = (50 − Csv)/(160 − 0) × 100 or Csv = 90 kg/cm²
At gain 2, proportional band Pb = 50%
= (160 × 50)/100 = 80 kg/cm²

The accompanying control line diagram describes the control activity: the process variable scale runs from 0 to 160, the controller output scale from 0.2 to 1.0, with marks at 0.2, 0.6 and 1.0; the set value sits at 90 and the 80 kg/cm² proportional band spans from 50 to 130.

Example 6.2

A temperature indicator controller (TIC) of proportional only type has range between 50°C and 450°C. When the process variable pointers indicate 75°C and 225°C, the controller output is 0.3 kg/cm² and 0.9 kg/cm² respectively. Calculate the gain value and set value of the controller.

Solution

Eₛ = (E₀ − Eₙ)/Pg and also Eₛ = (Cpv − Csv)/(Crmax − Crmin) × 100

which can be written as

Csv = Cpv − [(Crmax − Crmin)/100] × [(E₀ − Eₙ)/Pg] ...(i)

Here Crmax = 450°C; Crmin = 50°C; Eₙ = 0.6 kg/cm²; putting these values in equation (i) gives for 75°C process variable:

Csv = 75 − [(450 − 50)/100] × [(0.3 − 0.6)/(0.8 Pg)] × 100

[where 0.8 comes from the range of output medium, i.e. (1.0 − 0.2) = 0.8 kg/cm²]

= 75 + 150/Pg ...(ii)

Similarly, for 225°C process variable:

Csv = 225 − [(450 − 50)/100] × [(0.9 − 0.6)/(0.8 Pg)] × 100
= 225 + 150/Pg ...(iii)

Now since both Csv have to be equal, hence,

75 + 150/Pg = 225 − 150/Pg, which gives Pg = 2
Hence, Csv = 75 + 150/2 = 150°C

The control line diagram shows marks at 50, 75, 150, 225 and 450 on the process variable scale and at 0.3, 0.6 and 0.9 on the controller output scale; the set value sits at 150. Since the value 0.6 kg/cm² is halfway between 0.3 kg/cm² and 0.9 kg/cm² (given values), the set value must be halfway between 75°C and 225°C.

Example 6.3

The diagram shows a level control loop where the level of the tank is being controlled by putting water through a control valve (CV) which delivers 1000 m³/hr in full open position. The control valve characteristic is such that it passes 10 m³/hr per percentage change in controller output signal. The level has been maintained originally for a load of 500 m³/hr. If the PB setting of the level indicator controller (LIC) of proportional only type is 10%, calculate the controller output and offset error when the load is changed to 600 m³/hr.

Solution

The required controller output = (600 m³/hr)/(10 m³/hr per cent change in controller output) = 60%
Therefore, the offset error = (E₀ − Eₙ)/Pg ...(i)
Here PB = 10% = 0.1, hence Pg = 1/0.1 = 10
Putting the value of Pg in equation (i) gives the offset error, which is equal to (60 − 50)/10 = 1%

Example 6.4

A level indicator controller (LIC) of range 10 to 15 metres and of integral type has a set value of 12 metres with the original controller output at 21%. If the level goes down to 13.5 metres, calculate the controller output after 2 seconds. Assume the integral rate of the controller to be set at 9 repeats per minute. Also assume the error to remain constant.

Solution

Cpv = 13.5 metres; Csv = 12 metres; Crmax = 15 metres; Crmin = 10 metres
Eₛ = (Cpv − Csv)/(Crmax − Crmin) × 100 = [(13.5 − 12)/(15 − 10)] × 100 = 30%
Ti = 9 repeats/minute = 0.15 repeats/second = 0.15% output/second/% error
dE₀/dt = Ti Eₛ = 0.15 × 30 = 4.5% per second
E₀ = Ti∫₀ᵗ Eₛ dt + Eₙ = Ti Eₛ t + Eₙ = 4.5 × 2 + 21 = 30%

Example 6.5

A level indicating controller (LIC) of proportional type has proportional band (P.B.) set at 75%. The range of the controller is from 0 to 10 metres and the set value is at 6.5 metres. The characteristic of the final control element through which the level is being maintained is linear. The supply of water is from a tank which is fed by a pump at 7 m³/hr rate. Estimate the level maintained if the final control element delivers 10 m³/hr at maximum opening.

Solution

Load = 7 m³/hr (pump capacity); maximum load = 10 m³/hr (capacity of final control element)
% load = (7/10) × 100 = 70%

Assuming constant differential pressure across the final control element, for balanced conditions flow in must equal flow out, so the controller output is E₀ = 70%, since Eₙ = 50% and Pg = 100/75.

Eₛ = [(70 − 50)/100] × 75 = 15%
% set value = (6.5 × 100)/Range = (6.5 × 100)/10 = 65%
and % Level = 65 + Eₛ = 65 + 15 = 80%
Hence, Level = 8 metres

Example 6.6

A temperature indicator controller (TIC) of PID type (proportional + integral + derivative) has a range of 0 to 100°C. The value of temperature measured and the set value are 35°C and 45°C respectively. For a gain of 2 and integral and derivative times of 4 minutes each, find the time taken by the controller to change its output by 5%, if the temperature is held constant.

Solution

Since the temperature is held constant, there shall not be any change due to either proportional or derivative action. However, the integral action certainly shall affect the change, but the proportional band will affect the integral response as well.

error = 45 − 35 = 10°C = (10/100) × 100 = 10%
Output due to proportional action = Error × Gain = 10 × 2 = 20%

Since integral action repeats the output of the proportional action, this 20% change will take place over the entire period of 4 minutes. Hence, a 5% change shall take place in one minute.

Deviation observed in a controller following a step disturbance is 4.8% (error) in the process variable. Calculate the error after elapsing two periods, assuming quarter amplitude decay in the response of the controller.

The damped oscillating curve shown has successive peaks A, B and C one period apart; here B/A = C/B = 1/4.

Error after the second period with quarter amplitude decay = 1.2/4 = 0.3%

Example 6.8

A temperature-indicating controller (TIC) of PID type has a range of 0 to 100°C. By manual adjustment of a 0.6 kg/cm² control signal to the final control element installed in a heating steam supply line, the temperature could be maintained at 50°C. By increasing the control signal to 0.8 kg/cm², a process reaction curve is plotted which shows a rise in temperature to 80°C within a period of 3 minutes. If the lag in the process is 2 minutes, find what type of controller is best suited and estimate the optimum controller settings as well.

Solution

The accompanying process reaction curve shows the temperature rising from 50°C to 80°C (a change of 30°C ≈ 30%) after a 2-minute lag and over a 3-minute interval.

Percentage change in final control element: ΔC = (0.8 − 0.6)/0.8 × 100 = 25%
Percentage change in temperature: Δt = (80 − 50)/(100 − 0) × 100 = 30%
Lag Lg = 2 minutes; duration T = 3 minutes; since Lg < T, a PID controller is most suitable for the control purpose
Process reaction rate PRR = Δt/T = 30/3 = 10% per minute
PB = [(80 + 3) + PRR × Lg]/ΔC = (83 × 10 × 2)/25 = 66.4%
Integral rate = 0.5/Lg = 0.5/2 = 0.25 repeats/minute
Derivative time = 0.5 Lg = 0.5 × 2 = 1 minute

Example 6.9

Why is a controller adjusted?

Solution

A controller is adjusted to match the gain and time functions of the controller to those of the process which is being controlled.

Example 6.10

What is the relation between the integral time and the derivative time of a properly adjusted PID controller installed in a process control loop?

Solution

In such a controller, integral time is just equivalent to derivative time.

In a typical cascade control situation where the time constant of the secondary control loop happens to be more than the time constant of the primary control loop, what is the benefit of selecting a cascade loop?

Example 6.13

A level-indicating controller (LIC) of proportional type has a range of 0 to 90 inches. PB is set at 70%, bias at 50% and the set value is at 45 inches. What is the level at steady state control if the load is fixed at 3.6 gpm? The final control element has a linear characteristic and can supply 6 gpm at 100% stroke.

Solution

Flow in = (3.6 gpm/6 gpm) × 100 = 60%

At steady state condition, flow in shall equal flow out. The controller output should therefore equal the percentage flow in, i.e. 60% = E₀.

Percentage set value = 45 inches/90 inches = 50% = Csv
E₀ = [100/(%PB)](Csv − Cpv) + 50%
60 = (100/70)(50 − Cpv) + 50
Cpv = 50 − [(60 − 50) × 70]/100 = 43%
= 0.43 × 90 = 38.7 inches
