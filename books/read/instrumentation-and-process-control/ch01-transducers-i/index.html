This chapter defines and classifies transducers, distinguishes a transducer from a transmitter and from an inverse transducer, sets out the three groups of characteristics (input, output and transfer), and surveys the mechanical, electrical and optical conversion elements together with the secondary transducing systems.

1.1 Introduction

The origin of sensing and transducing can be traced to the very start of life on the earth itself. For instance, our sensory organs every time interact with the environments and transform the sensory input into brain stimuli. This in turn compels us to act the way we act. Sometimes this seems surprising as to how unconsciously we perform the way we actually are required to. Here we realize that the human beings are the ultimate control system. The control system devised by the Almighty seems to be very simple. But the real complexity, we start realizing when we think of designing and implementing our own control system. For instance, a mechanical machine for recognizing patterns or a machine for recognizing different voices. These would involve countless hours of complex computer programming and very sophisticated sensing capabilities. Such combination allows an optical device to first differentiate one pattern from the other and then select the proper one. Similar is the case with the voice recognition system. Whatever may be the complexities the sensor and the transducer combinations are the components of prime importance for any control system organization.

Transducer is in fact the basic input element to the man-made instrumentation system. In effect a transducer is a complete package consisting of sensor as well as conditioning system. A sensor interacts with its environments in such a way as to measure something changing in the environment. For instance, change in heat content inside a particular boundary changes the resistance of a selected material immersed inside it. This change in resistance when connected to a wheatstone bridge results in an out of balance current of the order of microampere. This out of balance current is proportional to the resistance change. This current after suitable amplification and compensation (to be carried out in the signal conditioning unit) can be used as input to instrumentation system.

1.2 Definition of Transducer

A transducer can be defined as a complete device in itself. It takes changes in process as input and transforms these into suitable output form in such a way that the output is the proportionate representation of the input. A general transducer arrangement has been shown in the block diagram (Figure 1.1). Sometimes transducers are understood to be a transmitters as well. A transmitter essentially is a device that responds to a changing process variable and produces a proportional signal. This signal is fed to a receiver instrument for indication or control purposes as shown in the block diagram in Figure 1.2. A transducer, however, is in physical contact with the process and produces a response in terms of motion or force when the process variable changes. When this response is used to generate a signal with respect to a particular scale used for measurement or control, the transducer is said to be performing as a transmitter. In this way, all transmitters are essentially transducers, but all transducers are not necessarily transmitters.

1.3 Classification of Transducer

Following are the various transducers.

1.3.1 Primary and Secondary Transducers

Motion or force detectors produce response in terms of displacement of the order of 0.001 inch from the input transducers. These primary transducers are also called Primary Measuring Elements. These sensing elements are used to generate a signal with respect to a particular scale, relative to a measured variable. The signal-generating unit that is actuated essentially by the input or primary transducer is hereafter called secondary transducer or output transducer. The secondary transducers generate a standard signal that is 4 to 20 mA or 1 to 5 volt in terms of electrical signal and/or 0.2 to 1.0 kg/cm2 in terms of pneumatic signal.

Sometimes an available electrical signal may have to be converted to equivalent pneumatic signal and vice-versa. Such converters are also called transducers. For instance, Electro-pneumatic Transducer which is normally used for operation of pneumatic control valves in process control. Similarly, a pneumatic-to-electrical transducer which is used for obtaining the pneumatic indication in terms of electrical indication.

It is important to note that the output signal does not start from zero. This means that some output is available at zero process variable as well, that is 4 mA electrical. This is known as Live Zero System, because the output signal of 4 mA is available even when the process variable is at its lowest limit. The advantage of this system is that it provides a means of detecting an open circuit fault in the signal system.

1.3.2 Active and Passive Transducer

Active transducers, as the name suggests, are active without the help of any outside supply source. They are self-generating (voltage or current) transducers. Thus, they are themselves capable of producing output for actuation of the secondary measurement system. The energy required for production of output is inherent in the process itself which is being measured. For instance, a thermocouple element which is a temperature measurement element. The principle of working of thermocouple is very simple. When two dissimilar metals (say platinum and rhodium) of appropriate characteristics are fused together it becomes a thermocouple, as shown in the Figure 1.3. The fused tip is called hot junction since this has to be immersed in the hot medium. For measurement purposes, this end is placed inside a hot medium whose temperature has to be measured. The other ends are connected to the measurement circuit to form a cold junction. Due to difference in temperature of the hot and cold junction, thermo-emf is induced which is responsible for a current flow through the measurement circuit. This emf is of the order of several millivolts which can be preamplified for obtaining a proportional indication for the measurement system. Other examples of active transducers are tachogenerator, photovoltaic cells and all transducers fabricated on the principle of piezoelectric effects.

The passive transducers on the other hand are externally powered transducers. These generate output signals with respect to power supplied to it. For instance, a Resistance Temperature Detector (RTD) is an element which changes its resistance due to change of temperature. Hence, when this RTD is immersed in a hot medium, its resistance will change. This change, however, cannot be felt unless it is connected to some source. Usually, such a system is connected to wheatstone bridge powered by a supply battery. The out of balance current of the bridge when passed through the detecting element (DE) shall indicate the change in resistance brought about by the change in temperature. The system has been shown in the Figure 1.4. This is an example of passive transducer. Other examples are pneumatic pressure transducers which are powered pneumatically (supply pressure being 1.4 kg/cm2 dry air and corresponding output signal being 0.2 to 1.0 kg/cm2 air).

1.3.3 Analog and Digital Transducers

The analog transducers convert the input variable into an analog output. The analog output varies with respect to time in continuous fashion. Thus, the output is a continuous function of time. Almost all types of conventional transducers are analog because their output is a continuous function of time. However, nowadays a new class of transducers has appeared in the instrumentation field called digital transducers. They convert the input variable into an electrical output which is in pulse form. In that way an output of NO and NC (normally open and normally closed) contacts of electromagnetic relay two position contact points are also considered to be a digital transducer. This is due to the fact that it provides two distinct positions of the system similar to an on-off switch. The NO and NC contacts have been shown in the Figure 1.5.

Sometimes the primary transducer is in the form of analog itself and provides output in the analog form but by providing a secondary transducer, it is later on converted to digital form. Such transducers, can also be regarded as digital transducers. It is worth mentioning that a digital transducer presents the measurement signal output in digital form for indication. Figure 1.6 shows a combination of pressure transducer (as primary transducer) and analog to digital converter (as secondary transducer) connected in between the input and the output.

1.4 Inverse Transducer

Whatever we have discussed till now is that a transducer converts the input process variable into proportional electrical output or pneumatic output. This output is essentially used as input for indication, recording or controlling. The controller also presents an output signal, which is called control output. This control output is used for manipulating the input through specified final control element to bring back the process variable to the desired level. It is desirable that control output should be adaptable to the final control element. This is usually called inverse transduction. Hence, the inverse transducer is a transducer element that converts the control output (electrical output in particular) into suitable form for actuation of the final control element.

Figure 1.7 shows a typical pressure control loop usually employed in the process industry for measurement as well as control. The location of the inverse transducer in this control loop is before the final control element. This transducer converts the output from pressure indicator controller into appropriate signal suitable for operation of the final control element, the final control element in this case being a pneumatically operated control valve. Another name for the inverse transducer here shall be electro-pneumatic transducer.

1.5 Characteristics of Transducers

While in operation, a transducer goes through several stages of changes in its behaviour. Changes in output behaviour and in between them lies a transfer behaviour. Based on these behavioural changes a transducer experiences, three different characteristics are observed, in general, which are input characteristics, output characteristics and transfer characteristics.

1.5.1 Input Characteristics

As we know, the primary portion of transducer changes are due to changes in process variable to be measured. The changes follow strictly the characteristics of the process itself and is termed input characteristics. In this particular case, the range of changes, i.e. from a minimum (initial state) to a maximum (final state) must correspond to the operating range of the input transducer itself. The resolution of the input transducer for these changes must be the same through the entire range of the changes of the process variable to be measured.

Sometimes loading effect becomes prominent and that shifts the input characteristic to a new position. Loading essentially means extracting energy from the system for the operation of the transducer. In this condition measurement functions by the transducers cannot be carried out accurately and therefore, suitable means are employed to minimize the effect.

1.5.2 Output Characteristics

Output characteristics constitute four objectives:

  • Choice of signal output type.
  • Choice of signal level and range.
  • Output impedance level.
  • Noise consideration.

(i) Output type

Output characteristics, as we know are related to types of output signal, i.e. A.C. or D.C. and also voltage or current level.

D.C. versus A.C.: Usually the transducer output has to travel upto a long distance in practical instrumentation scheme, therefore low level, low frequency A.C. output is not advisable to be used because 50 Hz stray fields will affect it. High level D.C. output can be considered, however, which practically is immune to the effect of outside interferences or pick-ups.

In A.C. output system, phase shift may occur and that has to be taken into consideration in measurement system. D.C. output systems do not face such problem.

D.C. output system (especially at low level) requires high grade instrumentation amplifier. On the other hand, A.C. output systems do not require such complicated amplification.

D.C. output (in terms of current) can be converted into force by passing D.C. current through a coil placed in a magnetic field. Hence D.C. output system eliminates the need of rectification if A.C. output system is used. Thus, low-level D.C. output system shall require amplification while low-level A.C. output system shall require both amplification as well as rectification.

If the instrumentation system is located in hazardous locations, it is advisable to use D.C. output system with low energy level for safety considerations.

Voltage versus current: Current output system can develop higher voltages very easily and hence can be made more immune to the effect of thermal and inductive voltages. Current transducers are more expensive than voltage transducers but inexpensive receiver instrumentation system can be employed effectively in conjunction with current transducers.

Current output systems are preferable to voltage output system since all receiver instruments can be connected in series without significant interactions. On the other hand, voltage output systems require careful consideration of output impedances to avoid loading effect.

Practically, many receiver instruments are voltage operated. The required voltage is developed by passing the current output through a precision register. Voltage operated electro-pneumatic transducers shall require temperature compensated coil for its operation. A current output system, however, shall not require such coil compensation.

(ii) Signal level and range

Normal practice is to use commonly accepted standard output of 4 to 20 mA D.C. for transducers. This is known as the live zero system because it provides a means of detecting on open circuit fault.

The receiver instruments are operated in the range of 0.25 V to 1.25 V D.C. which is developed across a 62.5 ohms precision resistor placed in series with the current output.

(iii) Output impedance level

Output impedance is the characteristic of transducer output circuit. Loading of successive stages in any instrumentation scheme is determined primarily by output impedances of the transducers. To minimize loading effect, the output impedance should have minimum possible value.

If the output impedance of a transducer is lower with respect to load impedance of successive stages, the system exhibits the characteristics of a constant voltage source (a voltage output transducer). But if the output impedance of transducer is higher, it resembles the characteristics of a constant current source (a current output transducer). The third case is when these impendances are of comparable values, i.e. a case of maximum power transfer from the output of the transducer to the successive instrumentation system. The efficiency in such condition is, however, only 50%.

(iv) Noise considerations

The useful range of output of a transducer is decided by noise consideration. Noise in any instrumentation system limits the precision of transducer output signal measurement. Pickups, crosstalk, interference and hash are the other names of different forms of noise. The transducer output signal is of low level and, therefore, the instrumentation system becomes more sensitive to noise. Power lines, transformers, motors, relays, inverters, etc. are the main sources of noise in industrial environment. A good design of instrumentation scheme calls for adequate protection from noise.

When transducer signal system passes through the area of varying magnetic fields due to powerlines, transformers, etc. a superfluous voltage is superimposed on it. This is known as electromagnetic noise. When the transducer signal carrying cable is in the vicinity of power cable, a 50 Hz A.C. signal superimposed due to potential difference across coupling capacitance. This is known as electrostatic noise. If in a bunch of cable pairs, one pair of cable carries a pulsating current, then a voltage is superimposed on adjacent pair due to capacitive coupling. This is known as crosstalk.

Electromagnetic, electrostatic and crosstalk come under the category of Normal Mode Noise. This type of noise is also known as Series Mode Noise or Transverse Noise. It directly impresses a voltage upon the transducer output as legitimate signal and appears as part of transducer output constituting error.

The level of normal mode noise can be of the order of 10 mV at 50 Hz in a typical industrial installation. Output of low-level thermocouple transducer, which handles signals of around 5 mV, can be significantly affected by the normal mode noise.

The Common Mode Noise, on the other hand, changes the voltage level by the same amount with respect to earth on both the output terminals of the transducer. This is called Longitudinal Noise. It is injected in the transducer output system by sources having one terminal grounded and other coupled to the receiver circuit capacitively or inductively. Voltage produced by a noise generator as the result of having two different potential grounds in the same loop is an example of common mode noise.

In industrial installation, ungrounded wires often have common mode noise level at 4 to 5 volt at 50 Hz. These effects can be eliminated by using receiver instrument with differential input which provides high impedance between the terminals and the ground. Nowadays, however, instrumentation systems are designed to withstand a very high level of common mode noise of around 100V.

Common mode noise gets converted to normal mode when signal wires have different impedances. Such problem appears when thermocouple is connected to the transducer through extension wire having different impedances due to different metals. The effect is minimized by grounding the thermocouple.

1.5.3 Transfer Characteristics

Transfer characteristics define that portion of the transducer operation where actual change from input to output takes place. The transfer characteristic encapsulates three performance criteria, which are detrimental for transducer operation:

  • Transfer function.
  • Error.
  • Effects due to environmental changes.

(i) Transfer function

Transfer function of a transfer unit is defined as f = po / pi, where po and pi are output and input signal level of the transducer.

Similarly, Gain of the transfer unit is defined as incremental change in the output with respect to incremental change in input, i.e. G = Δpo / Δpi. This is also called sensitivity.

Scale factor (S) is inverse of sensitivity and is given by S = (Δpi / Δpo) / pi = 1 / G

(ii) Error

Sometimes the transducer units do not obey the transfer function relationship the way it is required, resulting in error. For instance, if in some transducers, the output obtained is po′ in place of po then the error e shall be given by e = po′ − po.

Transducer error can be looked upon in following ways:

  • (a) Scale error,
  • (b) Dynamic error,
  • (c) Error due to noise and drift;
  • (d) Error due to frequency change.

(a) Scale error

Scale error can be divided into four groups:

Zero error: In zero error, the output deviates from its normal value by a fixed amount throughout the scale range as shown in the Figure 1.8.

Sensitivity error: If the output is k po in place of po throughout the scale range (where k is a constant), then this type of error is called sensitivity error as shown in the Figure 1.9.

Non-conformity error: When the actual transducer function is different from the desired transducer function for almost all values of input then this type of error is called non-conformity error as shown in the Figure 1.10.

Hysteresis error: When there is a marked difference in increasing order of transducer output with respect to decreasing order, hysteresis error results. As shown in the Figure 1.11, the decreasing values are higher than that of the increasing values.

(b) Dynamic error

In transducer systems where input variable constantly changes with respect to time, the output becomes incapable of following the changes exactly. Therefore, a lag between the input change and the corresponding transducer output change results. Error so obtained is known as dynamic error.

Any transducer circuit containing a combination of energy storing element in series with resistor is an example, exhibiting the type of error described above. If, a step voltage input Vi is applied to a R-C series circuit, the potential Vt across capacitor after time T shall be given by:

Vt = Vi (1 − e^(−T/RC)) ...(1.1)

where RC is time constant.

The dynamic error λ = Vi − Vt = Vi · e^(−T/RC). ...(1.2)

When T is small, λ is minimum because λ is function of time.

(c) Error due to noise and drift

The description regarding error due to noise outlined in case of output characteristic apply to this case as well. As far as drift is concerned, it has the characteristic of gradual shift in transducer output from its original position with respect to time. The resulting error is called error due to drift. This error being related to the output of the transducer only, it does not depend upon the input condition.

(d) Error due to frequency change

In general, a linear transducer when applied with a sinusoidal input results in sinusoidal output. But if the frequency of the input is increased, then beyond a particular value, the transducer output starts dropping. Phase shift between input and output also increases. This response has been shown in the Figure 1.12.

(iii) Effect due to environmental changes

Performance criteria generally accepted for a transducer changes greatly due to environmental changes. A transducer needs a stable environment. Disturbance like electrostatic as well as electromagnetic field of stray nature affects the performance to a great extent. Similarly, mechanical shocks and vibration, temperature, pressure and humidity changes also affect the performance. Sometimes unstable mechanical mounting also affects the performance. However, suitable precautions are applied for minimizing the above unwanted disturbances.

1.6 Transducer Elements

Transducer elements may be described in two parts:

  • (i) Primary transducer element and
  • (ii) Secondary transducer element.

Primary transducer element is essentially the basic sensor element, which is directly in contact with the process to be measured. Secondary transducer element, on the other hand, converts the changes in variable, made available by the primary transducer element into a suitable output form. This output is then used for further processing in the required instrumentation scheme.

Primary transducer element can be further divided into three general categories:

  • (i) Mechanical conversion element,
  • (ii) Electrical conversion element,
  • (iii) Optical conversion element.

1.6.1 Mechanical Conversion Element

In this category, a mechanical object which is a device having spring characteristics works as primary sensor. The driving source is essentially a mechanical force acting in linear and/or in circular direction. There are as many as five major types of basic mechanical objects that can be used as primary sensing element. These are:

  • (i) Springs,
  • (ii) Torsion bar,
  • (iii) Proving ring,
  • (iv) Flexure arm and
  • (v) Cantilever beam.

In addition to the above five elements, other elements are also in use which are primarily pressure transducer elements and are placed in the same category.

  • (vi) Bourdon tube,
  • (vii) Diaphragm,
  • (viii) Capsule,
  • (ix) Bellows.

Now we shall study the above-mentioned elements in some detail one by one.

(i) Springs

Springs are supposed to be the most universal type of primary sensing elements. In general, when a force is applied to a spring, it changes its shape depending upon the line of action of force. If the applied force is compressive, compression in the spring takes place. If the force is of tensile type, elongation takes place. Whatever may be the line of action of force; there is a definite displacement experienced in the spring, which is the measure of the applied force. In addition to that the spring provides restoring force as well, which is characteristic of any spring.

Springs may be found in two shapes: spiral and helical as shown in the Figures 1.13 and 1.14.

Deflection Q in spiral-shaped spring can be obtained by mathematical expression:

Q = 12 · Td · L / (E · b · t³) ...(1.3)

where E is Youngs modulus of elasticity (N/m2), b is width of spring (metre), t is thickness of spring (metre), L is length of spring (metre), Td is torque (N m).

Similarly, displacement D in helical-shaped spring can be obtained from mathematical expression:

D = 8 · Fa · d1³ · n / (Gs · d2⁴) ...(1.4)

where Fa is force applied, (N), d1 is mean diameter of coiled spring (metre), d2 is diameter of spring wire (metre), n is number of wires, Gs is shear modulus (N/m2).

(ii) Torsion bar

As the name suggests, a torsion bar is in fact a torque-sensing element. When a torque T = Iδ, (where I is moment of inertia and δ is angular acceleration) is applied to a torsion bar, a torsional/twisting moment is generated in the torsion bar. This twisting moment tends to twist the torsion bar and keeps it in twisting stress. When the applied torque is released slowly, a restoring torque comes into existence, which brings back the torsion bar to its normal state, thereby releasing the stress. This principle is utilized in design and fabrication of Torque Tube, which is an essential part of Displacer Type level measuring transducers.

Q = 16 · Ta · L / (π · Gs · d³) radians ...(1.5)

where Ta is the torque applied (N m), Gs is the shear modulus (N/m2), d is the diameter of torsion bar (metre).

(iii) Proving ring

Proving ring or a ring dynamometer is a force measuring sensing device (a kind of load cell) shown in the Figure 1.15.

This is in fact a modern and developed version of the original design, which has been a column type load cell. When the forces Fa are applied as indicated in the diagram, a circumferential stress is generated. This stress in turn creates compressive strains on the inner surfaces and tensile strains on the outer surfaces. Strain gauges are employed for measurement of these strains.

Q = 0.244 · Fa · d³ / (E · b · t²) ...(1.6)

where Fa is applied force, d is the mean diameter, t is the thickness of wall, b is the width of the ring, E is Youngs modulus of elasticity.

(iv) Flexure arm

Flexure arm is a flexible thin steel strip pivoted by brazing onto a free body upon which the applied force will act for deflection as shown in the Figure 1.16.

The most fundamental part of a flexure arm is that it creates restrained deflection in a very systematic manner. The flexure arm is pivoted at both ends to the free body as shown. The flexure arm being of very very smaller size in comparison to free body shall ultimately take up the deflection. When the force is withdrawn, however, the flexure restores its original state. The most notable advantage of a flexure arm is that its movement is more or less frictionless.

(v) Cantilever beam

A cantilever beam is such a structure, which is rigidly fixed at one end, the other end being free to move under the influence of an applied force. The resulting reactive force is generated from the point where it is rigidly fixed as shown in the Figure 1.17.

When a force Fa is applied (as shown) at the free end, a bending moment is developed. The magnitude of this bending moment is proportional to the applied force Fa. Due to this bending moment, the fixed end of the cantilever beam experiences stress on top as well as bottom portion of the structure. Strain gages may be employed to measure the tensile strain on top and the compressive strain on bottom of the surfaces. The maximum deflection, however, shall be observed at the free end while maximum strain shall be experienced at the fixed end. The deflection d at the free end shall be given by the expression:

d = 4 · Fa · L³ / (E · b · t³) ...(1.7)

where E is Youngs modulus of elasticity, Fa is the force applied, b is the width of the cantilever, t is thickness of the cantilever, and L is the overall length of the cantilever.

ε = 6 · Fa · L / (E · b · t²) ...(1.8)

If it is desired to have a particular value of stress with respect to a fixed applied force Fa, the value of L, b and t may be optimized by usual optimization techniques. This will result in the best possible performance.

(vi) Bourdon tube

The bourdon tubes are elastic elements. These are fabricated by elliptically flattened tube of phosphor bronze or stainless steel material. Other materials in use are monel, brass, beryllium copper, etc. Selection of material depends upon pressure range, process medium, temperature, corrosive nature, viscosity, etc.

One end of the bourdon tube is sealed and the other one is open for allowing the fluid to enter for pressure sensing. Entry of the fluid tends to realign the shape of the bourdon tube. In effect the tip of the sealed end is displaced. This displacement is proportional to the pressure exerted by the fluid that entered the bourdon tube. The displacement being too small, is amplified further by mechanical linkages and gears to drive a sector and pinion combination. This combination is connected to a suitable pointer for indication of the applied pressure.

Bourdon tube elements have several advantages over other elements, e.g. they are inexpensive, have simple design, have reasonable and acceptable accuracy. The only disadvantage is its inability for precise measurement due to hysteresis.

In general, bourdon tubes are available in three different shapes: C-shape, spiral shape and helical shape. All these shapes have been shown in the Figure 1.18(a), (b), (c) and (d).

C shape bourdon tube. This is the most universal type of bourdon tube transducer element used for pressure sensing devices. The total angle subtended by the arc of C shape may be up to 225C or so.

Tip displacement d may be obtained by use of an empirical equation:

d = 0.33 · (Pa · L³ / E · t) · (W2 / W1)^0.33 · (D / t)² ...(1.9)

where W1 and W2 are the cross-sectional length and breadth of the bourdon tube, t is the thickness of the material, D is the diameter of the arc of bourdon tube, L is the circumferential length, E is the Youngs modulus of elasticity and Pa is the applied pressure.

As is seen clearly, the expression of tip displacement is non-linear. Linearization may be done by mechanical means in this case. The accuracy of measurement resulted by C shape bourdon tube ranges from ± 0.5% to ± 2.0%.

Spiral shape bourdon tube. An analysis of expression 1.9 reveals that the tip displacement may be increased by simply increasing the length L of the arc. By keeping the cross-section of the tube smaller and increasing the arc by a few turns results in a shape commonly known as spiral. Therefore, the amplification of tip displacement is not required at all since the spiral shape itself magnifies the movement. In this way use of mechanical linkages and gears to drive the sector and pinion combination is avoided. This eliminates the effect of backless that results due to wear and tear of the gears.

When the applied fluid pressure enters the spiral, it uncoils producing large tip movement. This tip movement may be directly used for indication without any amplification. As is obvious, the accuracy of spiral type design is superior to that of C type and is about ± 0.5%. This design is invariably used for manufacturing delicate instruments of extremely low range.

Helical shape bourdon tube. Helical type design is similar to spiral type but is more robust. The tip displacement for this is longer than that of spiral type. This design is applicable for higher ranges of pressure. Its over-range capability is extremely high due to its robustness. Due to higher volumetric space available with the helical shape, the internal space functions as capacitive chamber. If the fluid pressure under measurement is fluctuating, this design shall absorb entire fluctuations and will result is stable tip movement.

The number of turns of the helix is selected depending upon the magnitude of pressure to be measured. The accuracy of this design ranges from ± 0.5% to ± 1%.

In general, material of construction selected depends upon the type of measurement fluid as well as range of pressure to be measured. For non-corrosive atmosphere and low-pressure application phosphor bronze and beryllium copper are used. For corrosive atmosphere and high-pressure application, stainless steel or monel are used. Range up to 40 kg/cm2 is considered to be low pressure in industrial application whereas range from 60 kg/cm2 to 600 kg/cm2 or more is considered to be high pressure.

(vii) Diaphragm element

It is a thin membrane made of metallic circular strip clamped securely at the periphery. The installation is performed by welding its periphery on to a solid circular flange with a hole of appropriate size at the centre of the flange. When the fluid pressure is applied in between the flange and the circular strip, measurable displacement in the strip is observed which is proportional to applied pressure. The linear displacement arrangement is mainly due to bending stress developed in the flat piece.

The shape of diaphragm is of two types: flat and corrugated as shown in the Figure 1.19. The design of corrugation may be of sawtooth, trapezoidal or sinusoidal from.

The corrugation in the diaphragm increases the surface area, which results in greater overall displacement in comparison to flat diaphragm. The design accuracy in manufacturing of correct corrugation, clamping system and the base plate are paramount in a single diaphragm sensing devices. The displacement d of flat diaphragm at the centre due to applied pressure Pa is given by:

d = 0.1875 · Pa · r⁴ · (1 − υ²) / (E · t³) ...(1.10)

where υ is the Poissons ratio, t is the thickness of the diaphragm material and E is Youngs modulus of elasticity of the material of diaphragm.

The expression gives the displacement at the centre point of the diaphragm, which goes on decreasing in magnitude towards the periphery and becomes zero just near the periphery of the diaphragm.

The use of flat diaphragm is limited to a displacement less than the half (upto 30% of t) of the thickness of the diaphragm. The linear relationship between displacement and the applied pressure is limited to this restriction for flat diaphragm. Considering the case of large displacement, d shall be given by expression:

d³ = 0.648 · Pa · r⁴ / (E · t) ...(1.11)

The above expression where the value of υ has been considered 0.3 for larger displacement, clearly indicates non-linearity between applied pressure Pa and resulting displacement d.

For obtaining higher displacement (greater than half of thickness), use of corrugation is recommended. Various shapes of corrugation have been shown in the Figure 1.20. The corrugated diaphragms are used for static measurement only. This is because of its susceptibility to vibration due to large reselience.

In general, the materials used for manufacture of diaphragms are those used for bourdon tubes as well. In industrial application, however, neoprene rubber (nylon impregnated) and Teflon materials are also used. For corrosive and high-pressure services, materials like SS316 L, Haslelloy c, Monel, etc. are also in use.

(viii) Capsule element

Two identical corrugated diaphragms sealed together at the periphery form a diaphragm capsule element. Obviously, this construction doubles the displacement with respect to single diaphragm. One end is kept open for applying the fluid pressure. The other end is connected suitably for taking up the displacement.

The construction has been shown in the Figure 1.21. The displacement is proportional to difference of applied pressure Pa and the outside pressure acting on the capsule element. Depending upon the applied pressure to be measured, the sealing on the periphery is done by soldering, brazing or welding. After fabrication stress relieving is carried out for stability.

To further enhance the displacement, two or more capsules may be joined together by means of spacers.

(ix) Bellows element

At one glance, the bellows look like staggered capsule elements put on one after another as shown in the Figure 1.22. But the internal construction of the bellows are quite different. These are thin flexible shells with convolutions as shown in the Figure 1.22. One end has been sealed for taking up the displacement. The other end is open to atmosphere for taking up the fluid pressure applied.

The choice of number of convolutions is important in the sense that it is entirely dependent upon the range of pressure applied. The displacement d generated by the bellows when a pressure Pa is applied shall be given by the expression:

d = N · Ae · Pa · ra³ / (E · t³) ...(1.12)

where N is the number of convolutions on the bellows, Ae is the effective overall area of the bellows, ra is the average radius, E is Youngs modulus of elasticity of material of bellows and t is the average thickness of the material of which the bellows have been constructed.

As is obvious from the construction of the bellows element, it can generate large displacement but is sensitive to vibration. The larger displacement can be restrained by use of a calibrated spring so that only a desired range of displacement may be utilized.

Two identical bellows when connected together mechanically, the resultant relative displacement produced by them shall be proportional to the difference in the two pressures applied separately to the two respective bellows. Such an arrangement may be useful in measurement of absolute pressure, or gage pressure or even differential pressure.

The advantages of bellows element are:

  • (i) Simple construction,
  • (ii) Inexpensive,
  • (iii) Wide application (to measure low, medium and high pressure, absolute pressure as well as gage pressure),
  • (iv) Accuracy accounts to 0.5% and reduction in drift as well as hysteresis.

The disadvantages include:

  • (i) Unsuitable for dynamic measurement,
  • (ii) Temperature compensation is required to minimize error in measurement due to ambient changes.

1.6.2 Electrical Conversion Element

In this system, variation in the features of substances brought about by mechanical or thermal energy may be reflected back in terms of changes in electrical property. These changes in effect are the changes in resistance, inductance or capacitance of the electrical system. Leaving aside these major changes, in some cases, small amount of voltages (of the order of mV) may also be induced when the changes take place due to change in thermal energy, i.e. change in temperature. Based on this broad principle, several practical transducer mechanisms have been developed. These mechanisms also come under primary sensing elements and can be enumerated as:

  • (i) Strain gage elements,
  • (ii) Resistance temperature detectors (RTD),
  • (iii) Thermocouple elements,
  • (iv) Thermistor elements,
  • (v) Variable inductance system,
  • (vi) Variable capacitance system,
  • (vii) Piezoelectric system,
  • (viii) Hall effect transducer element.

1.6.3 Strain Gage Element

As the name suggests, strain gage means gaging through strain. The relation between applied force/weight and elongation is characterized in terms of resultant strain. The strain is defined as the change (ΔL) observed in original length L per unit of overall length of the substance. Hence, strain can be expressed as ε = ΔL / L microstrains (one microstrain being equal to one micrometre/metre).

The usual expression of stress and strain while performing simple tension and/or compression test for strength of material is given by:

ε = σ / E ...(1.13)

where ε is strain, σ is stress and E is Youngs modulus of elasticity. This relation is more or less linear below elastic limit.

Due to application of force/weight, change in cross-section takes place in the substance. This action brings about changes in resistance, inductance and capacitance. For strain gaging system, change in resistance is more important. Therefore, ratio of change in resistance per unit overall resistance with respect to change in length per unit overall length is observed and represented by the expression (ΔR/R)/(ΔL/L); where ΔR is change in resistance, R is overall resistance, ΔL is change in length, L is overall length.

This ratio is called Gage factor (Gs) and is represented by the expression:

Gs = (ΔR/R)/(ΔL/L) = 1 + 2υ + (Δρ/ρ)/(ΔL/L) ...(1.14)

where υ is the Poissons ratio, ρ is the specific resistance of wire material (ohm-mt). This expression has been deduced from original equation R = ρ L/A which for incremental changes reduces to:

ΔR/R = Δρ/ρ + ΔL/L − ΔA/A ...(1.15)

This expression states that for a particular change in stress, the effective change in R is supplemented by proportionate change in length L, cross-sectional area A and also resistivity ρ. For a circular cross-sectional shape, the change in area A may be represented in terms of change in its diameter D by the following expression if the changes are extremely small: ΔA/A = 2 ΔD/D. On similar argument, ΔD/D = −υ ΔL/L.

(ΔR/R)/(ΔL/L) = 1 + 2υ + (Δρ/ρ)/(ΔL/L) ...(1.16)

In the above expression, the term 2υ represents the effect of dimensional changes whereas the term (Δρ/ρ)/(ΔL/L) represents piezoresistive effect. Piezoresistive effect is defined as change in resistivity with respect to strain. The equation (1.16) signifies that changes in resistances (due to strain) is not only because of changes in dimension but also due to change in resistivity. In fact, value of Poissons ratio is more or less fixed (being 0.3) for most of the materials used for the strain gage. In this light, if the piezoresistive effect can be considered exceptionally small then it can be neglected. Eventually, the expression for gage factor would be reduced to:

Gs = 1 + 2υ ...(1.17)

(a) Wire strain gage

Wire strain gages are made of fine resistance wire or foil called grid. The desirable properties for a resistance wire to be able to be used as a strain gage element are:

  • The value of resistance selected should be high enough in order to swamp out the undesirable effects due to variations in resistance in different parts of the circuit. However, this consideration decreases the sensitivity and therefore, a compromise is called for.
  • It should have low resistance temperature coefficient. This results in lower error due to temperature changes.
  • It should have minimum possible hysteresis effect in its response.
  • It should have linear variations in resistance with respect to strain developed.
  • Its frequency response should be in the acceptable range since strain gages are employed for dynamic measurement as well.

This results in unbalance of bridge and hence an out of balance voltage proportional to applied pressure/force/weight is obtained. (b) Thin metal film strain gage

Thin metal film strain gage is the latest technological innovation. In this technology, thin films of high-grade metals such as gold, platinum, nickel or even aluminium are formed onto an insulating substrate. The formation is carried out in vacuum by thermal evaporation process. The substrate is then attached to the elastic specimen. The process of vaporization and condensation are controlled to give desired shape.

In this metal film gaging, the specific resistance of the film RF is a basic property of the film and is important for gage factor calculation. The RF is related to the resistance of the film RG by the relation RF = RG · b/l, where b and l are the width and the length of the film respectively. RG is also given by the conventional relation RG = ρF · l/A where ρF and A as usual the resistivity and the cross-sectional area of the thin film. Hence the expression for RF takes the form RF = ρF/t [t is thickness].

RF = ρF / t ...(1.19)

This relation indicates that high resistance gages can be designed by selecting smaller dimension of film. The result of such design shall be improved sensitivity and reduced power consumption. In this way, this design technology is the best alternative to bonded foil strain gages.

Advantages: (i) The thin film gage has very stable operation over temperature range from -200 to + 400C. (ii) This has higher gage resistance, higher gage factor, higher sensitivity and higher accuracy. (iii) Its construction is rugged. (iv) It shows minimum possible hysteresis and creep.

(c) Semiconductor strain gage

The principle behind the design of semiconductor strain gages is completely different from the metal resistance strain gages. It employs the piezoresistive property of the semiconductor material. In metal resistance strain gages, change in strain is developed due to change in dimension and not due to change in resistivity of the material. But in semiconductor strain gage, it is the resistivity of the semiconductor material which is responsible for change in strain. The change in resistance due to strain is quite large with respect to metal resistance strain gage. In some cases the change is even 100 times more.

Gs = (ΔR/R)/(ΔL/L) = 1 + 2υ + M ...(1.20)

where M is the product of coefficient of piezo-resistance along the axis of gage and Young's modulus of elasticity of the gage along its length. Figure 1.26 shows a variety of construction of this type of gage.

The common material used for manufacture of semiconductor strain gage is silica doped with impurity having resistivity of 2 × 10⁻⁴ ohm-metre. The electrical contacts are made of gold wires. The backing technique is same as employed for conventional wire or foil strain gages. Since the semiconductor strain gages are delicate in nature, these are handled with care.

Advantages: (i) With semiconductor strain gages, changes in resistance due to strain is nearly 100 times greater than conventional strain gage. (ii) It is chemically inert. (iii) It has high gage factor. (iv) It is free from hysteresis and creep. (v) It is immune to fatigue.

Disadvantages: (i) Semiconductor strain gages are very sensitive to temperature changes. (ii) It has poor linearity. (iii) It is more expensive.

1.6.4 Resistance Temperature Detector Element (RTD)

Resistance temperature detector element is an important basic transducer element for measurement of temperature. The basic characteristic of change in resistance of a metal due to change in temperature has been utilized in the development of RTD element.

As we know, the crystalline structure of a metal has plenty of free electrons. When a voltage is applied, these electrons start flowing in a particular direction in a combined fashion. Resultant collisions of these electrons amongst themselves and with other ions present in the metal create natural restriction to the flow of electrons. In effect, it is observed as characteristic resistance of the metal. If we add substantial amount of heat to the metal, an increase in amplitude of collision is observed. This in effect results in an increase in resistance of the metal. Apart from the other factors, change in resistance due to change in temperature is dependent mainly upon temperature coefficient of resistance.

Variation of resistance Rt at temperature tC of the metal is however, governed by general equation:

Rt = Ro (1 + At + Bt² + Ct³ + ...) ...(1.21)

Where Ro is the resistance of metal at 0C and A, B, C, D are constants to be determined experimentally depending upon the particular range of measurement of temperature.

Requirement of a metal for use in manufacture of RTD element are:

  • Change in resistance due to per unit change in temperature should be large for better accuracy.
  • RTD is made small and for that the conducting material used should have high resistivity.
  • Relation between resistance of metal and temperature should be stable.

1.6.5 Thermocouple Element (T-C)

This is yet another most versatile transducer element for measurement of temperature. This element is made up of two dissimilar thermoelements which produce thermo-electric emf when heated. The emf e produced is governed by the equation e = a + bt + ct² where a, b, c, are constants.

e = a + bt + ct² ...(1.22)

The value of these constants depends upon the characteristics of the thermoelements and are found experimentally. The value of a indicates the elevation of the range. One point of the thermocouple is kept at 0C condition and the other one at temperature tC.

Basic theory of thermocouple. Seebeck found that if a closed path is formed by two different metals and the two junctions so formed are maintained at the different temperatures an electric current flow through the path as shown in Figure 1.28. He tried to select as many as 25-numbers of such metals. A few of them are Nickel, Platinum, Copper, Manganese, Chromium, Molybdenum, Silver, Cadmium, Zinc, Iron, etc.

Later on Peltier observed that heat was absorbed at hot junction while liberated at cold junction, the direction of current flow being different from one another. This observation led to the assumption that the junction of the metals may be a seat of emf found in seebeck effect. That means an emf exists across the junction of dissimilar metals in general, the magnitude being proportional to the temperature of the junctions. When both junctions happen to be at the same temperature the effective emf becomes zero.

Further experiment made by Kelvin in this direction revealed that the magnitude of emf produced was not exactly proportional to the difference of temperature. The emf first increased due to change in differential temperature, but further change in differential temperature decreased the emf and passing through zero line, it actually got reversed. It was found that in some metal (say copper) heat is liberated at a particular point along the heated conductor when current flows in the direction of heat flow. While heat is absorbed at that point when the current flows in the opposite direction. But in the other metal (say iron) the heat shall be absorbed when the current flows in the direction of heat flow while heat is liberated when the current flows in the opposite direction of heat flow. Hence, it can be concluded that the Seebeck emf which is a combination of Peltier as well as Kelvin effect is dependent upon temperature difference between the junctions and the metals selected for the formation of the couple. This type of emf, produced by any couple can be obtained from a thermoelectric diagram shown in the Figure 1.29.

In this diagram the y-axis represents thermoelectric power. The emf produced by the metals 1 and 2 when the junctions are maintained at temperatures T1 and T2 (abs.), given by the area ABCD is:

Area ABCD = (AD + BC)/2 × (T2 − T1) ...(1.23)

Figure 1.30 shows the emf per degree vs. temperature graph for a few metals out of which any two suitable metals are selected for fabricating a thermocouple.

In the diagram, iron and copper lines cross each other at 270C. Therefore, for a copper-iron thermocouple, if cold junction is maintained at bellow 270C and the temperature of hot junction is increased, the emf generated shall go on increasing till it reaches 270C. This fixed temperature is called neutral temperature for copper-iron thermocouple. If we increase the temperature further (from neutral temperature), increase in emf shall be observed from iron to copper and hence that will be called iron-copper thermocouple.

Selection criteria for thermocouple material.

  • Materials selected should have high emf per degree centigrade temperature.
  • While in operation, the generated emf should not vary with respect to calibrated value.
  • Composition of the materials should not change at elevated temperature.
  • If contamination and mechanical strains are envisaged while in use, suitable protective arrangement should also be selected.
  • The selected material should have stable thermo-elastic characteristic so that successive batches of similar thermocouples can be manufactured.
  • Proper ageing of the selected material should be done upto a value of maximum temperature in use.

Advantages: (i) It is inexpensive. (ii) This is perfectly suitable for making remote temperature recording system. Disadvantages: (i) This is less accurate and therefore, cannot be used for precision measurement work. (ii) Thermocouples become contaminated quite often and therefore, should be protected properly. (iii) For remote measurement/recording system, thermocouples are to be connected through compensating cable having the same characteristic as thermocouple.

1.6.6 Thermistors

There are certain well-constructed thermo-elements which have negative temperature characteristics. These are usually mixtures of oxides of nickel, copper, iron, manganese and uranium. These are called thermal resistors or thermistor. The resistance of thermistor element decreases with respect to increase in temperature. In certain cases this change is very large making the thermistor very sensitive, higher than RTD or even thermocouple. These are used in precision temperature measurement in the range of -60C to + 15C. Resistance of thermistor range for a few ohms to as high as 1 Mega ohm. The characteristic is highly non-linear.

There are three important characteristics of thermistors:

  • (i) Resistivity vs temperature characteristic.
  • (ii) Voltage vs current characteristic.
  • (iii) Current vs time characteristic.

(i) Resistivity vs temperature characteristic. This characteristic curve has been shown on the Figure 1.31. The relation between resistivity and temperature can be expressed by the expression:

R1 = R2 · exp[β (1/T1 − 1/T2)] ...(1.24)

where R1 and R2 are the resistances at temperatures T1 and T2 Kelvin. β is a constant which depends upon the characteristic of the thermo-element. As is seen in the diagram, the curve can be approximated to a linear one over a limited range of operation.

(ii) Voltage vs current characteristic. This characteristic curve has been shown in Figure 1.32. The two curves drawn for ambient temperature ta and for any other temperature t1 (say 50C) are typical curves indicating negative resistance characteristic. Application of small voltage results in microampere (μA) current and therefore, no sufficient heat is evolved to raise the temperature of the thermistor. Once enough heat is evolved on higher current, further increase in temperature decreases the resistance of thermistor and as a result more current passes through. This phenomenon continues till the applied power equals the heat absorbed. This characteristic of thermistor is typical and can be used to manufacture transducer for measurement of process variables in chemical industries.

(iii) Current vs time characteristics. This is another typical characteristic of a thermistor which allows the thermistor to be used in manufacturing of time delay relay cut-outs. As seen from the Figure 1.33, specified time interval is required to be elapsed for raising the temperature of the thermistor and resultant increase in steady state current to take place. This time interval can, however, be changed by changing applied voltage.

Advantages: (i) It has wide range of response time, typically from a fraction of a second to a few minutes. (ii) This is inexpensive. (iii) It has good stability. (iv) Though the used range of measurement is -60C to +15C, the range of measurement can be increased upto 400C or so by proper connection to withstand this much temperature. Disadvantages: (i) Since the thermistor has negative temperature characteristic, the current should be maintained as low as practicable to avoid error caused by self-heating of the element.

1.6.7 Inductive Transducer

Inductor coil, in its original form, is another very important as well as popular transducer element. In practice, however, there are three characteristics connected to an inductor coil upon which the construction of inductive transducers are based. They are:

  • (i) Self-inductance of the coil L,
  • (ii) Mutual inductance M, when two inductive coils are placed together and,
  • (iii) Eddy current which is produced when a conducting material is brought near the inductor coil carrying A.C.

(a) Inductive transducer based on self-inductance L. Self-inductance L of a coil of N turns is given by:

L = μ · N² · A / l ...(1.25)

where l and A are length and cross-sectional area of conductor of which the coil is made. μ is the permeability of the medium. The above expression indicates that by varying the geometric shape, A/l, of the conductor coil, the L can be varied and the transducer can be designed accordingly.

(b) Inductive transducer based on mutual inductance M. Mutual inductance M between two inductor coils of self-inductances L1 and L2 is given by M = Kc √(L1 L2), where Kc is the coupling constant.

M = Kc · √(L1 · L2) ...(1.26)

The value of Kc depends upon the orientation of two inductor coils and the medium in which the coils are placed. When the two inductor coils are connected in series with one coil being stationary and other in motion, the inductance of such an arrangement varies between (L1 + L2 − 2M) and (L1 + L2 + 2M). However, mutual inductance M varies with the movement of coil in motion.

Two types of coils can be used for designing a inductive transducer: (i) Iron-cored coil and (ii) Air-cored coil.

(i) Iron-cored coil. In iron-cored coil, the coil is wound on a core made of iron. Advantages: (i) Since the permeability of iron is high, the size becomes economical and loss of magnetic field strength is ineffective. (ii) Stray/external magnetic field does not affect the magnetic field produced since the field produced is stronger. Disadvantages: (i) Inductance is not constant but dependent upon the current flow in the coil. (ii) At higher frequency, eddy current loss is significant. Supply frequency should be kept under limit to minimize such losses.

(ii) Air-cored coils. This coil is in fact core-less coil. This means that the space where iron was located in case of iron-cored coil is free here and the coil is located in air. Advantages: (i) This can be operated at higher frequency because there is no eddy current loss. (ii) The permeability of the medium, i.e. air is constant and does not change according to current flow in the coil. Disadvantage: (i) The size becomes larger.

(c) Transducers based on eddy current produced. Inductive transducer based upon this principle has essentially a conducting plate placed before a coil in which an alternating current is flowing. The eddy current so produced in the plate is responsible for a magnetic filed in addition to the magnetic field produced by the current carrying coil. These two magnetic fluxes act against each other. As a result the net flux as well as inductance get reduced. Reduction in inductance varies according to the variation of space between the plate and the current carrying coil.

1.6.8 Capacitive Transducer

A capacitance is formed when an insulated substance is sandwiched between two conducting surfaces. The value of capacitance of such an arrangement shall depend upon the characteristics of the medium and the physical orientation of the conducting surfaces. In practice, generally parallel plate capacitors are used for realization of transducer. The value of capacitance C in such case is given by C = ε A/D where A is the cross-sectional area of the conducting surface and D is gap length between the two conducting surfaces. ε is permittivity of the medium located between the two conducting surfaces.

C = ε · A / D ...(1.27)

The principle of operation of capacitive transducer is based on three variables which in fact influence the value of capacitance:

  • (i) Change in gap length.
  • (ii) Change in relative cross-sectional area between the conducting surfaces.
  • (iii) Change in dielectric strength of the medium between the surfaces.

(i) Capacitive transducer based on change in gap length D. In this arrangement, one conducting surface is kept stationary while the other one is allowed is move relative to the previous one. The resulting change in capacitance C shall be function of D as shown in the Figure 1.34. The relation is non-linear. However, non-linearity can be minimized by utilizing medium having high dielectric constant. In this way the non-linearity can be approximated to linear one.

(ii) Capacitive transducer based on change in cross-sectional area A. The design of capacitive transducer, based on this principle is best suited when two hollow metallic tubes are employed for parallel conducting surfaces. The capacitance of the arrangement shall change when one tube is wound on the other or unwound from the other. In this case also, one tube is kept stationary while the other is allowed to displace relative to the stationary one. The rectangular conducting surfaces can also be employed, arranged one upon the other and the gap filled with dielectric material. Both the arrangements have been shown in the Figures 1.35 and 1.36. The variation of capacitance C with respect to cross-sectional area A is linear as shown in the Figure 1.37.

(iii) Capacitive transducer based on change in dielectric strength of the medium. Variation in dielectric strength can be realized in practice only when the characteristic of the material to be filled in between the conducting surfaces becomes one variable. For instance, when a non-conducting material like coal is put into a hopper, the dielectric strength of the system changes as the coal fills up the hopper. In this way, measurement of coal quantity in terms of level of the hopper can be done.

Advantages. (i) Capacitive transducer is very sensitive. (ii) In this case, due to high input impedance, loading effect is minimized. (iii) Resolution is high. (iv) It consumes less power. (v) Its performance is more reliable where inductive transducers are ineffective due to excessive interference of stray magnetic field.

Disadvantages. (i) Output impedance of a capacitive transducer is very high which results in loading effect. The effect of this can be minimized by keeping the insulation resistance of the cable very high. (ii) Surface capacitance contributes to the non-linearity, however, use of guard rings can minimize this effect. (iii) Stray capacitance also affects the measurement. This, however, can be minimized by proper earthing. (iv) All cables have capacitance. The effect of cable capacitance is not so alarming in other systems. But in capacitive transducer, effect of cable capacitance becomes pronounced and contributes to the measurement error significantly due to loading effect. To minimize this effect, cable having lower capacitance value should be employed. (v) The most peculiar thing of capacitive transducer is that its performance is affected by dust, moisture, elevated temperature, etc. That is why, the system becomes very complicated.

1.6.9 Piezoelectric Transducer Element

There are certain crystalline materials available in nature, which exhibits the property of inducing electric charges on application of mechanical force on them. They actually get deformed under applied forces. In this line of materials, quartz and Rochelle salt are two very important naturally occurring piezoelectric materials. Certain artificial materials, e.g., lithium sulphate, ethylene diamine tartarate also have piezoelectric property.

Induction of electric charge, as a result of deformation of piezoelectric material under applied forces Fa appears as voltage across the electrode as shown in Figure 1.38. The induced voltage Ve is given by Ve = qe/C, where qe is the charge induced and C is the capacitance of the piezoelectric material placed between the electrodes.

Ve = qe / C ...(1.28)

The piezoelectric effect is directional. This means if direction of applied force Fa changes, the polarity of induced voltage also gets changed. Therefore, if a tensile force induces a voltage of one particular polarity then a compressive force shall induce voltage of opposite polarity. Thus it is concluded that the magnitude and polarity of voltage Ve produced by electric charge qe shall be proportional to magnitude and polarity of applied force Fa.

The relation between applied force Fa and deformation Δy can be given by Fa = (E S/y) Δy, where E is Youngs modulus of elasticity, S and y are surface area and thickness respectively of the piezoelectric substance.

Fa = (E · S / y) · Δy ...(1.29)

Since E = (Fa/S)/(Δy/y) = Fa·y/(Δy·S), and qe = Kc Fa where Kc is charge sensitivity constant of a particular portion under discussion of the piezoelectric substance. By using expressions 1.29, 1.30 and 1.31, we obtain a general relation of qe given by qe = (Kc E S/y) Δy and by applying expression (1.28), we get the relation for Ve as Ve = Kc E S Δy / (y C) = Kc E Δy / ε, where C = ε S/y and ε = εo εr.

Ve = Kc · E · Δy / ε ...(1.33)

Kv = Ea / σ ...(1.36)

1.6.10 Hall Effect Transducer Element

1.6.11 Optical Conversion Element

This type of transducer element is called optoelectric transducer element or optical transducer. The optical transducer converts light energy into proportional electric current. The light photons fall onto the p-type semiconductor surface and conduction takes place with respect to the n-type semiconductor surface placed opposite to each other. On this basic principle, there are four types of optical transducer elements that are popularly in use:

  • (i) Photovoltaic cell,
  • (ii) Photocell
  • (iii) Photodiode
  • (iv) Phototransistor

(i) Photovoltaic cell

Light energy particularly in the range of 0.4 to 0.8 micron wavelength when incident upon a properly designed and fabricated photovoltaic cell, electric current is produced. Figure 1.41(a) shows a design of typical photovoltaic cell fabricated from a combination of p-type and n-type semiconductor material.

The top layer of the cell is a glass cover to protect the cell from dust and moisture. The next layer is a transparent adhesive for holding the glass cover. The layer of anti-reflection coating prevents the reflection of light. The p-type and n-type semiconductor layers are placed in between metallic contacts as shown in Figure 1.41(a). These two metallic contacts collect the output. When the light is incident, the photons generate minority carriers, i.e. electrons in p-type layer and holes in n-type layers. These carriers freely cross the junction.

In the circuit model Figure 1.41(b) Ie is the major component of the current generated by the incident light. Ic is the dark current that flows even in the absence of any incident light and is related to an invisible current It generated thermally which tends to flow in the reverse direction. With Vt taken as available terminal voltage, the current Il is established through load, which is given by:

Il = Ie − Ic − It · [exp(e·Vt/λ) − 1] ...(1.39)

where e is the charge on electron and λ is the wavelength. When Vt = 0 (a short circuit condition) Isc = Ie. When Il = 0 (an open circuit condition), Voc = (λ/e) log(1 + Ie/It).

Isc = Ie ...(1.40)
Voc = (λ/e) · log(1 + Ie/It) ...(1.41)

Figure 1.41(c) shows output current with respect to intensity of incident light of different internal resistances of cells. The photovoltaic cells are effectively responsive upto a maximum of 125 Celcius only. The two most important photovoltaic cell elements are selenium and silicon.

Use: In addition to be used as transducer elements, these can be used in application where battery or other forms of supply systems are not advantageous to use. For instance provision of supply system for spacecraft where sun rays are available in abundance and therefore, solar cell is used to convert solar energy to electrical one.

(ii) Photocell or photoconductive cell

The basic principle of photocell is that when light is incident upon some typical semiconductor materials like cadmium sulphide or cadmium selenide, their resistance decreases as the intensity of light increases. A D.C. supply system is invariably employed in conjunction with photoconductive cell to make it conductive and that is why these are called photoconductive cells as well.

There is a peculiarity found with the photocell that if the photocell is kept in dark, the resistance attains a tremendously high value of the order of 10¹² ohms. Afterwards when the same is put in the vicinity of light, the resistance goes on decreasing. The decrease of resistance depends upon physical dimension as well as geometric shape in addition to the intensity of light. During conduction, the current flowing is only a few milliampere.

Sensitivity of a photocell can be defined as the change in resistance per unit change in incident light. When it is compared from dark to normal incident light, the resistance change for dark to light has been observed as high as 1000 : 1. The disadvantage of the photo cells is that these are not used in analog application.

(iii) Photodiode

The peculiar behaviour of a reverse biased diode in the presence of increased incident light gave birth to a new type of diode called photodiode. When there is no light, the photodiode allows a small amount of reverse saturation current due to thermally generated electron-hole pairs. The number of these minority carriers depends upon the intensity of incident light on the PN junction. Figure 1.42 shows a photodiode connected to a circuit. When the intensity of incident light is increased, the current increases as shown by the characteristic curves in Figure 1.43.

Therefore, it is the light intensity that makes the photodiode more conductive even if the reverse resistance of the junction is very high. The dark current that continuously flows whenever there is no light in the vicinity of the photodiode is called leakage current.

Advantages of (i) Linearity is achieved successfully over practical region of operation. (ii) Since the noise-pickup is less, SNR (signal to noise ratio) is higher. (iii) Better frequency response is achieved. Disadvantages (i) If temperature of the environment is increased by any means, the value of dark current increases. (ii) Supply battery power is very much required for its operation. (iii) In case of poor intensity of incident light, current flow is lowered and as a result amplifier shall be needed for suitable amplification.

(iv) Phototransistor

Phototransistor is just a junction transistor in which base-emitter junction (P-N) is allowed to be exposed to incident light as shown in Figure 1.44. The junction is covered with glass to allow light to incident upon. When the light is incident upon the junction, large amount of photons generate large amount of electrons hole pairs. The electrons move toward collector and the holes move towards the emitter. The electron flow constitutes phototransistor current. The base current to the base-emitter circuit is supplied by incident light energy.

Physically, phototransistor seems just like photodiode, but they differ in sensitivity. The sensitivity of the phototransistor is larger than photodiode.

Advantages (i) Power consumption is low. (ii) Response is faster. (iii) Operation life is longer. (iv) Higher gain is achievable. (v) These can be used in digital application. Disadvantages The only disadvantage of photo-transistor is the difference between rise-time (dark-to-light) and fall-time (light-to-dark) in the response, the rise-time being 1/10 of the fall time.

1.6.12 Fiber Optic Sensor Element for Temperature

The technology of optical fibre temperature sensing system is a combination of fibre optic transmission system and photo detection system. However, thermometry is based upon Plancks theory of black body radiation. For this, the black body cavity is formed by sputtering a thin film of iridium onto a thin crystal of sapphire. The energy radiated in this way shall be the characteristics of the temperature to which the cavity is exposed. This radiant energy in the form of light is transmitted through the fibre optic cabling to a photo detection system.

The above technique has been successfully used for the range of measurement from 500C to 2000C.

Advantages (i) This measurement system is better than thermocouple measurement system. (ii) Achievable accuracy is as high as 0.0025%. (iii) By this method temperature variations can be measured more accurately. (iv) It is insensitive to electromagnetic interferences. (v) Longer life since there is no thermal ageing.

Commercial availability. Commercial units complete with temperature sensor, optical detector and signal processor is available. The system can be interfaced to a process computer through IEEE488 or RS232C buses as well.

1.7 Secondary Transducer Element

As we have indicated on earlier references, the secondary transducing elements are those that convert the intermediate output available from the primary transducing elements into suitable form for indication/recording/control purposes on receiver side. As such there can be five different categories of secondary transducing systems:

  • (i) Pneumatic flapper-nozzle system
  • (ii) Pneumatic-electric conversion system
  • (iii) Electro-pneumatic conversion system
  • (iv) Wheatstone bridge-RTD system
  • (v) Potentiometer-thermocouple system.

1.7.1 Flapper-nozzle System

Figure 1.46 shows a conventional flapper-nozzle system, which is essentially pneumatic-operated. The force applied by the measurement input through process/measurement bellow is restrained by the force generated by air pressure through feedback bellow. The overall motion of the flapper being required in this system is very little (0.0254 millimetre). The 1.4 kg/cm2 pneumatic pressure is applied to the nozzle through orifice restrictor as shown. This air constantly bleeds through the nozzle. The measurement input applies force to the flapper, which caps the nozzle. This action generates back-pressure through the office restrictor. This is the resulting output of the flapper-nozzle system, which is proportional to measurement input and can be applied to operate an indication or other systems. Simultaneously, this output is fed to feedback system as well to restore the balance of the flapper-nozzle system.

The diameter of the nozzle happens to be from 1.5 to 2.5 times greater than the size of orifice restrictor, which helps in generating sufficient back pressure. With 1.4 kg/cm2 pneumatic supply, an output of 0.2 to 1.0 kg/cm2 can be easily generated through the flapper-nozzle system. The diagram shows the graph of output vs. flapper-nozzle gap. It is clear that perfect linearity is achieved between 0.5 kg/cm2 and 1.0 kg/cm2 and an approximate linearity is obtained between 0.2 and 1.0 kg/cm2. That is why, this range of output can be accepted for all practical purposes. Range of span in terms of pneumatic output shall be 1.0 − 0.2 = 0.8 kg/cm2.

1.7.2 Pneumatic-electric Conversion System

This transducer converts the measurement input which is in the form of 0.2 to 1.0 kg/cm2 (pneumatic) to electrical output (in the form of 4 to 20 mAmps. D.C.). Though this requirement of the instrument is on very rare occasions, yet it needs discussion on academic grounds.

The diagram shows one of such secondary transducers. A change in pneumatic input signal (0.2 to 1.0 kg/cm2) brings change in air gap of the detector assembly. The detector assembly is made of two pieces of ferrite material as shown in the Figure 1.48. The changes in the air gap of the detector assembly change the inductance of the oscillator circuit resulting in change of output current (4 to 20 mAmps. D.C.). In addition to the output to be fed for indication, this current is fed to feedback system as well to restore the equilibrium of the force beam at new operating condition. This system is used to obtain proportional electrical signal in place of available pneumatic signal which is in the form of 0.2 to 1.0 kg/cm2.

1.7.3 Electro-pneumatic Conversion System

As we have observed during earlier discussion that a 4 to 20 mAmps. D.C signal can be converted into force by passing the current signal through a coil placed in a magnetic field. This principle is utilized in converting the electrical signal to pneumatic form.

As shown in Figure 1.49, the current carrying coil is placed to move within a permanent magnet system. When a 4 to 20 mAmps. current signal is passed through the coil, a displacement in the coil is experienced. The entire system is connected through a pneumatic flapper-nozzle system, which generates output in terms of 0.2 to 1.0 kg/cm2. This output signal is used generally for operating final control element placed in a closed loop control system.

1.7.4 Wheatsone Bridge-RTD System

As described earlier, a change in resistance in RTD (resistance temperature detector) due to temperature change is sensed by Wheatstone bridge. The out of balance current of a Wheatstone bridge-RTD combined system is suitably amplified and a measurement signal in terms of 4 to 20 mAmps. D.C. can be obtained. This current shall be proportional to change in resistance experienced in RTD due to temperature change.

The output signal can be used to balance the bridge by means of a servomotor arrangement. The servomotor shall vary the resistance Rb for balancing the bridge when the moving/sliding system placed onto Rb is linked mechanically with the servomotor. This principle of secondary transducing is utilized in temperaturing recording as well as controlling system based on RTD input.

1.7.5 Potentiometer-Thermocouple System

This system is somewhat different from the Wheartstone-bridge-RTD system. Here potentiometer is employed in place of Wheatstone bridge for measurement circuit. The input millivolt generated by thermocouple with respect to changes in temperature is fed to potentiometer measuring circuit. The out of balance D.C. voltage so produced is converted to A.C. voltage by means of converter circuit as shown in Figure 1.51.

The A.C. voltage obtained from the converter shall be used for driving the servomotor for balancing the potentiometer. In this way this system is just like self-balancing potentiometer system. Since higher voltage level is required to drive the servomotor, a two-stage amplification system (preamplification and servo-amplification) is the basic requirement of the system. The moving mechanism which has been used for balancing the potentiometer shall be used for recording the temperature as well. More details of this system shall be described at proper place.

Examples

Example 1.1. A resistance strain gage with a gage factor of 2 is fastened to a steel rod, subjected to a stress of 500 kg/cm2. If modulus of elasticity of steel is 2 × 10⁶ kg/cm2, find the change in resistance of the strain gage element due to the applied stress.

Solution. Here gage factor Gf = 2, Stress σ = 500 kg/cm2, Modulus of elasticity E = 2 × 10⁶ kg/cm2. Now E = σ/ε [where ε is strain] or ε = σ/E = 500/(2 × 10⁶) = 2.5 × 10⁻⁴. Since Gf = (ΔR/R)/(ΔL/L) = (ΔR/R)/ε or ΔR/R = Gf · ε = 2 × 2.5 × 10⁻⁴ = 5 × 10⁻⁴.

Example 1.2. A strain gage is fastened to a beam to measure the strain experienced by the beam. The system of measurement is a strain gage bridge as shown on the diagram. The variable resistance R4 is of 121 Ω at zero strain and 121.6 Ω with strain ε. If the gage factor is 2, find the strain in the beam.

Solution. Here, Rs = R2·R4/R3 = R4 = 121 Ω. When the beam is under strain, the new resistance value shall be given by Rs + ΔRs = R2·R4/R3 = R4 = 121.6 Ω. ∴ΔRs = 0.6 Ω or ΔR4/R4 = 0.6/121. Now the gage factor Gt = (ΔR4/R4)/ε ∴ε = ΔR4/(R4·Gt) = (0.6/121)/2 = 2.48 × 10⁻³.

Example 1.3. A capacitance type transducer has two plates of surface area 400 mm2 (20 × 20 mm) each. The distance between the plates is 2 mm. The value of capacitance of the set-up is 10 picofarad without any external pressure on the plate. Find out the change in capacitance when a pressure of 2 × 10⁵ Newton per square metre is applied on the plate due to which the distance between the plates is reduced to 1.5 mm.

Solution. Here area of plate A = 400 mm2 = 4 × 10⁻⁴ metre2, distance d = 2mm = 2 × 10⁻³ metre, Capacitance C = 10 × 10⁻¹². Now C = ε A/d [where ε is dielectric strength] or ε = C d/A = (10 × 10⁻¹² × 2 × 10⁻³)/(4 × 10⁻⁴) = 0.5 × 10⁻¹⁰ F/m. On application of external pressure, d′ = 1.5 × 10⁻³ metre. Hence the changed capacitance C′ = ε A/d′ = (0.5 × 10⁻¹⁰ × 4 × 10⁻⁴)/(1.5 × 10⁻³) = 1.33 × 10⁻¹¹ F. Hence, the new value of capacitance = 13.3 pf and Change in capacitance = 13.3 pf − 10 pf = 3.3 pf.

Example 1.4. A thermistor of 5 KΩ is used to measure the temperature along with a metre of range 0 to 60 milliampere and internal resistance of 5 Ω as shown in the diagram. The variable resistance Rr is set at 20 Ω and power Eb is of 16 volt. What is the reading of ammeter at 25C and at 75C? [Given resistance at 25C is 4 KΩ and resistance at 75C is 950 Ω.]

Solution. Here current indicated in ammeter at 25C = Eb/Req = 16/(4000 + 20 + 5) = 4 mA. Similarly, current indicated in ammeter at 75C = Eb/(950 + 20 + 5) = 16/975 = 16.4 mA.

Example 1.5. A low resistance LDR of 20 ohms operated at certain intensity of light is to be protected by applying a series resistance in such a way that upto 12 milliampere current is to flow at a supply voltage of 10 volt. Find the value of protective resistance.

Solution. Current passing through LDR = 0.012 ampere. Voltage across LDR = 0.012 × 20 = 0.24 volt. Voltage across the series protective resistance = 10 − 0.24 = 9.76 volt. Hence, the value of protective resistance = 9.76/0.012 = 813.33 ohms.

Example 1.6. An electromechanical relay is to be controlled by a photoconductive cell. The measuring potentiometer delivers 12 milliampere current at 36 volt setting when the photo conductive cell is illuminated at 600 lumens/m2 and is required to be de-energized when the photo cell is dark. Find out the series resistance and dark current. [Given the photocell resistance equal to 1.2 KΩ at 600 lumens/metre2 and dark resistance of photocell 100 KΩ.]

Solution. At 600 lumens/m2, resistance of photocell is 1.2 KΩ, hence current through the relay is equal to 36/(Rs + 1.2K + 3K) = 0.012 Amp. or Rs = 36/0.012 − 1.2K − 3K = 2 KΩ. Now dark resistance = 100 KΩ ∴ Dark current = 36V/(2K + 100K) = 36V/102K = 0.353 mA.

Example 1.7. A flat circular diaphragm of a metal has a diameter of 15 millimetre. Find the thickness of the diaphragm if the maximum stress is not to exceed 300 MN/m2 If pressure applied is 300 KN/m2, find the deflection at the centre for a pressure of 150 KN/m2. Youngs modulus of elasticity of the metal is 300 GN/m2 and Poissons ratio is 0.28.

Solution. Applying equation σm = 16 Pa d²/(3 t²) where σm is circumferential maximum stress, d is diameter, Pa is applied pressure and t is thickness, we get t = √[3 Pa d²/(16 σm)] = √[(3 × 300 × 10³ × (15 × 10⁻³)²)/(16 × 300 × 10⁶)] = 0.205 × 10⁻³ metre = 0.205 millimetre. Deflection at the centre due to pressure 150 KN/m2 = Pa d⁴ (1 − μ³)/(256 Y t³) = 4.74 × 10⁻⁵ metre = 0.0476 millimetre.

Example 1.8. A capacitive transducer with its plate separation of 0.1 millimetre under static conditions has a capacitance of 10 picofarad. If the change in capacitance due to displacement is measured to be + 1 picofarad, find the displacement.

Solution. We know that C = K A/d where C is capacitance, K is dielectric constant, A is area of plate and d is the separation between the plates. Now when C = 10 picofarad and d = 0.1 millimetre, we get 10 × 10⁻¹² = K A/(0.1 × 10⁻³) ...(i). In second case when, C is 1 picofarad, we get 1 × 10⁻¹² = K A/d ...(ii). Dividing (i) by (ii), we get (10 × 10⁻¹²)/(1 × 10⁻¹²) = d/(0.1 × 10⁻³) ∴ d = 1 × 10⁻³ metre = 1 millimetre.
