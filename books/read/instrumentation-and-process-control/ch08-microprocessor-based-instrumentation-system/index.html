8.1 Introduction

Conventional electronics entered the field of instrumentation and control long back starting from vacuum tubes and later on transistors in their original form. Conventional electronic-based dedicated computer was developed during world war II but soon the designer came to know the general-purpose nature of the wonder machine they designed. This computer and the successive computers that came into existence, later on are all based upon the innovative concept of stored program and data organization, which was called John Von Neumann architecture in the honour of the designer.

This is Von Neumanns classification of computer system, the other classification is called Flynns classification. It was the innovative effort of John Von Neumann who for the first time presented the Stored Program concept and described the general-purpose nature of the computer as well. Even today the majority of the computers are based upon this basic architecture. The only limitation of the earlier designed computer was that CPU (Central Processing Unit) was made of many components.

Then another innovative changes came into existence based upon the same stored Program concept altogether but integrated almost all the components of the CPU on a single IC chip (Integrated Circuit Chip). The complete CPU functioning was implemented on LSI (large-scale integration) IC chip and was called as Microprocessor. The design of microprocessor was a great step towards miniaturisation as well as more generalization. The great characteristics of a microprocessor is its programmability and that makes it a more general item. The characteristics of miniaturization and programmability put together was so attractive that almost all domestic as well as entertainment appliances were automated by using microprocessor.

The specific functions of a microprocessor are: (i) Data processing, (ii) mathematical operations, and (iii) logical operations. These three functions are very important because any problem can be solved by combining these three functions. Almost all practical operations involve essentially either or all of these functions.

As far as the field of instrumentation and control is concerned, microprocessor entered this field immediately after its birth. In fact, all three functions of microprocessor is utilized in instrumentation. Thus, we define that in any instrumentation scheme where a microprocessor has been invariably employed for carrying out measurement, display and control operations is known as microprocessor-based instrumentation system. As we are aware, there are three basic functional blocks which define the complete instrumentation system as shown in Figure 8.1.

  • Measurement systems: smart functions; remote calibration adjustment and other programming operations.
  • Display devices: programmable recorder and indication functions.
  • Control actions: generation of fast and precise control actions with programmable functions.

The measurement system constitutes the primary initiating operation of the system and the measured value is displayed or recorded on the display devices. For an open loop instrumentation scheme, this completes the function for a particular process operation. But for a closed loop scheme, the generation of desired control action (based upon measured display) and its incorporation into the process through suitable final control elements becomes necessary.

Use of microprocessor in measurement system adds smart features in addition to providing remote calibration options as well as other programming operations whatsoever required. Smart functions in a measurement system includes digital signal conditioning with the availability of analog signal transmission in 4 to 20 mAmps form. Digital signal conditioning includes correction in ambient temperature variation, linearisation and compensation in static pressure variations in the process. On the way of 4 to 20 mAmps analog signal transmission equivalent signal in the digital form also run simultaneously on the same cable without interfering each other.

Remote calibration option provides methods for recalibration as well as rechecking, changing calibration ranges from the operator interface located inside the central control room or by means of HART communicator from anywhere in the field. Programming facilities allow to program the measurement system for any operation, the system is capable of performing as such.

Use of microprocessor in display device increases the effectiveness of display as well as speed of response. In a recording system, microprocessor performs logical operation in addition to linearisation.

The architectural details of microprocessor does not include the peripherals like memory, I/O system, external bus, etc. When a microprocessor is integrated with above peripherals, they take the form of a microcomputer as shown in Figure 8.2.

When a microprocessor is integrated with memory (having stored program and data) as well as I/O devices essentially on a single IC chip and is dedicated for control applications, the combination is known as single station micro-controller. This microcontroller generates necessary control actions by the help of mathematical and logical operations after processing the input data from the measurement system. The controller output data is then processed and sent to final control element for normalizing the process disturbances by manipulating the process parameters.

8.2 Interface System Techniques

To make a microprocessor work practically in a co-ordinated manner, many supporting equipment or devices called peripherals are to be interfaced. Memory requirement other than that integrated with the microprocessor itself (in the form of registers) and similar other peripherals (keyboard, monitor, etc.) which makes the microprocessor generally operable in useful ways are not being considered here for discussion. Here we are mainly interested in interfacing the process variable instrumentation units. Before we come to the point of actual interfacing of process I/O, we shall see the basic concept of interfacing techniques.

Basic concept behind interfacing any I/O device with a microprocessor depends upon the set of instructions that have been used for data transfer operations. Interfacing of I/O devices can be I/O mapped or memory mapped. In general, I/O mapped scheme is superior to that of memory mapped. The I/O mapped scheme has 8 bit address to identify the particular device and only two instructions are used for data transfer operation, i.e. IN and OUT. Less hardware is required for carrying out such an implementation. On the other hand, memory-mapped I/O interfacing uses 16 bits address for identifying the device and many instructions are required for effecting the data transfer activity. Hardware complications are more in this system. Only advantage with this scheme is that the mathematical as well as logical operations can be done directly on the I/O data. This feature, however, is not available with I/O mapped scheme due to limited number of hardware.

In general, in instrumentation system all I/O devices are interfaced with the microprocessor on I/O mapped techniques. The IN instruction inputs the data from input devices into the accumulator. The OUT instruction sends the contents of the accumulator out of the microprocessor for further processing. The two byte instruction set used in this operation for IN as well as OUT comprises one byte of opcode and the other byte for port address.

I/O interfacing requires first decoding of the address. Many methods are available for this purpose, but the most popular method is decoding by 3-to-8 decoder as shown in Figure 8.3.

Here 3 out of 8 address lines A2 A1 A0 are input to the decoder circuit whereas remaining A7A6A5A4A3 (5 numbers) are enabling signals for the decoder. These can generate address for 8 different I/O devices. 8.2.1 Interfacing of Instrumentation Units

(i) Interfacing of transducers

A transducer under an instrumentation scheme can have analog signal alone as output or analog as well as digital signals both superimposed upon each other running upon the same wire. Generation of such dual signal output is possible with application of smart transducers. Since the microprocessor applications accept digital signal only, dual signalling provides measurement signal in the form of digital output directly and therefore, can be interfaced quite conveniently to the microprocessors. But the transducers generating output signals in the form of analog only cannot be interfaced directly. This interfacing shall be done, however, through an analog-to-digital converter (ADC). In addition to this, a transducer can have varieties of other process variable signal levels e.g., 0 to 10 volt; 0.25 to 1.25 volt; 4 to 20 mAmps; millivolt from Thermocouples or resistance temperature detectors (RTD); milliampere from thermistors, millivolt or microvolt from process analyzers and several others. To put all the above forms of process variable instrumentation signals into voltage form for making them compatible for connection to input port of an ADC, suitable amplifiers may also have to be used. As far as 4 to 20 mAmps signal is concerned (which constitutes industry standard process variable signal), it can be converted into voltage signal very easily in the form of 0.25 to 1.25 volt by passing the current signal through a 62.5 ohm precision resistor.

Interfacing of an ADC in fact is important and can be implemented in two different ways:

  1. Interfacing using polling
  2. Interfacing using interrupts

The simplified diagram on Figure 8.4 shows an interfacing technique for ADC using polling method. The ADC has one analog input line and 8 output lines for digital output to be connected to the microprocessor. The analog input signal is in the form of voltage. There are two control lines: START (ST) and DATA READY DR. Conversion is started by initiating the START pulse and disabling the tri-state output buffer. Completion of conversion is verified by polling. When the conversion is completed, DATA READY comes alive.

The address decoder creates three addresses 1, 2 and 3. The address line 3 sends START pulse. When the conversion starts, DR, i.e. DATA READY line is monitored by one of the digital outputs D0 through a tri-state buffer and address line 1 as shown on the diagram. When the DR is activated, the data is put on the output line and microprocessor can read the data by the activation of address line 2.

Interfacing technique for ADC using interrupt has been shown on the simplified diagram in Figure 8.5.

In this case also, the analog input signal is in the form of voltage having usual connections. The ADC is clock driven between 100 KHz and 800 KHz which can be any of the two: external or internal. There are three numbers of built-in control signals, e.g. WR (Write); RD (Read) and CS (Chip select).

When WR goes high, conversion starts. On completion of conversion interrupt goes low and the data becomes available at the output line. The interrupt line goes to microprocessor to initiate the interruption for enabling the microprocessor to read the data.

8.2.3 Isolation and Masking

Digital signals are used to operate low level solenoids for generating switching operations. At the same time, it needs isolation also, from each other because of difference in operating voltage level. This can be done by use of a relay system being of magnetic type or optical type. Low level solenoid is better operated by optical isolation as shown in Figure 8.8. The operation is simple.

The digital input (DI) drives the LED (Gallium Arsenide infrared light emitting diode) which emits light from it at input stage and operates the silicon N-P-N photo transistor at the output stage. The source current applied between collector and the base of the sensing system is used to generate current by incident rays on the base. The base current is directly proportional to the emitted radiation from the diode.

While the process is in operation some digital ports of a PIA may not be relevant and therefore, are required to be kept isolated. Such isolation is of temporary nature and is called masking. Masking is implemented by means of AND logic gate as shown in Figure 8.9. Here the data bit is ANDed with the masking bit. When the masking bit is 0, the output becomes 0 or can otherwise retain its last value as programmed.

(iii) Interfacing of control output to final control elements

Interfacing of control output to final control element is carried out through several stages. The control output from a microprocessor based controller is essentially in digital form. First of all, this digital signal has to be converted to analog signal output by means of digital-to-analog converter (DAC). The DAC output can be in current form as well as in voltage form. Since the instrumentation signal required is of current output form (4 to 20 mAmps), the DAC having current output can be directly connected to electro-pneumatic converter. The electro-pneumatic converter converts the current into 0.2 to 1.0 kg/cm² pneumatic signal for operation of final control element (the pneumatically operated control valves) installed in the process flow line. If, on the other hand, the DAC output happens to be in voltage form, another instrument called voltage to current converter also is to be employed. The output of address decoder in combination with IOW control line enables the latch unit. The data bits go to DAC and after conversion (considering current output from the DAC) the current output is sent to electro-pneumatic converter. The electro-pneumatic converter converts the current into pneumatic form (0.2 to 1.0 kg/cm²) which ultimately operates the pneumatic control valve (the final control element) installed in the process flow line.

8.3 MICROPROCESSOR BASED PROGRAMMABLE CONTROLLER

This equipment in short is called microcontroller as well because it has been configured around 8-bit microprocessor and has been integrated with memory and I/O system on the same IC chip. The microcontroller communicates through ASCII keyboard (input unit) as well as through LCD display device (output unit). In addition to the above, it can also be connected to PC (personal computer) for uploading or downloading of programs and also for other communication functions. The following hardware can be found in almost all micro-controllers:

  • 8-bit CPU (oscillator and clock circuit on chip)
  • 64 bytes EPROM (further expandable as well)
  • 8 to 32 kbytes RAM
  • 48 I/O lines
  • Serial Port EIA RS-232C
  • 16 bits timer/counters (three in numbers)
  • Real Time clock interface
  • ADC and DAC
  • Relay having NO and NC contacts
  • Optically isolated input system (if required)
  • LCD Display system
  • General purpose ASCII keyboard
  • Power supply system

The microcontroller normally operates at crystal frequency of 20 MHz having on chip oscillator and clock circuitry. The microcontroller IC chip is capable of addressing at least 64 Kbytes of external data as well as program memory each. The clock circuit is also on chip and is operated by crystal input at 20 MHz. Clock pulse for rest of the controller circuitry is provided by a two-operation circuit. Memory available for the microcontroller is in EPROM and RAM form. A portion of the RAM (hexagonal address scheme to be provided by the manufacturer) is battery backed as well in order to save the program in the event of power failure.

A general-purpose programmable I/O component for interfacing, the peripheral equipment to the system bus, called Programmable Peripheral Interface (PPI) provides the necessary programmability to the microcontroller. The functionality of PPI is system-software programmed and therefore, no external logic device is required. This can be used with any type of microprocessor based controller however. This is having three I/O ports of 8 lines each (port A, port B and port C). Port C is grouped into two sub-ports having 4 lines each (named Cu and Cl). Any I/O combination of port A, B, Cu, Cl can be configured by the software command. The port addresses have to be supplied by the manufacturer. In addition to the above, the PPI can be used for interfacing ADC and seven segment LED display devices as well. In fact the interfacing technique using PPI is considered to be superior to the methods suggested earlier in this chapter.

The programmable interval timer/counter PIT/C is another important equipment which is used for process control functions especially. For instance, implementation of time delay functions, rate generation, event counting, rate multiplication in binary format, etc. are done through software control very effectively by means of PIT/C.

Uploading of a program from the controller to an additional PC-based support/back-up system or downloading of any program from the PC to the controller is done by connecting the PC by means of available serial interface EIA RS-232C through appropriate software command available with the system. The EIA RS-232C interface is implemented through UART (Universal Asynchronous Receiver/Transmitter). This is a programmable communication interface used as peripheral device for command execution as well. This device transforms the parallel data from CPU to serial form for transmission and also transfers the received serial data into parallel data to be accepted by the CPU. Such connection of PC is, however, to be done through a terminal emulator software, e.g. XTALK which is easily available. The installation of XTALK package is done through usual installation procedure.

A process controller needs date and time also to be displayed on the front panel alongwith tag number, process value and set value figures. These are accomplished by a Red Time Clock (RTC) which is operated by a crystal oscillator at a particular frequency between 32 and 33 KHz. Interrupt facility is also available with the RTC by the help of which a particular control data processing or monitoring can be accomplished very effectively in process control operations.

An eight-channel ADC (monolithic CMOS based, conversion time upto 100 microsecond) having eight-bit output is suitably interfaced through address decoder. Through the ADC, the controller is directly connected to Analog Input Signal Port. An eight-bit DAC, having current output with settling time of 100 nanosecond is connected to the controller output. Therefore, the controller gives out control output directly in analog current form for operation of final control element. Since the DAC interfacing is memory mapped, any mathematical or logical operation can be performed directly on the output data. Such operations include split control action (for operating two parellely connected final control elements), multiplication of analog output signal by a constant (for operation of especially designed high capacity quick-acting ON-OFF valve). Any other type of manipulation on the output signal can also be carried out very effectively and conveniently.

For alarm operations which are based on abnormalities on process variables, two NO/NC contacts, one normally open and the other one normally closed are provided on the controller output board. The existing alarm/hooter system can also be hooked up with the available relay output contacts if so desired.

8.4 Bus System, Interrupt Services and Communication Protocols

Bus System

Data transmission has been discussed at length in the Telemetry chapter but, for microprocessor-based instrumentation applications, parallel as well as serial data transmission amongst their interfaces and between the transducer-receiver instrument scheme need more discussion. For this, we would like to refer once again to the diagram on Figure 8.2 of the bus-oriented microprocessor architecture system, wherein the microprocessor has been connected to the memory unit (having stored program as well as data) and to I/O devices through address, control and data buses. These buses are essentially parallel buses having a minimum of eight parallel wires for eight-bit digital signalling. For a similar scheme having sixteen bits, there shall be sixteen parallel wires, and so on. As shown, the data bus as well as the control lines are bidirectional for all three connected units but the address bus is unidirectional since the microprocessor is the only source of address generation function. The three buses mentioned above are system-level buses. The component-level buses are placed inside the microprocessor and does not constitute topic of our discussion at this stage. There is another type of bus which is called inter-system bus which supports connection of any multiple system through the microprocessor for communication by means of suitable protocols.

The system bus constitutes three very important considerations: input interface (providing buffering between source and data bus), output interface (providing latching of information bits available from data bus) and above all is the address decoding function which ultimately finalises the selection of the above two as to which input or the output interface shall be connected to data bus and at what timing. These are, however, performed by the microprocessor very conveniently by the help of a three-state gate (an input interface), a latch circuit (an output interface) and an address decoder circuit in combination with appropriate control signal, e.g. IOW (I/O Write); IOR (I/O Read); MEMR (memory read); MEMW (memory write) and CS (chip select line). These control signals are very important which ultimately accomplish successful data transfer operations. The data transfer operation usually takes one microprocessor cycle time and the requirement imposed by the microprocessor is that all the connected devices should complete their operations within this time cycle. This is called synchronous bus protocol, frequency of which is essentially dependent upon the speed of the slowest device present in the system. The difference in response time amongst various connected devices constitutes limitation for synchronous bus protocol. A handshake I/O control signalling system consisting of Data-Request (generated within the I/O interface of slower device) followed by Wait (generated by slower device to signal the microprocessor) is the obvious solution which is applied in this case by the microprocessor application methodologies.

The microprocessor generates necessary wait states if the allotted time cycle for a data transfer is not sufficient. On the other hand, in asynchronous bus protocol the Data-Request signal is part of control bus and devices respond with a Wait signal for transfer of data.

8.4.1 Interrupt Services

In normal case, a microprocessor usually runs through defined programs in sequence uninterruptively. In the meanwhile, if any process variable attains abnormal value which requires suitable action to be taken by the microprocessor for keeping the variable at its original normal value, an interruption on the part of microprocessor is necessary. Such sudden but important interruption is part of the real-time system design and is called interrupt services. The interrupt is implemented practically through interrupt service routines. Interrupt services routine supports all interrupts generated by various sources designed for the real time processes.

However, identification of a particular interrupt (if only one has been initiated) or resolving conflict in the event of multiple interrupt initiation, (based on a priority scheme) is important. Two well-known interrupt schemes are applied to accomplish the purpose: polled interrupt and vectored interrupt. A block diagram representation of multiple interrupting devices has been shown in Figure 8.15 alongwith IRQ (interrupt) and INTA (interrupt acknowledge). The microprocessor responds to IRQ through INTA.

In general, the interrupting devices are equipped with necessary logic unit for generating an IRQ. The status bit also accordingly is set for identifying the interrupting device. This is read by the microprocessor and after the read operation is over, the status bit is normalised for another interrupt initiation.

Polling is essentially a software operated process where the microprocessor reads one-by-one the connected interrupting devices status bit. First the IRQ enables the sequence controller for initiating the interrupt services routine which starts polling the devices. The microprocessors have designated vector to know the location of the interrupt services routine. The sequence controller provides the address of interrupt services routine from the vectored location and then automatically branches to the routine.

Another way to resolve the conflict amongst several interrupting devices is vectored interrupt system. Vector is in fact an address location of interrupt services routine which is to be executed actually. The interrupting devices place the vector addresses onto the Data bus using INTA. This is a normal way of recognizing a vectored IRQ. In another version of vectored IRQ, the microprocessor provides multiple IRQ input connection and have specific memory locations dedicated to each of the vectored IRQ. This design has been implemented quite successfully in modern single station, multifunction programmable controller wherein several I/O features have been integrated into the controller. The vectored IRQ is flexible and is fast acting but requires additional hardware and therefore, becomes expensive as well as complicated.

To assist the operation of interrupt services, a programmable interrupt controller is also interfaced with the microprocessor. This controller resolves priorities of several interrupting devices by software priority resolving technique (in case of polled interrupt system) or by hardware priority resolving technique (in case of vectored interrupt system). In this way the microprocessor is able to obtain the correct address of interrupt services routine and before starting the execution it saves and returns the address of the previous task it was performing into its PCB (Process Control Block). This is an usual method of operation of microprocessor wherein a running process is recognized by its PCB which is generated at the time when a process is about to start.

8.4.2 Communication Protocols

In general, a microprocessor instrumentation system does not use parallel I/O data transfer technique because of cost consideration. Moreover, the cable for transmission of data becomes bulky and presents handling problems. Therefore, serial I/O data transfer technique is almost invariably used for microprocessor based instrumentation applications. In serial I/O scheme, the data is transferred serially bit-by-bit (one-by-one). Since the data output from microprocessor based equipment is in parallel form, the parallel data is converted to serial form by a parallel-to-serial converter at the transmitting end and again back to parallel form at the receiving end.

The requirements for such an arrangement to be effective are proper coding of data, synchronization of receiver and transmitter, selection of transmission rate, selection of logical signal values, provision of hand shaking signal, etc. These requirements are well defined by different communication protocols designed for microprocessor based instrumentation scheme. The communication protocols define the details of physical link as well. In this way, the protocol details combined with physical link is called digital communication link. Physical link may be one out of several available options e.g. twisted pair cable, coaxial cable, modem on telephone link, satellite link, fibre optic link, etc.

Protocol means data packaging as well as directed guidelines which the communication link must follow for effecting the data transmission functions properly. Communication protocols also allow multi-vendor integration in microprocessor based instrumentation scheme. When only two digital instrumentation systems are attached to each other and the protocol does not have any other link, it is called point-to-point communication link. But when many digital instrumentation systems are attached to a common communication link, the link is called network communication link. The protocols also differ between these two communication link being simpler for point-to-point link and sophisticated for network link. The interconnection of devices on a network constitutes physical structure and is called topology.

In network link many topologies are in use. Bus network topology (Refer Figure 8.16); ring network topology (Refer Figure 8.17) and star network topology (Refer Figure 8.18) are more popular topologies for microprocessor based instrumentation scheme.

In bus topology arrangement, the data is seen by all devices but is acknowledged by the particular device for which it is meant. This system is very useful for multi-drop instrumentation network. This topology supports peer-to-peer communication which is very much required by the process instrumentation scheme inside the control room.

In ring topology arrangement, on the other hand, the devices are connected in a circle where the data have to pass through each and every device. If the data is not meant for a particular device, the device regenerates the signal and the data is tranferred to the next device. This process goes on until the actual device receives the data. This topology also supports peer-to-peer communication.

The star topology arrangement is a very old configuration and was designed to support polling type of network protocol. Here the master device (also called server device) is centrally located and all other devices are connected to this master device separately. The master device controls all communications to the connected devices. The disadvantage of this topology however is that it does not support peer-to-peer communication which is very much required by the instrumentation scheme.

The development of communication standards and specifications are governed by two international organizations. One is Institute of Electrical and Electronics Engineers (IEEE) and the other one is International Standards Organizations (ISO). The standards 802.3 Ethernet CSMA/CD (Carrier Sence Multiple Access/Collision Detection); 802.4 Token Bus (Token passing system on bus topology scheme) and 802.5 Token Ring (Token passing system on ring topology scheme) are three most important which are employed in various instrumentation schemes.

The ISO developed a 7-layer network model: physical layer, data link layer, network layer, transport layer, session layer, presentation layer and application layer. This model does not support any proprietary protocol but provides proper connection as well as communication amongst all networks whatsoever. The 7-layer model possesses proper functional documentation details for every layer to facilitate ease of communications.

8.4.3 Modbus

Modbus network protocol supports standard communication between MODICON make Programmable Logic Controllers (PLC) and other devices (upto 247 in numbers) connected to the system. We shall discuss PLC a little later. Modbus protocol access the network through polling. It does not support peer-to-peer communications. The improved version is called Modbus Plus which of course supports peer-to-peer communication but is not so popular.

The point-to-point (store and then forward technique) communication link uses Modbus protocol because of its simple master-slave polling characteristic. But unfortunately the Modbus network supports only one master as shown in Figure 8.19 (the bus based Modbus network). If it becomes absolutely necessary to attach more than one master (an usual requirement in many instrumentation schemes), it can only be implemented by creating separate Modbus network for each master to be attached. The slave devices, however, have to be multiplexed in this arrangement.

Another matter of concern with Modbus system is that the slave is not capable of responding unless polled by the master. Sometimes this poses limitations because certain important alarm initiating conditions can go unattended when it is actually required to be attended.

Therefore, a time delay is envisaged in such a reporting scheme because the slave has to wait for the response till it is polled in the coming scan. The master device sends message (in a data package format) with unique address for a slave device. The slave device sends back the response message to the master device. The master device also sometimes does global messaging (if so required for the operation) for the slave devices to which no response is expected in fact.

The message generated and sent by the master device consists of address code, function code, data and check code as shown Figure 8.20. The address code ranges upto 247 slave addresses. Function code is different commands generated by the master device for implementing different functions. A few of the important codes along with their functions have been shown in the Table 8.1. The check code is calculated by the master device based in the message format codes and then it is recalculated in the slave device for comparison. If match is found, data is accepted, if not, the data is discarded outright and an exception message is passed on to the master device.

For message coding two formats are employed: Modbus ASCII and Modbus RTU. Modbus ASCII uses ASCII characters for message coding whereas Modbus RTU uses 8-bit binary digits. Message coding with ASCII is easy but slow and inefficient whereas coding with RTU is complicated but efficient and fast.

The codes and their functions are as follows:

  • 01 — Read coil status,
  • 02 — Read input status
  • 03 — Read holding resister,
  • 04 — Read input register,
  • 07 — Read exception status
  • 09 — Program
  • 10 — Poll program complete
  • 17 — Report Slave I/D,
  • 19 — Reset communication link,
  • 20 — Read general reference,
  • 21 — W rite general reference,

An improved version to existing Modbus protocol is Modbus Plus protocol which has been developed for higher data rate and peer-to-peer communication to support advanced microprocessor based instrumentation system. This is a type of proprietary two-wire network protocol service from MODICON which employs token passing system. The data rate is upto 1 Megabits per second (Mbps). The most important characteristic of a Modbus plus protocol is that it conforms to ISO standards and therefore, any type of network connectivity is available through proper gateways. This characteristic makes this protocol very useful for advanced instrumentation system, e.g. SCADA (Supervisory Control and Data Acquisition System) and DCS (Distributed Control System) etc. A description of SCADA and DCS shall be given at appropriate place.

8.4.4 ABDH

This is actually Allen Bradley Data Highway protocol. This was originally a proprietary protocol designed for communication through AllenBradley PLC system, but is supported and well accepted by other instrument manufacturers as well. All types of network connectivity is available through suitable gateways designed and supplied by Allen Bradley. This protocol supports peer-to-peer communication as well. When referred to ISO model, it consists of only two layers: Data link layer and Application layer. Data link layer formats for device addressing, character framing, error check coding and network transmission. Application layer executes communication between network devices including packaging of device command and response generation.

8.4.5 ARCNET

ARCNET protocol was originally developed for office automation but later on it was modified to suit the requirement of industrial automation as well. ARCNET has been designed for data rate between 156 kbps and 5 Mbps with a data packet of 512 bytes maximum. It is a token passing protocol and can access upto 255 connected devices. It can be used for star as well as bus topology system. In star topology, the centrally located hub is connected to remote devices in point-to-point fashion. The hub actually functions like a repeater and limits its operation only for signalling the incoming data towards remote devices.

With reference to ISO model, three layers are prominent in ARCNET. The physical, data link layer and the network layer. The data link layer is consisting of media access control and logical link control. The media access control allows sequential access to shared bus system through token passing scheme. When the device receives a token, it starts transmitting the message. After completion it releases the token to the medium. If at all a particular device has no message to be transmitted, it transfers the token immediately.

A very good characteristic of ARCNET is that the token passing system is configured automatically and immediately when the network comes into action and goes on reconfiguring dynamically as and when a new device or station joins or leaves the network system. If sometimes a token is lost the reconfiguration process starts almost immediately after generating a new token by itself.

The modified ARCNET is a generalized type of protocol. In instrumentation scheme new transducers or display devices or process controllers can be added or removed from the system almost invariably. ARCNET adapts to new addition or removal almost immediately. Only changes come is that the network takes time to reconfigure to take up the change. The single station programmable controller uses ARCNET token bus network system for industrial instrumentation networking solution.

8.4.6 IEEE 1118

This is also a very important and popular protocol for serial bus, interconnected programmable microcontrollers, distributed I/O devices, RS 232 peripherals, display devices, transducers, final control elements and other control and instrumentation devices. This is a multidrop serial communication protocol most suitable for almost all instrumentation related works. The message consists of 128 bytes supported by a data transfer rate ranging between 50 and 500 kbps.

The protocol supports two different types of physical connections: Type 3 twisted pair cable and Type 5 twisted pair cable. The former which consists of two wires gently twisted together, is employed when compatibility with existing instruments is required. Data rate for this range between 62 Kbps (for an optimum cable length of 300 metres) and 375 Kbps (for an optimum cable length of 1200 metres). Type 5 cable, which has more twists per centimetre length of cable, is employed when the transmission media is noisy and cross talks degrades the main signal. This cable operates between 76.8 Kbps and 268.8 Kbps data rate. The coding method employed is Non-Return-to-Zero Inverted (NRZI).

The data link layer used HDLC protocol (High level Data Link Control Protocol, a very old protocol but still in use with modifications) which provides three classes of data transmission services: connection-oriented, connectionless and device control services. In connection-oriented services, first a connection is established between the two devices and then only data transmission is initiated (just like in telephonic messaging). In a connectionless transmission services, acknowledgement follows on completion of data transmission, else a retransmission is invoked (just like in electronic mailing system). The device control services provides necessary mechanism for proper identification as well as testing of the devices connected to the bus. In addition to above three services, broadcasting to all devices and group addressing services can also be made available by the system.

Other services provided by the application layer of the protocol which support programmable controller implementation also, are categorized as follows:

  • Device Control Services,
  • Memory Services,
  • I/O Services,
  • Process Access Services,
  • Bus control Services,
  • Process Monitoring Services,
  • Error Detection/Recovery Services, etc.

In short, this protocol is more versatile and more effective for instrumentation scheme.

8.4.7 PROFIBUS

The PROFIBUS (a fieldbus protocol) was designed according to German standards and conforms practically to only three of the ISO layers, e.g. physical, Data link layer and the application layers. The physical layer specifies twisted pair cable of 1200 meter length with data transfer rate between 9.6 and 500 Kbps. The maximum number of devices address is limited to 127.

Data link layer of PROFIBUS is different from other protocol. Here bus accessing is master-slave combined with token passing. All active devices are considered to be master devices and a token is established amongst these active devices. The devices with token communicate through master-slave communication method. For real time processes of instrumentation, token passing from one master device to another have to be completed within a time allotted for.

As many as four different data transfer modes are found in PROFIBUS protocol:

  • SDN (Send with no acknowledgement required),
  • SDA (Send with proper acknowledgement),
  • RDR (Request with proper reply), and
  • SRD (Send and request for data).

All four modes are important and are required for carrying out specific tasks. For instance, SDN is employed for broadcasting purpose, SDA and RDR are for master-slave protocol and SRD is to reduce the bus load when SDA and RDR in combination performs successfully.

The application layer is composed of two sublayers: LLI (Lower Layer Interface) and FMS (Fieldbus Message Specification). The LLI performs flow control, establishes/terminates connections and converts data link layer to FMS. Application process amongst devices can be accessed through FMS services.

8.4.8 FIP (Factory Instrumentation Protocol)

FIP being an open fieldbus protocol has been designed according to French standards and conforms practically to only three of the ISO layers, e.g. physical, data link and the application layer. In addition to performing serial networking for field mounted transmitters and control valves, FIP also manages distributed databases. Physical layer is specified to be upto 2000 metre of twisted pair or fiber optic cable length and connects upto 256 devices having data transmission rate between 31.25 Kbps and 2.5 Mbps.

Data link layer specifies a special method for media access which is based on PDU (Producer Distributor User) model. PDU model functions on broadcasting techniques like master-slave but have no address architecture for the slave. On request from distributor devices, producer devices broadcast the message variables without knowing the destination user. A user takes up only those message variables for which it has been configured. A bus arbitrator performs the job of distributor. Any FIP device can perform the job of bus arbitrator but at one particular time, only one device can be active as bus arbitrator. If a particular selected bus arbitrator device fails in its operation, another device is selected immediately by the system to operate as bus arbitrator.

The application layer provides system for reading/writing the variable values in whatever form it be may available. Real time selection of scanning cycles and proper co-operation amongst the different elements of the distributed databases are also performed by the application layer. In addition to the above, the proper sampling of transmitters, to be connected to the system and the final control elements to be commanded for operation are also performed by the application layer.

8.5 PLC (Programmable Logic Controller)

Basically, PLC is a third level of electrical interlock control system. First in its line have been conventional electromechanical relay based control scheme through which ON-OFF control actions or start-stop-trip systems or emergency shut-down schemes were used to be implemented in the process industries. A 110 volt D.C. control supply operated ON-OFF interlock system to control the level of an overhead sanitary water tank has been shown in Figures 8.21 (process drawing) and 8.22 (interlock drawing).

The interlock drawing of Figure 8.22 uses a 110 volt D.C. control supply. Its terminal details are listed below.

The terminal details are as follows:

  • 1,2 : 110 Volt D.C. Control Supply,
  • 1,3 : Low Level Switch (LLS) contact,
  • 1,4 : High Level Switch (HLS) contact,
  • 1,5 : Normally open contact of Relay R2,
  • 1,6 : Normally open contact of Relay R1,
  • 1,7 : Normally close contact of Relay R2,
  • 3,2 : Relay coil R1,
  • 4,2 : Relay coil R2,
  • 5,2 : Solenoid valve SV2
  • 7,2 : Solenoid valve SV1

The above ON-OFF control scheme operates between 20% and 80% of level which means when the tank attains a level of 20% the LLS (Low Level Switch) operates and opens the inlet filling valve through solenoid valve SV1. In an abnormal condition when water is not used, then if the level goes to 80%, the HLS (High Level Switch) gets operational and opens outlet drain valve through solenoid valve SV2.

The same interlock control scheme can also be implemented by utilizing electronic digital logic gates in place of electromechanical relays which shall constitute second level of the interlock system in the line as shown in Figure 8.23.

As we can observe, the interlock system elements based on the above basic concepts are scattered and are wired through flexible control cables. The disadvantage lies with the fact that such systems are prone to component failure. The component replacement and rewiring takes appreciable amount of time, which increases the downtime of the process plants. This disadvantage, however, was removed by designing third level of interlock system that is PLC as shown in the simplified diagram of Figure 8.24.

PLC is a complex, hard-wired compact logic control units. Only I/O interfacings are located outside the system. In operation, it examines the status of input at input port and generates control actions for processes or machines to which it is tied up. The control actions appear as output on the output port. The entire operation is carried out by a control program which is stored in memory. A programmable device is used to upload the control program into the system. This control program is scanned by the microprocessor on a scheduled basis in sequential manner. The time taken in examining the input, performing the control logics and executing the output is called SCAN time.

The simplified diagram in Figure 8.24 represents the example interlock control circuit described above wherein the low and high level switches (LLS and HLS) are wired to the input circuit terminal and the output circuit terminals drive the solenoid valves SV1 and SV2. The input and output circuits are controlled by the program of the PLC.

8.5.1 Components of PLC

All types of PLCs have the same basic components, e.g. microprocessor, an I/O system, a memory unit, a programming device and a power supply system. These components have been shown in combined way in Figure 8.25

(i) Microprocessor unit

The microprocessor is normally custom made and performs the mathematical, logical and memory related functions. It reads the input status and then executes the mathematical and logical functions based on the application programs loaded. Finally it generates output according to the logic. The application program and the data are stored in the memory. The application program consists of detailed program files, subroutine files and error files. The data consists of control program related data, e.g. I/O status, counter and timer adjustment valves and other constants and variables. In addition to these, the microprocessor has system memory as well for execution of control program, scanning of input and updating of outputs. The former memory is user based and can be accessed by the user but the later memory is manufacturer based and therefore, cannot be accessed by the user.

The complete operation cycle in one scan consists of Input Scan, Program Scan and finally Output Scan as shown in Figure 8.26.

In the input scan, the PLC scans input devices sequentially for a signal presence (ON or OFF state in the first instant) and the status of input so obtained are stored in memory in tabular form called input image table. In the program scan, the PLC scans the instruction sets of the control program and with respect to input status, generates the required outputs. The generated output is recorded again in a tabular form called output image table. External devices are controlled by the PLC, based on the data available on the output image table. Operation cycle time of a typical PLC system ranges between 1 and 25 milliseconds.

There is a Watchdog Timer (WDT) line programmed by the manufacturers themselves (cannot be altered by the user) to monitor the proper functioning of the PLC system. The WDT rung is a combination of a few NC (Normally closed) contacts connected in series and referenced to itself. The WDT rung is ON for one scan, OFF for the next scan. That means, it takes two scans for the WDT to complete a full cycle operation. The microprocessor monitors the WDT rung and is designed in such a way that if the WDT does not change its state regularly as designed, the PLC shall be forced to go into shutdown state (output OFF; Run Light OFF). This ensures that if there is a hardware failure and the PLC cannot solve a specific rung or stops scanning, a known state shall be reached with all outputs OFF and the RUN light extinguished.

(ii) I/O unit

I/O unit connects the PLC to the process variables on one end and to the final control elements on the other end. Input one received from the field transducer/transmitter in the form of physical contacts (simulating ON/OFF condition i.e. discrete input); millivolt from thermocouples, RTD and strain gages; milliampere from process variable transmitters; microvolt/millivolt from analysis instruments. These signals pass through appropriate signal conditioners before entering the PLC. Similarly, output from the PLC in discrete form actuates the final control elements through suitable control devices which operate at PLC control voltage. The operating voltage level of final control elements is higher than the PLC control voltage. The I/O modules together are in the form of Analog I/O module (containing transmitter signals in the form of 4 to 20 mAmps), Discrete I/O module (containing ON/OFF contacts) and special I/O modules which contain thermocouples, RTD, analysers, special communication module to communicate with distributed control system (DCS) and other PLC systems, interface for SCADA software implementation and for any other computer control systems.

It is the number of I/O counts that decides the complexity of a PLC structure. Starting from 32 I/O for micro PLC, it ranges upto large PLC structure having more than 1024 I/O. In between the two more varieties can be accommodated e.g. medium PLC having upto 1024 I/O and small PLC having upto 256 I/O. The micro PLC comes in package form and the rest come in modular form. In modular PLC, all modules are interconnected together. Figure 8.27, which shows the example interlock control system described in this chapter, indicates a packaged PLC whereas Figure 8.28 shows a modular PLC unit.

I/O modules are constructed on PCB (printed circuit board) and are installed on racks as shown on the modular diagram. These PCBs can be removed for maintenance work as and when required without interrupting the power supply. The modular housing is constructed universal type so that any I/O PCB can be installed on any slot. The back of the modular housing makes provision for interconnection of data communication systems.

(iii) Memory unit

Memory unit of a PLC system is two-fold: Operating System (O.S.) memory and application memory. The O.S. memory is generated by the system designer or manufacturer and is required for performing various operations of the PLC unit, e.g. control program execution, communication linkage with I/O devices and similar other functions. The application memory contains control program, data and I/O image tables.

The I/O image tables are bits in arrays for storing the input available from process as well as for controlling the output devices. The maximum number of bits in I/O image tables shall be separately divided between process input and output devices. For instance, an I/O counts of 1024 can be distributed equally or otherwise into maximum number of bits of 1024 only.

The content of O.S. memory cannot be accessed by the user at all and is not required also. But the application memory is user accessible. This memory gives programmability to the user where the user performs all programming for making the PLC usable for a particular application.

Memory capacity is related to I/O counts as well as complexities of control program. Capacity of memory unit is generally 64 K or more. Requirement of memory is first (generally) calculated empirically as 10 times of the number of I/O counts and this figure is multiplied by 40% more (a figure which is based on practical experience).

(iv) Programming unit

The programming unit is used to perform the programming related works on the PLC, programming constitutes loading of PLC software. Programming unit can be a dedicated unit or a PC based system. While PLC is in operation the programming unit is kept disconnected. It is connected to the PLC unit only during programming, start up and troubleshooting operation. A dedicated programming unit is normally used for smaller PLC unit only. For other units PC based system is employed as shown in Figure 8.29.

When a PC based system is selected for a particular process application, the software SCADA (supervisory control and data acquisition) can also be implemented for operating the PLC from remote location say central control room. The advantage of the SCADA software is that in addition to offering assistance in operating the PLC from remote location, it presents detailed graphic displays in the form of graphic panel for the process on the monitor. The SCADA software for the particular application have to be loaded on the personal computer for its implementation.

(v) Power supply unit

Power supply unit is installed in the form of PCB which takes 230 volt A.C. from the mains and converts it to appropriate voltage levels internally which are required for the operation of PLC. In general, three types of voltage level is required for the PLC implementation. 24 volt D.C. is used for input circuit operation, 5 volt D.C. is used for the operation of logic circuits inside the PLC and a 110 volt D.C. is used for output devices operation, e.g. solenoid valves, starters etc.

Power supply unit is designed to eliminate or minimize all sorts of noise generated by the A.C. power lines or by other equipment. They withstand severe atmospheric temperature changes inside the plant areas as well as the effect of humid environments prevailing inside most of the industrial establishments.

8.5.2 Programming of PLC

Proper communication with the PLC requires proper programming of the PLC. Programming of PLC employs a particular programming language. The control program describes instruction sets which are sequentially arranged for controlling the process variables for maintaining proper operation of the plant.

There are many programming languages for programming the PLC but Ladder Logic is the basic and the most popular programming language for general purpose PLC implementation. Ladder logic diagram resembles to electro-mechanical relay based interlock system and therefore, is easily understandable to every instrument engineer and technician.

The ladder logic diagram consists essentially of a number of instruments representative symbols interconnected by straight lines indicating current flow through the connected devices. The power source forms the sideways of the ladder logic diagram and the lines through which current flows are called rungs of ladder logic diagram. In fact, the ladder logic diagram indicates the required circuit for basic operation of electrical interlock control system and the associated wiring diagram indicates physical connection of the final control devices. The ladder logic diagram for the example interlock system for overhead tank level control has been shown in Figure 8.30. The low level and high-level control switches (LLS and HLS) control the operation of the electro-mechanical relays R1 and R2 respectively,

and similarly the NO (normally open) contact 1R1 in logical combination of NC (normally closed) contact 2R2 controls the solenoid valve SV1 and the NO contact 1R2 control the solenoid valve SV2. The decimal digit indicates the terminal wiring number of the diagram. A typical ladder logic diagram for PLC programming has been shown in Figure 8.31.

The basic requirement of the ladder logic programming is that with the given logical input condition, the required logical output condition should meet only when there is a logical continuity for energizing the output devices. In the ladder logic programming also, the usual three instructions are employed for implementing the program: NO instruction, NC instruction and OTE (output coil energized) instruction. The NO instruction asks the microprocessor to examine its assigned bit location in the program memory. If the bit is ON (indicated by logic 1), the instruction is logically true resulting in logical continuity through the ladder rung. On the other hand, if the bit indicates OFF (indicated by logic O), no logical continuity can be ascertained on the ladder rung.

Similarly, the NC instruction from the ladder logic diagram is in fact to examine OFF instruction. This instructs the microprocessor to examine the existence of OFF condition. If the bit indicates OFF, the instruction is logically true resulting in logical continuity through the ladder rung. On the other hand, if the bit indicates ON, the NC instruction is false and no logical continuity can be ascertained on the ladder rung. The OTE instruction is just equivalent to relay coil which instructs the microprocessor to set a particular memory location to ON (logical bit 1), if a logic continuity exists in any logic rung preceding it. In case the situation is otherwise, the microprocessor sets the output coil instruction bit to OFF (logical O).

In Figure 8.31, I and O represent input and output condition. The three digits associated with I or O represent word number and the rest two digits represent logic bits. These altogether represent reference address in the memory where the logic operation shall be implemented. In our example interlock control system, the logical continuity in rung 3 opens inlet water control valve whereas the logical continuity in rung 4 closes the inlet valve and opens the outlet valve.

There are other types of instructions as well in the ladder logic programming. They are Latch coil instruction (LI); Unlatch coil instruction (UI); One shot input instruction (ONS); Timer ON delay instruction (TON) and Timer OFF delay instruction (TOF) and several others. The programming of LI for a specific output is required if that output is to remain energized even if the initiating condition has changed its status. On the other hand, the programming of UI is required for resetting a latched output of the same address reference. ONS instruction is such instruction that is active for one micro-processor scan only when there is change in condition, e.g. false to true. Normally triggering actions are implemented by ONS instruction. The TON and TOF instructions are programmed to create time delay action. TON is for starting the timer action and TOF is for stopping the timer action. Arrangement is provided in the timer circuit for adjustment of time. After the preset time elapses, the connected circuit is made ON or OFF depending upon the requirement of the interlock control scheme.

The instructions outlined above are sufficient for four basic control functions of a PLC, e.g. ON/OFF control; data manipulation; timing and counting. For making the PLC perform more advanced control functions, more advanced programming techniques are required to be implemented. Advanced programming techniques like Function Block (FB) instruction; Structural Text (ST) language; Sequential Function Chart (SFC), etc. are capable of programming the PLC for performing analog control actions, complex file operation, sequencing and reporting function etc.

8.6 DCS (Distributed Control System)

Distributed control system functions in distributed manner. It consists of a number of microprocessor based instrument modules that work together in synchronized manner to monitor and control the operation of process plant as shown briefly in Figure 8.32. As is seen, the entire control functions of a process unit are distributed in the different microprocessor based instrument modules and these modules are distributed geographically in the entire plant area.

Figure 8.32 legend — Tx: process transmitters; FCE: final control element. Labels on the diagram: local I/O interface, bus, process computer, communication card, engineering configuration station, operator station, main plant data highway, controller files, I/O interface module, process operation unit, pH analyzer and oxygen analyzer.

DCS is essentially a computer network for process automation and does not function in isolation like office automation. It, in fact, performs real-time processing.

A DCS is connected to measurement instrument, e.g. process variable transmitters, process analysers, belt weigher, contact switches and final control elements e.g. control valves, stepper motors, starters and solenoid valves all located in the field side of a process plant. All process measurement signals in the form of 4 to 20 mAmps D.C.; 1 to 5 volt D.C. ; 0.25 to 1.25 volt D.C. and others are converted to digital form by ADC and then fed to the process computer. The process computer controls different loops; monitors input and output; executes alarm conditions related to plant operation; executes special logical function (if any); executes trending, logging and data reporting, etc.

Analog control loops generally involve maintenance of process variable at its set value. In some of the control loops feedforward, lead-lag, cascade or ratio actions are also required to be implemented. Discrete control loops involve process interlocking or sequencing like PLC operation. The DCS combines all analog loops, discrete loops and perform upon them independently and successfully. Being a computer based system, a DCS can incorporate any number of I/O, control loops and logic interlocks. It scans all the connected measurement input transmitters, analyses the input variables and alarms them according to the abnormality findings; generate control output and directs them to final control elements throughout the plant in synchronized manner, i.e. as and wherever required basis. It continuously re-evaluates the plant performance along with their real-time status and makes thousands of incremental decisions in only a fraction of a second. The above functions are performed by DCS quite comfortably because the system consists of independent instrument modules that are capable of performing their actions simultaneously by carrying out the various intermediate control signals from one module to other through real-time data highway (communication links). Fast incremental control adjustments and efficient operations allow more co-ordinated control resulting in consistency in quantity as well as quality of products in addition to maintaining optimum consumption of raw materials. In case of change in plant conditions, a DCS tracks the changes and through proper communication, data storage and retrieval, it controls the plant on different sets of conditions quite effectively. It makes timely and accurate decisions whereas human mind cannot do this. The expert alarm management system offered by DCS forces the operators to respond promptly.

Since the DCS is a computer based instrumentation system, software implementation is an important feature. For proper operation of the process plant, the DCS is programmed properly which constitutes uploading of process related information and control related algorithms. The operator interface machine must be loaded with required instructions for operating the plant. The complete software can be understood by putting them in three groups: Distributed operating system (DOS), system software for user and application software. Entire works of DCS operation, e.g. memory management functions, scheduling, supervision of I/O related operations are conducted by DOS. The system software support user functions, e.g. language translation, editing and debugging. And finally the application software which functions at boundary layer of the computer system performs reading operation of I/O into memory, generates control output based upon the condition of input, the set point and the selected algorithm and also does calculation for assigning proper engineering units to the variables.

As far as hardware is concerned, the DCS, in general, have following hardwares:

  • (i) Communication System (CS)
  • (ii) Controller Files (CF)
  • (iii) Process Variables Interface Unit (PVIU)
  • (iv) Data Highway Port (DHP)
  • (v) Preferential Access Device (PAD)

The hardware are interconnected together in the manner shown in Figure 8.33 or any other manner required by the particular process design for implementing advanced control systems for the process.

(i) Communication System (CS)

The main communication system consists of Plant Data Highway (PDH); Highway Traffic Controller Module (HTCM); Preferential Access Device Link (PAD-Link), Physical Link (PL) and Programmable Logic Controller Link (PLC-Link).

Plant data highway is the principal communication channel which allows data communication amongst all the instrument devices distributed along the entire plant area which are systematically connected to it. The physical link which makes physical connection to all the devices is essentially coaxial cable consisting of designed number of branches, approximate length of each branch being upto 5000 metres which is more than sufficient for any plant. Normally upto 25 devices altogether should be allowed to be connected to each of the branches. PDHs have dual redundant communication system, i.e. they have two independent coaxial cables (one primary and other backup) and separate data highway interface. A Highway Traffic Controller Module (HTCM) controls the complete traffic of the data and includes independent electronics modules (PCB) for primary as well as backup coaxial cables. The HTCM includes wiring for preferential Access Devices as well.

Data highway interfaces are in fact electronic card module (PCB) to be inserted in each device. The PCB has electronic circuit required for communication having data packaging function, e.g. address assignment, address recognition, data conversion, error detection, etc. The modules shall have coaxial cable connector also with a Tee Connector joint to facilitate parallel connection of other modules. HTCM is another PCB (installed in back plane) having a set of electronic cards for each coaxial cable. The connection inlets are provided in HTCM for each coaxial cable as well as for preferential access devices. The HTCM monitors and co-ordinates data trafficking amongst devices connected to the plant data highway.

Figure 8.33 legend — HTCM: Highway Traffic Controller Module; ECS: Engineering Configuration Station; OS: Operator Station; PVIU: Process Variables Interface Unit; BC: Basic Controller; HLC: High-level Controller; PC: Process Computer; DHP: Data Highway Port; MC: Multifunction Controller; Local Operator Station / Local Controller Station; PLC: Programmable Logic Controller; PLCD: PLC Data Highway; PAL: Preferential Access Link.

The various devices connected to PDH are classified as preferential access device (PAD), polled device (PD) and respond only device (ROD). The PADs are process computer, operator station (OS) and engineering configuration station (ECS). These are connected to HTCM through preferential access link (PAL) and enjoy higher priority for communication than any other devices connected to the system. The number of such devices to be connected to the system are decided by the design requirement for a particular practical system. The polled devices are process variable interface units (PVIU) and data highway port (DHP). PVIUs are connected to different measurement transducers/transmitters installed near the process whereas DHP is connected to PLC network. These units/network can initiate data transfer only when polled by the HTCM. The respond only devices consist of all the controllers, e.g. basic controllers (BC), multifunction controllers (MC) and similar other devices. They themselves cannot initiate data transfer rather they have only to respond to the action initiated by the preferential access devices.

Data transfer through coaxial cable is in serial form at the rate of 250 kbps in general and is more than sufficient for process plants. Command and data word length is selected as per design requirement (it is actually selected by the vendor in co-ordination with the user). Each word shall include codes to identify the function of the device involved and the memory location, synchronizing bits at start and BCH (Bose Chaudhuri Hocquenghem) error checking code at the end of the message. Each word is checked for data integrity. The BCH error check code is computed by the sending device and by the receiving device as well and any discrepancy in the data received shall be reported to the operator station after rejection. The operator station runs a diagnostic routine for keeping the record of errors and if configuration demands, a command is issued by the operator station to the HTCM which changes over the communication channel from primary to back-up data highway. Important features of the DCS communication system are error checking arrangement, provision of back-up (and also sometimes redundant) system and the devices isolation by transformer coupling at the data highway interface so that failure of any device shall have no effect on other devices.

(ii) Controller Files (CF)

The controller files are process controllers which are of three different varieties: (a) Basic controller (BC), (b) High-level Controller (HLC), and (c) Multifunction controller (MC). Primary hardware of these controllers are composed of modular controller cards along with dedicated termination panels installed inside the cabinet. Each controller has normally 16-bit microprocessor, ROM, RAM, data highway interface and power regulator circuits. The microprocessor executes instructions from the ROM and operates upon the data available on the RAM. The ROM contains predefined control algorithms and other controls as well as monitoring functions. The controllers implement complete autonomous operations because of their own hardware, firmware and database system. These are completely independent of other devices present in the main system. Controller actions for control as well as monitoring functions are user configurable. The user first selects a particular control algorithm and then enters the constants and the parameters required for the specified process control. There is a specified number of control slots (may be eight or multiple of eight) and the high level as well as the multifunction controllers shall have normally twice the number available in the basic controller. An individual slot is designed to perform any action as configured, e.g. PID or any control mode, any auxiliary algorithm (signal conditions, mathematical operations, logical operations, lead/lag compensation and others). In addition to independent operations, the slots can be configured to form cascade loops as well.

The controller is capable of performing specified algorithm and calculating required output for each slot, at least three times in a second. In addition to above, it also performs diagnostics for itself. Each control slot has two input and one control output along with two alarm outputs (one high and one low) as shown in Figure 8.34. Input PV is the process variable whereas input SP is the set point adjustment.

Figure 8.34 labels — Input PV, Input SP, High alarm output, Control output (CO), Low alarm output and BC.

Operational modes of the slots have provisions of Manual, Automatic, Cascade or Computer mode. In manual mode the input SP does not affect the control output. It is generated by the operator station. In automatic mode, the input SP is generated by the local set point of the particular slot. In cascade mode of operation, the input SP is fed from other control slot where it is manipulated mathematically. In computer mode of operation the input SP and the control output are generated by a process computer. Such systems of control are better known as Supervisory or Set Point Control (SPC) and Direct Digital Control (DDC). These controls can also be implemented independently. SPC is performed through single station programmable controllers but the set points are generated by the process computer and fed through DAC (Digital-to-Analog Converter) and DEMUX (De-Multiplexer). On the other hand in the DDC, the controller files are located inside the process computer where all the set points and the control outputs are generated. The control outputs are fed in similar way as SPC.

The different control modes described above can be switched from one to another bumplessly by the help of PV-tracking (process variable tracking). The input-SP is instructed to track the PV while the controller is in manual mode. When the controller is switched to automatic mode, the set point equals the PV because the PV-tracking equalises the two internally. For the cascade operation of two slots, Initialization Manual is required. This is done by allowing a primary slot to generate an output which the secondary slot in cascade operation uses as its SP input. If the secondary slot is configured for initialization, a back calculation is performed by the controller when the secondary slot is brought from cascade to automatic or to manual operation mode. During PID operation, the output of primary slot is manipulated for use in secondary slot. These balancing of output which is performed internally helps in bumpless transfer between the modes.

The high level controllers have double the functional capability than the basic one. Any sophisticated control algorithm can be implemented through different I/O configuration techniques. Status output signals which is the speciality of this controller can configure various interlocking control systems.

The multifunction controllers have multifunction operational capability and contain a large number of logic blocks. In addition to normal modulating control functions, it has other functions as well, e.g. monitoring control and sequencing control function. All these three control functions work independently and in integrated manner as well. These controllers can also communicate with other group of similar controllers if they all are interconnected through a local communication link (LCLink). A separate operator station is also connected to the LCLink for effecting multi-operation facet. This operator station is called Local Operator Station (LOS) as shown on the diagram. Operation of the control slots of these controllers can be supervised by the sequencing control actions on any other separate host computer as well. Actually, it is the programming which makes the controller to perform sequencing of process operation through vendor specific proprietary software. For development of such proprietary software, however, the process design engineers along with process and instrumentation diagram (PI Diagram) have to co-ordinate with vendors instrument design engineers.

The LCLink which is a multicore cable of upto 30 metre or so in length enables communication amongst the multifunction controllers. The LC-Link also gives access to the operating personnel to monitor and control the operation of MC as and when required from LOS. This link also gives access to MCs to interfarce with each others operation. Thus, the functions of MC is very complex and that is why a specific software of proprietary nature is required to suit a particular process operation system.

In DCS there are series of reserve controller files (RCF) as well which come into action in the event of malfunctioning of inline process controller files. However, the malfunctioning of inline controller is detected and reported by the RCF itself to the operator interface and thereafter the changeover takes place. The changeover is effected along with all I/O functionings, e.g. all control input and output are transferred to RCF and also all control related information stored in the back-up memory are immediately uploaded to RCF. The beauty of the system is that these operations do not require help of data highway since these changes take place at control loop level.

The operation of RCF is based on diagnostic routines, the result of which is monitored by reserve controller directory (RCD). It performs diagnostic run on its own circuit as well. If the result of diagnostic run reports failure of inline controller (RCF and RCD being normal), then automatic changeover sequencing starts systematically. In the same way, the high-level controller (HLC) and the multifunction controller (MC) have also their respective RCD and the operation of these are more or less similar as described for basic controller.

(iii) Process Variables Interface Unit (PVIU)

PVIU connects all process variable input signals to the system, PVIU possess a microprocessor, a memory unit (consisting of ROM and RAM) and process variable interface card. The ROM contains required operation program for the system, RAM contains configuration data (configured by the operator) for all the input devices, e.g. tag number, transmitter details, signal conditioning details, calibration range and also the address of PVIU. The system operation program scans all the input devices in sequence and stores in RAM and thereafter data is sent to preferential access device (PAD) on demand through DMA transfer (Direct Memory Access). If HTCM issues poll for data transfer, the PVIU sends latest updated data to the PAD. During all these operation sequences, the diagnostic routines are run by the microprocessor continuously and any problem occurring inside the system is immediately reported to PAD through data highway.

The PVIUs normally are divided in three groups for ease of signal conditioning function, e.g. High Level PVIU, Low Level PVIU and Low Energy PVIU. All these three units cover almost all varieties of process I/O signals spread all over the process plant area.

(iv) Data Highway Port (DHP)

As described earlier, the DHP facilitates interconnection of PLCs into the DCS. The DHP module contains microprocessor, memory (ROM and RAM) and data highway interface system. The DHP functions as communication module for monitoring and control of PLC. The DHP scans in sequence all the PLC connected to it and maintains record in its memory unit. The data recorded in memory unit of DHP is sent on demand to PAD. In general, monitoring and control of the system to which the PLCs are connected are done entirely by the PLCs themselves, but the supervisory roll is performed by the DCS for PLC operation through DHP database.

For inter transfer of PLC related data, the ports of DHP are connected to PLC local data highway through RS 232 modem. The two renowned PLC type e.g. MODICON and ALLEN BRADLEY use modem almost invariably because the length of interconnected cable being twisted pair type happens to be more than 15 metres.

(v) Preferential Access Device (PAD)

The PAD constitutes the main controlling agent for entire DCS operation. These comprise operator station (OS), Engineering Configuration Station (ECS) and Process Computer (PC). The main function of DCS is to monitor and control the process plant parameters in a co-ordinated manner. The OS contains monitor, keyboard, CD drive, card file and UPS board. The monitor presents graphic display of process unit operation system in its entirety in three different manners: Overview, Group and Digital display. In fact, the entire DCS is divided into several groups where each group covers almost all individual control loops with complete graphic display of process variables (PV) and set points (SP) in the form of bar graphs as well as decimal digits. The overview display shows entire system divided in groups and indicates trend in change of PV, SP and others. The group display shows one complete group specifically selected for visual monitoring and verbal reporting. The detail display shows individual system or control loop indicating almost all details pertaining to that system-control/loop. All variables of detail display are accessible from the OS as well as ECS through keyboard input and therefore, all modifications if required can be initiated from OS or ECS. Other special displays as per requirement of the user can also be implemented at the design stage itself.

In addition to the above, the following displays are also incorporated in the DCS:

  • (a) Real time trending of almost all process variables, set points and control output in the form of dot, detailed display and historical trending.
  • (b) Alarm recording in the form of tag number, time of occurrence, type, group number, last alarm, first trip alarm if any. First trip alarm indicates the main reason behind initiation of trip of the plant.
  • (c) System diagnostic and reporting thereof is a very important operation considering the hardware complications involved in the system design.
  • (d) Averaging, efficiency and other user-defined calculations are also displayed after calculation.
  • (e) User defined critical data, related to specific unit operation are also recorded on specified time intervals. These user-configured displays are operator interaction based and therefore, can be reinterpreted from time-to-time as situation demands.
  • (f) Some of the processes are discontinuous in nature, e.g. soot blowing operation in boilers for removal of soot formation on the outer surface of water tubes; condensate blow down operation in utility steam system, etc. A set of special displays related to these operations is also included in the DCS design.

ECS is meant for carrying out the entry of configuration data. Configuration program is uploaded into the OS. All standard data for operation of the process units are configured through the keyboard of ECS.

In normal operation of the process units, the configured value of set point and generated value of control output can be readjusted by the operator. Real time trending, hard copy printing of data on specified intervals are all initated from the operator station.

Procedural activities during process upsets (which have been envisaged during system design itself and suitable sequential operations have been incorporated into the system through suitable software) are configured into the operator station. But the upsets that could have not been envisaged earlier and hence not configured can be evaluated only on the basis of alarm summary details. In such situations, the operators have to initiate all the necessary actions at that particular moment.

System monitoring is observed by the operator station, e.g. status of devices connected to the data highway. The individual device connected to the data highway continuously performs self-diagnostics and any abnormality is reported to the operator station during scanning operation initiated by the operator station for the devices. The highway status display feature of the DCS indicate all details pertaining to the abnormalities when called up by the operator.

The process computer is capable of performing all operations that operator station is performing inline. This means the process computer can be treated as hot standby. In addition, the process computer is also connected to management information system (MIS). This allows management to see all activities being performed inside the plant. This also allows some instructions to pass on to the operator for implementation.

In general, DCS is capable of all advanced process control and direct management intervention as well as process optimization. Because of its high degree of effectiveness for the process control system and its ability to adopt to changing process requirements, it would continue to enjoy its importance in modern instrumentation system. Such system capability, the DCS has derived by implementing Artificial Intelligence and Expert Systems. It also assist the human operators during eventualities/process upsets and relieves them from tension.
