Basic Principles of Fuzzy Logic and Neural Networks

Wolfgang Altmann · IDC Technologies (Phiên bản 3.02)

Chapter 13 · 17 source pages · 8 figures · companion notes
Key terms. Crisp set — an element either belongs or does not; there is no middle ground. · Fuzzy set — an element belongs to some degree, and may belong to several sets at once. · Membership μ — the number from 0 to 1 saying how far an element belongs. · Fuzzy patch — the region of the input–output plane a rule covers. · Defuzzification — turning the fuzzy output curve into one number. · Neuron — the element that forms a weighted sum and bends the result. · Back-propagation — adjusting the weights in the direction that reduces the error.

Every chapter so far has stood on an unspoken assumption: that the designer can write down the process equation. A transfer function, a time constant, a dead time — and the controller follows from them. Chapter 12 went a step further and built a parallel model to cancel a dead time.

Some processes will not give you that equation, and not because you are lazy — because it does not exist. A clinker kiln, a twelve-tray column, a line whose feedstock changes with every batch: nobody can write their transfer function, and if they could the coefficients would drift within weeks of operation.

This chapter presents two ways out, and they are different in kind. Fuzzy logic replaces the equation with words: a good operator always knows what to do, they simply cannot write it as a formula — fuzzy logic is the notation that turns those “if … then …” sentences into something a machine can execute. A neural network does not even need the words: it extracts the rule from the data, and what it learns lives scattered across a set of weights that nobody can read back as a sentence.

Fuzziness is not probability § 13.4

The single most useful distinction in the chapter is stated in one line and deserves more space than it gets. Probability measures whether something will occur. Fuzziness measures the degree to which something does occur.

“There is a 70 per cent chance of rain” is a statement about a future event that has not happened. “The air is 70 per cent warm” is a statement about the present, which is already the case — it is merely described by a degree rather than a threshold. Confusing the two produces the classic mistake of building a fuzzy system whose memberships are really likelihoods, and then wondering why the output drifts.

Figure 13-1 is the whole of section 13.3 in one picture. The crisp set is a step: an element is in or out, and the boundary is a vertical line. The fuzzy set tapers, so the same element can belong partly and not belong partly at the same time. Everything else in fuzzy control follows from allowing that taper.

The one hard constraint: complements sum to one § 13.5

Fuzzy logic gives up very little. It abandons the law of the excluded middle, but keeps one rule that stops the whole structure collapsing into contradiction: an element's membership in a set and in that set's complement must sum to one. If something is 30 per cent cool it is, by definition, 70 per cent not cool. Without that, you could have a thing that is fully cool and fully not cool, and formal logic would be gone.

(13.1)μA(x) + μĀ(x) = 1

Note carefully what the constraint does not say. It applies to a set and its own complement, not to two neighbouring sets. “Cool” and “just right” are different sets, not two halves of a complementary pair, so their memberships need not sum to one. This is worth stating because the chapter's own worked example later appears to assume otherwise — see the translator's note on the 20 / 70 split.

Russell's paradox, and why 50 per cent is the hard case § 13.5.1

The Cretan who says all Cretans lie is not a curiosity; it is the reason fuzzy logic needs degrees at all. Classical logic surrenders in front of the paradox because the statement is both true and false. Fuzzy logic answers “half true, half false” — and then makes a sharper point than it first appears to.

A bivalent system can always rescue itself from a partial membership by rounding: 0.2 becomes 0, 0.9 becomes 1. The rounding is a lie, but a small one, and the system keeps working. At exactly 0.5 the trick fails, because 0.5 rounds neither up nor down. Half-truth is the one value a two-valued system cannot approximate, which is precisely why the paradox has no classical resolution and why fuzzy logic does.

Patches: the rule as a region, not a point § 13.5.3–13.5.4

The chapter's best idea is visual. Plot the input on one axis and the output on the other, and a rule stops being a sentence and becomes an area — a fuzzy patch. The patch is the product of the input set and the output set, and its size is a direct measure of how vague the rule is.

That framing does two useful things. First, it makes the collapse to classical logic visible: shrink the input set to a single value (21.5 °C) and the patch becomes a spike; shrink both and it becomes a point, which is just a lookup table. Fuzzy logic is a generalisation of the lookup table, not a replacement for it. Second, it makes coverage an engineering question rather than a matter of taste: enough small patches cover any input–output relationship to any accuracy you care to name, and you can pick the maximum error in advance and be sure a finite number of rules achieves it.

Five rules, one air conditioner § 13.6

Figure 13-2 is the chapter's worked example. Five temperature sets, five speed sets, five rules, five patches stepping up the diagonal. Because the patches overlap, any temperature in range fires at least two rules — and each fires only to a degree.

The example's numbers are 20 per cent cool and 70 per cent just right at 68 °F. Two things are worth noticing. The two memberships sum to 90 per cent, not 100, which contradicts the complement rule only if you assume cool and just-right are complements — they are not, they are neighbours, so the sum is free to be anything. And the remaining 10 per cent is simply unassigned, which in a real controller would be a gap in coverage and a sign that the sets need widening.

Figure 13-3 is the step the chapter describes but does not draw. Acting on the rules means clipping: the SLOW curve is cut down to 20 per cent of its height, the MEDIUM curve to 70 per cent, because those are the degrees to which their rules fired. The two clipped slices are then unioned point by point, and the outer envelope is the fuzzy output set.

Defuzzification then collapses that envelope to one number: the abscissa of its centroid. This is where the chapter's arithmetic does not close. With the two triangular sets and the 0.20 / 0.70 cuts it describes, the centroid lands at about 45 per cent of the speed scale — and stays between 44.7 and 45.1 for every set width from 15 to 25 units. The book states 47 rpm. The figure draws the computed centroid and marks the book's 47 alongside it so the discrepancy is visible rather than hidden.

The Achilles heel is the rules, and it is a real one § 13.7

The weakness the chapter names is not a technical limitation but an organisational one. The rules are written by engineers who know the application, and tuning them — together with the sets — is a long, manual, undocumented process. A fuzzy controller is only as good as the operator whose knowledge it encodes, and it will reproduce that operator's mistakes with perfect consistency and no judgement.

A fuzzy system does not know whether it is right. It does exactly what was written into it — including when what was written into it is wrong.

The consequence of section 13.7

That is the honest reason the chapter turns to neural networks next. If the weakness is that humans must write the rules, a system that derives its own rules from data addresses exactly that weakness. Whether it addresses it well is a separate question, taken up below.

A neuron is a weighted sum and a bend § 13.8

Figure 13-5 shows the topology: three layers, every neuron connected to every neuron of the next. The input layer does nothing but hold the numbers. The hidden layer does the work. The output layer produces the answer. Nothing in the diagram is more complicated than that, and it is worth saying plainly because the vocabulary around neural networks suggests otherwise.

Figure 13-6 opens one neuron. Inputs arrive, each multiplied by a weight, summed, offset by a bias, and passed through a fixed non-linear function. The function is fixed. The bias is fixed once chosen. The only thing that can change is the weights — and that single fact is what makes training possible at all: the learning problem reduces to finding a set of numbers.

(13.2)Ij = Σi Wji · Xi + Bj

The source's formula is destroyed in the scan — it appears as a scatter of subscripts — so the equation above is reconstructed from the prose that describes it. The convention is the usual one: the first subscript is the receiving neuron, the second the sending one.

The sigmoid, and why its exact shape does not matter § 13.9.2

Figure 13-7 plots f(I) = 1/(1 + e−(I−T)) at two thresholds. The chapter's list of properties — continuous, S-shaped, monotonically increasing, asymptotic — is a list of things that are true of almost any sigmoid, and that is the point it is making: the steepness and even the exact formula matter far less than the S shape. What the S shape buys you is differentiability everywhere, which is what makes the gradient in the next section exist.

(13.3)f(Ij) = 1 / (1 + e−(Ij − T))

Back-propagation is gradient descent, and the bowl explains the tuning § 13.9.3

The delta rule is the whole of training: compute the error at the output, propagate it backwards one layer at a time, and nudge every weight in the direction that reduces it. The chapter describes it as descending the gradient of a bowl-shaped error surface, and that image is worth taking literally, because it explains every one of the seven training techniques in section 13.10.

(13.4)Δwji(k) = α · Δwji(k−1) − η · ∂E/∂wji

Figure 13-8 draws the bowl from above. Real error surfaces are elongated, not circular: the curvature is steep in some directions and shallow in others. Plain gradient descent therefore does two things at once — it damps the wobble across the steep axis within a couple of steps, and then crawls along the shallow axis for hundreds more. The momentum term α is precisely a device for speeding up the crawl: it accumulates velocity along the direction that keeps pointing the same way.

Read the seven techniques in section 13.10 against that picture and they stop being a list of tricks. Re-initialising the weights escapes a local minimum. A small random push escapes a flat spot where the gradient has vanished. Reducing hidden neurons removes redundant directions that make the bowl unnecessarily elongated. Changing α trades crawl speed against wobble. Adding noise stops the network memorising the training set. Loosening the tolerance stops it chasing noise forever. Increasing hidden neurons, the last resort, adds the capacity the bowl was missing. Every one of them is a statement about the shape of the error surface.

What the chapter is too optimistic about § 13.11

The conclusion promises control systems that are error-free and cope with all variances, including the operator, until we finally have a “perfect” system. That is the one paragraph in the chapter that has not aged well, and it is worth saying so plainly.

Neither technique replaced the PID controller, and in regulatory control neither was ever going to. What actually happened in the two decades after this text is more modest and more useful: fuzzy logic found a durable niche in multivariable supervisory layers and in appliances where the rule base is small and stable; neural networks, after a long winter, returned as the soft sensor and the inferential model — predicting a quality variable from the variables you can measure, feeding it to a controller that is still a PID.

The judgement worth carrying away is this. Use fuzzy logic when an experienced operator can articulate the rule but nobody can write the equation. Use a neural network when you have history, a target you can measure, and no theory. Do not use either where a PID would do — and in process control, a PID does more often than the literature suggests.
