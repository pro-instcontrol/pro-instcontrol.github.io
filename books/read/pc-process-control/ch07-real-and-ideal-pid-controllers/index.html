Real and Ideal P.I.D. Controllers

Wolfgang Altmann · IDC Technologies (Phiên bản 3.02)

Chapter 7 · 8 source pages · 9 figures · companion notes
Key terms. Non-interactive controller — the ideal form, where P, I and D are computed separately and added. · Interactive controller — the real form, where a PI block is followed by a lead and the three terms cannot be separated. · Ideal process variable — a variable that has been modelled, statistically filtered and predicted, not taken raw from a sensor. · PV filter time constant (T_D) — the low-pass placed ahead of the derivative. · PB — proportional band, in percent.

The previous chapter drew the same controller twice and said that the second drawing — the lead — is the one that lives in the field. This chapter asks the question from the other side: not what the controller computes, but why one PID law has to exist in two shapes at all, and how you decide which shape you are holding.

Altmann names the two shapes by where the instrument sits, not by what the algebra looks like. Non-interactive and interactive describe the wiring, and the wiring is what decides everything else in the chapter.

Two names for the same law

The ideal form is the teaching model. Three branches, three gains, three time constants, and the student can point at each one and say what it does. That legibility is not a cosmetic property: it is why the ideal form survives in textbooks a century after anybody stopped building it into a panel instrument.

Figure 7-2 is the same model in algebra: the output is the gain multiplied by the sum of the three control terms, which rearranges into K times the bracket (1 + 1/(T_int·s) + T_der·s) applied to the error. That equation is what every tuning rule in this book is written against, and it is worth noticing how much it conceals — three time constants that a panel instrument could never set independently of one another.

The interactive form is what you get when the same law has to survive a cable. Proportional and integral are computed together as a PI block, and derivative is not a differentiator at all but a lead — a differentiator followed by a first-order lag whose time constant is α times smaller. You can no longer point at a term and say what it does alone, because the three actions interact: changing the integral time moves the corner frequency of the lead as well.

Figure 7-4 draws that arrangement, and Figure 7-5 shows why it was worth the trouble: the lead is a differentiator followed by a lag, with α the ratio of the two time constants — something a panel instrument can actually build, rather than the ideal differentiator that would differentiate the noise for ever. The cost is the coupling. Integral time and the lead's corner frequency are set by the same components, so tuning one moves the other, and that is the sentence to remember when a controller behaves differently from what the tuning formula predicted.

What noise actually does to a derivative

The claim in the chapter is that derivative action multiplies noise. It is worth being precise about the arithmetic, because the precision is what turns the claim into a design rule. An ideal differentiator has a gain that rises linearly with frequency, without limit. A 50 Hz component is amplified 314 times more than a 0.16 Hz component. Process signals of interest rarely carry useful information above a few tenths of a hertz.

The lead flattens that gain at α. With α between 8 and 12, which is what industrial equipment uses, the derivative stops differentiating somewhere above the corner frequency and the high-frequency gain settles at a finite number instead of running away. Figure 7-6 puts the two asymptotes on the same log-log axes; the gap between them at the right-hand edge is the entire engineering content of the word “filtered”.

Noise and instability are not the same fault

This is the observation I would keep if I could keep only one thing from the chapter. A controller output that moves too much is diagnosed as instability almost every time, and the remedy applied is a change of tuning. If the cause was noise, the remedy makes it worse: slowing the loop down does not remove the noise, it only makes the resulting wander slower and larger.

The two faults separate cleanly on a trend if you look at the envelope rather than the amplitude. Noise has a constant envelope and a frequency set by the electrical environment — mains, variable-speed drives, contactor chatter. Instability has a growing or decaying envelope and a frequency set by the loop itself, usually well below one hertz. Noise responds to filtering. Instability responds to tuning. Applying either remedy to the other fault is how a small problem becomes a shift-long one.

The filter is not free

A PV filter is a first-order lag, and a first-order lag is phase. Adding a filter therefore spends stability margin to buy noise rejection, and the exchange rate is fixed by physics, not by the instrument vendor. Every filter time constant you add is a time constant the loop must now carry on top of the process's own.

The practical rule that follows is to filter no more than the noise requires, and to place the filter where it does the least harm. Filtering the PV ahead of the derivative is nearly free, because the derivative is what needs the protection. Filtering the PV ahead of the proportional term is expensive, because the proportional term is what gives the loop its speed. Most field controllers offer only the first, which is the right default.

The KENT K90 as an implementation, not an equation

The worked example at the end of the chapter is worth reading as a specimen of how a real product translates the three tuning constants a technician can see — proportional band, integral time, derivative time — into three numbers a scan can multiply by. Three details generalise well beyond KENT.

  • The integral gain is proportional to the scan period. Change the scan and every integral time in the plant silently changes with it — which is the practical reason a controller that runs on a variable scan needs its constants recomputed each pass.
  • The derivative is taken on the measurement, not on the error. A step change in set point would otherwise produce a derivative spike of unbounded height in a single scan, and the valve would take it.
  • Both integral and derivative are normalised by the output range, so the same tuning numbers drive actuators of different spans without rescaling.
