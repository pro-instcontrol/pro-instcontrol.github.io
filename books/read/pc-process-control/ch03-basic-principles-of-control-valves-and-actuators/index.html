Basic Principles of Control Valves and Actuators

Wolfgang Altmann · IDC Technologies (Phiên bản 3.02)

Chapter 3 · 40 source pages · 24 figures · companion notes
Key terms. Control valve — the final control element that converts the controller output into a throttled flow. · Inherent characteristic — flow versus travel at constant valve pressure drop. · Rangeability — maximum to minimum controllable flow. · Distortion coefficient DC — the share of the total system pressure drop that appears across the valve at maximum flow. · Positioner — a local high-gain proportional loop closed around stem position.

This chapter is a catalogue with an argument inside it. The catalogue is the eight valve families — ball, butterfly, digital, globe, pinch, plug, Saunders diaphragm, sliding gate — each with its sizes, pressure classes, temperature limits and rangeability. The argument is that none of those numbers means anything until you know the valve's gain, and the gain is the one property that the installation changes for you.

Read it in that order. Sections 3.2.1–3.2.9 are reference material you will come back to; sections 3.3 onwards are the part that decides whether a loop is stable.

Two entries deserve a warning. The digital valve's rangeability of 255:1 at eight bits is real, but it is bought with cost — roughly three times a single-port globe of the same size. And the rangeabilities in the third column are all inherent figures, measured on a bench with constant pressure drop; section 3.3.3 explains why the installed figure is usually much smaller.

Ball

A sphere with a bore through it, turned a quarter turn. The plain port is a gate rather than a throttle: its characteristic is quick opening, and it is at its worst halfway through the stroke, where the opening is a pair of crescents and the flow area changes fastest. Cut a V-notch or a parabolic slot into the leading edge and the same body becomes a usable control valve — at the cost of the bubble-tight shut-off the plain port gave you.

The notch does more than soften the characteristic. On water the characterised trim tracks equal percentage closely; on gas at critical velocity the same trim drifts towards linear, because the flow is choked and the port area stops being the limiting quantity. That is the first reminder in this chapter that a characteristic belongs to the fluid as much as to the metal.

Butterfly

A disc on a shaft, and the only family that scales painlessly to a metre and a half of bore. At 0° the disc stands across the flow and seals; at 90° it lies parallel to the flow and the valve is wide open. Nothing about that geometry is linear, and the torque curve is worse than the flow curve.

Throttling is therefore confined to the first 60° or so of opening, where the disc still has authority and the actuator still has margin. Whether the shaft passes through the disc (aligned) or sits behind it (eccentric) changes both the characteristic and the torque, and so does the shaft diameter relative to the bore — which is why two butterfly valves of the same size from two makers can behave quite differently in the same line.

Digital

Not one orifice but a manifold of them, each element twice the area of its neighbour — 1, 2, 4, 8 and so on. Open the ports in binary order and eight on/off solenoids give 255 discrete positions. The characteristic is linear in port count by construction, rangeability is set by the smallest port, and because every element is either shut or open there is no stem to stick and no packing to leak. The cost is the solenoid count and the finite step at the bottom of the range.

Globe

The reference design against which the others are judged. A plug travels linearly into a seat, so the flow path turns twice and the body absorbs energy that would otherwise leave as noise or cavitation. Trim is interchangeable — contoured for equal percentage, drilled cage for low noise, multistage for a large pressure drop — which is why the family still covers applications that no other type can reach. The penalties are weight and permanent pressure loss.

Pinch

A rubber sleeve squeezed shut by air or by a bar. Nothing in the wetted path but the sleeve, so abrasive slurry and corrosive liquor see no metal and there is no packing to leak past. Rangeability is poor and the sleeve is a consumable, but on a slurry line the alternative is a valve that erodes itself out of calibration within months.

Reducing the port or coning the sleeve linearises what is otherwise a quick-opening characteristic, and a variable orifice inside the sleeve recovers throttling in the upper half of the stroke, where a plain sleeve has already passed full flow.

Plug

The oldest throttling geometry still in service: a tapered plug with a rectangular or round port, turned 90° in a matching body. The plug is held against its seat by the process pressure itself, so shut-off is tight and the valve is self-cleaning in viscous and fibrous service — the reason it survives in paper stock and heavy oil despite everything the ball valve does better.

The characteristic is whatever the port shape makes it: a round hole gives quick opening, a V gives something closer to linear. There is no trim to change, so the port shape is chosen at order time and cannot be revised later.

Saunders

A diaphragm clamped over a weir and pushed down by a compressor. The diaphragm isolates the whole mechanism from the fluid, the body is full-bore when open and drains completely, and the assembly comes apart for cleaning — which is why food and pharmaceutical plants adopted it wholesale.

The conventional design is close to quick opening. The dual-range version brings in a second, smaller diaphragm area over the upper half of the stroke and comes out close to linear, which is the difference between a valve that can be tuned and one that can only be switched.

Sliding gate

Two plates with holes in them, one sliding across the other. Because the motion is shear rather than compression, the actuator sees almost no unbalanced force, so the valve stays small and cheap even at 10,000 psig — the highest pressure in the table and the one place where this family has no competitor.

The characteristic is set entirely by the insert: a V gives something near equal percentage, a round hole gives quick opening, and a stepped insert gives a characteristic with a deliberate plateau in the middle.

Four more that do not fit the table

The catalogue has variants that are not separate families but answers to specific failures. The dynamically balanced plug admits process pressure to the back of the plug so that the two faces cancel, and a small pilot poppet does the metering: the actuator no longer has to hold the line pressure, only the pilot.

The diaphragm-operated cylinder valve puts the same idea in-line for high-pressure drops. The expandable-element valve replaces the plug with a solid rubber cylinder that is expanded or contracted to change an annular area — a pinch valve turned inside out, with the same absence of packing and the same temperature limit.

The fluid-interaction valve has no moving part in the flow at all. Two lateral control ports sit at the base of a Y; a small control flow deflects the main jet from one port to the other in a flip-flop, and the valve is switched rather than throttled. It is a diverter, and it belongs in this chapter only because it is the limiting case of the argument: the less the valve obstructs the flow, the less it can modulate it.

Gain is the only number that matters

Equation 3.1 is the whole chapter in one line: K_LOOP = K_C × K_V × K_P × K_S = 0.5. The 0.5 is quarter-amplitude damping. If the process gain K_P rises when load rises — which is what most processes do — something else in the product has to fall by the same factor, or the loop goes from sluggish to oscillating as load changes.

The controller can do that (that is what gain scheduling is), or the valve can. Choosing an equal percentage valve is choosing to let the valve do it: its gain is proportional to flow, so when the process gain rises with flow the valve gain falls to compensate. A linear valve has constant gain and compensates for nothing.

That is why the Driskell table in the text is worth more than it looks. It is not a list of preferences; it is a list of which loops have a process gain that varies enough to need compensating. Level loops and gas-pressure loops get equal percentage when the valve takes more than 2:1 of the pressure drop; flow loops stay linear because a flow loop's process gain is already close to constant.

Distortion: why the bench curve is a lie

Boger's relation is the useful piece of mathematics here. If DC is the fraction of the total system pressure drop that appears across the valve at maximum flow, then the installed flow is q = f(x) / √(DC + (1−DC)·f(x)²). At DC = 1 — a valve with the whole system drop across it — the installed curve is the inherent curve. As DC falls, the curve saturates earlier and the usable travel shrinks.

Figure 3.21 plots that family. At DC = 0.05, a linear valve is fully open by 40 % travel: the last 60 % of the stroke does nothing. The valve is not faulty; the installation has removed its range. This is the single most common reason a valve that tested perfectly on the bench controls badly in the plant.

The practical consequence is in section 3.3.3: rangeability should be computed from the C_V needed at maximum flow (minimum drop) and the C_V needed at minimum flow (maximum drop), not taken from the catalogue. The catalogue figure assumes a constant drop that no real installation has.

Actuators: force balance, not magic

Equations 3.2 to 3.5 are one equation written four times, once for each combination of direction and flow. P·A is the diaphragm force, K·X the spring, P_V·A_V the force the process exerts on the plug. Set the sum to zero and you have the steady-state stem position. The sign of the P_V·A_V term is what makes a valve 'flow-to-open' or 'flow-to-close', and it is why a valve can be stable in one piping arrangement and unstable in the reverse.

The simplifications matter too. Friction in the packing, in the actuator guide and in the plug guide is left out, and so is inertia. Those omissions are exactly the hysteresis loop drawn in Figure 3.23: the real valve does not retrace its path, and the width of that loop is the dead band the controller has to overcome before anything moves.

The positioner is the answer to friction, and it is also a trap. A positioner is a high-gain proportional controller (K = 10 to 200, proportional band 10 % to 0.5 %) closed around stem position. It makes the valve follow its set point regardless of packing friction — and in doing so it inserts a second, fast loop inside the first. The rule in section 3.5.1 is the cascade rule: the slave must be at least ten times faster than the master, or the two loops fight.

Sizing: the mistake that is hard to see

Section 3.6 is short and is the most expensive page in the chapter. The instruction is to size between 0.8·C_V,min and 1.2·C_V,max and not to use a valve outside its rangeability. The trap it warns about is summing every pressure drop in a constant-speed pumping system and handing the total to the valve: the valve is then sized for a drop it will never see, ends up oversized, and spends its life at 5–10 % travel where the characteristic is most distorted and cavitation is most likely.

An oversized valve is worse than an undersized one because the failure is quiet. An undersized valve simply cannot pass the peak flow — obvious, and easy to justify replacing. An oversized valve passes everything, controls nothing well, and nobody notices until the loop is retuned for the third time.

A valve is not a tap you can turn anywhere. It is an element with its own gain, and that gain changes with flow, with pressure drop, and with the way you installed it.
