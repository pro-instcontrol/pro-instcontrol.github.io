Fundamentals of Control Systems

Wolfgang Altmann · IDC Technologies (Phiên bản 3.02)

Chapter 4 · 17 source pages · companion notes
Key terms. On-off (two-position) — a two-state output. · Modulating — a continuously variable output inside a defined range. · Open loop — the control action does not depend on the PV. · Feedforward — action taken on a measured disturbance instead of on the error. · Closed loop — action taken on the error Err = SP − PV. · Direct / reverse acting — PV up gives OP up / OP down. · Dead time L — the delay before the PV begins to respond. · Lag T — the inertia, measured as the 63.2 % point. · Dead zone — the band around the set point inside which no action is taken.

Chapter 3 dealt with the final control element; this chapter deals with the decision taken before the signal reaches it. Four strategies cover almost everything in practice — on-off, modulating, open loop and closed loop — and each is right only inside a particular range of conditions. The chapter closes with the two quantities that decide the fate of every loop: dead time and dead zone.

On-off control: crude, cheap, and hard to beat

A switch is the oldest controller there is. It never holds the process variable at the set point; it makes the variable oscillate about it, inside a band set by the hysteresis of the switch and by the thermal inertia of the plant. That sounds like a defect until you compare the cost: an on-off controller needs no model, no tuning, no valve and no transmitter calibration, and it cannot go unstable. For a domestic boiler, a refrigerator or a sump pump, a swing of one degree is entirely acceptable and a PID loop would be an expensive way of achieving nothing more.

The design decision is therefore not “which is better” but “how tight does the band have to be”. Once the answer is tighter than the switching differential can deliver, on-off is out and modulating control begins.

Open loop: fast, and blind

Feedforward control measures the disturbance and acts before the process variable has moved at all. That is its whole advantage: the correction arrives at the same time as the upset, not one dead time later. Its whole weakness is that nothing checks the result. A perfect model gives perfect correction; a model that is 10 % wrong leaves a 10 % error that nothing will ever remove.

This is why pure feedforward is rare and hybrid control is normal. The industrial rule of thumb is that feedforward should carry the bulk of the correction — 80 to 90 % — and the feedback loop should be left with only the residue. Sized that way, neither loop is asked to do more than it is good at: the feedforward branch is not pushed beyond the accuracy of its model, and the feedback branch is not asked to fight a disturbance it would only see a dead time too late.

Direct or reverse: one question, asked once

Every loop has a sign, and getting it wrong is the most common commissioning error in the trade. The question is not “what kind of valve is this” but “when the measurement rises, what must the output do to bring it back”. If the answer is open more, the controller is direct acting; if it is close more, the controller is reverse acting. A tank whose level is held by the outlet valve is direct acting — more level, more drain. The same tank held by the inlet valve is reverse acting — more level, less fill.

The P, I and D terms do not care which convention is used, because changing PV − SP into SP − PV only changes the sign of the error, and the sign can be absorbed anywhere in the chain. What cannot be absorbed is getting it wrong: a loop with the wrong sign is a positive-feedback amplifier, and it will either saturate or oscillate the moment it is put in automatic.

Dead time is the enemy

Dead time is not lag. Lag is inertia: the process starts responding at once and approaches the new value exponentially. Dead time is silence: the process does not respond at all, and the controller has no information about whether its last move was right, too small or far too large. A loop can tolerate a large lag; it cannot tolerate a large dead time, because every correction is applied blind and the controller ends up reacting to a state of the plant that no longer exists.

The number that matters is the ratio L/T, not either quantity alone. Below about 0.5 a conventional PID will do everything asked of it. Around 1 the loop becomes sluggish and detuned, and the achievable performance collapses quickly beyond that. When L exceeds T, the standard remedy is sample-and-hold: enable the controller for short windows, freeze the output in between, and let the process finish responding before looking again. The algorithms are the same; only the integral setting has to be raised, because the controller is in automatic for a fraction of the time.

What the three parameters actually describe

A process response curve carries three numbers, and every tuning method in the rest of the book is built on them. The gain K tells you how far the variable will travel for a given move of the valve — and, just as importantly, whether the plant is fast and twitchy (high K, flow and pH loops) or slow and stubborn (low K, furnaces and heated rooms). The dead time L tells you how long you must wait before you are allowed to learn anything. The lag T tells you how fast the variable moves once it starts.

The dead zone is the fourth, and the one most often forgotten. It is deliberately built into on-off controllers to stop them chattering, and it is accidentally built into every mechanical linkage as friction and backlash. Either way it produces a standing error that no amount of integral action can remove, because inside the band the controller is not being told that anything is wrong.
