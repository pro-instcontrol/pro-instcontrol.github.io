Stability and Control Modes of Closed Loops

Wolfgang Altmann · IDC Technologies (Phiên bản 3.02)

Chapter 5 · 23 source pages · 11 figures · companion notes
Key terms. Stability — the closed loop shows no sustained oscillation. · Critical frequency — the frequency at which the loop phase shift reaches 180°. · KLOOP — the total loop gain, KC × KP. · Offset — the error left standing once the loop has settled. · Proportional band — the percentage of PV change that drives the output through its full range. · TINT — the integral time constant. · TDER — the derivative time constant. · MANUAL — the output bias, historically called “manual reset”.

The previous chapter built the mechanism: measure the error, compute an action, apply it to the valve. This chapter asks the harder question — will that closed loop stand still, and what do the three modes P, I and D contribute to keeping it still. The whole chapter is written around one example, a feed heater ahead of a distillation column, because that example carries all of it at once: lag, dead time and four separate disturbances.

Stability is a phase problem, not an amplitude problem

The textbook condition for stability is usually stated as two inequalities — loop gain below one at the critical frequency, phase shift below 180° — and it is easy to read that as a statement about gain. It is not. Gain only decides what happens at the frequency where the loop already inverts its own signal. A loop with a gain of fifty is perfectly stable if its phase never reaches 180° inside the band where it has any authority; a loop with a gain of 0.9 will ring forever if the phase crosses 180° exactly where the gain is 0.9.

This is why dead time is so much more dangerous than lag. A first-order lag can never produce more than 90° of phase shift, no matter how slow it is; you can stack two of them and still stay below 180°. A dead time produces phase shift proportional to frequency and unbounded: 180° is reached at some frequency always, and what you control is only whether the gain has fallen below one by then. Every tuning rule in the literature is, at bottom, a way of guessing where that crossing happens without having to measure it.

Why proportional control can never finish the job

A proportional controller is a spring. Push the process away from the set point and the controller pushes back harder the further it is pushed, which is exactly what a spring does. But a spring only exerts force when it is displaced. If the loop must hold a steady load — a constant flow of feed that needs a constant flow of fuel — the controller must hold a steady output, and a steady output requires a steady non-zero input. The error is not a defect of the tuning; it is the price of the force.

Equation 5.4 makes the trade explicit: the offset is inversely proportional to one plus the loop gain. You can buy it down by raising gain, but you buy it with stability, and the exchange rate worsens as the dead time grows. For a loop with a lot of dead time, getting the offset below one percent may require a gain that leaves no margin at all. That is the point at which integral action stops being optional.

Integral action: the slow, patient cure that can kill the patient

Integral action is the only mechanism in the controller that knows about the past. It accumulates error and keeps pushing until the accumulation stops, which means it can only stop pushing when the error is exactly zero. That is its virtue and its danger in one sentence: it will remove an offset no proportional band can remove, and it will keep pushing through the moment when the process has already arrived and the controller has not noticed.

The 90° of phase lag is the mechanism of the danger. Add 90° from the integral term to 90° from the process lag and you are at 180° with nothing else in the loop. This is why the rule “never use I without P” is not a stylistic preference: the proportional term supplies the restoring force that stops the integrator from overrunning, and without it the loop has no damping at all.

Integral windup is the same problem in its most common industrial form. When the output saturates — the valve is fully open and the temperature is still low — the integrator continues to accumulate an error it cannot do anything about. When the process finally arrives, the accumulated total has to be unwound before the output starts falling, and the overshoot is proportional to how long the saturation lasted. Every modern controller therefore limits the integrator, either by freezing it at the output clamp or by back-calculating from it.

Derivative: expensive, noisy, and sometimes indispensable

Derivative action is the only term that looks forward, and it does so by extrapolating the current slope. On a clean signal this is exactly the lead the loop needs: the controller starts backing off before the variable arrives, which is the only way to get a fast response without overshoot. On a real signal, the slope is dominated by whatever noise is sitting on the measurement, and the derivative of noise is enormous. The result is a controller that twitches the valve continuously and wears out the packing.

Hence the standard practice: derivative is used on temperature and composition loops, where the measurement is smooth and the process is slow, and it is not used on flow and pressure loops, where the measurement is fast and noisy and the process has almost no lag to compensate. The mode is not a measure of sophistication. A flow loop with derivative turned on is a worse loop, not a better one.

Tuning is a negotiation, not a calculation

The three constants are not independent, and no formula produces them from first principles. What every tuning method does is pick a point on a curve of trade-offs: speed against robustness, offset against oscillation, disturbance rejection against set-point tracking. Two engineers can tune the same loop to the same numbers and get different behaviour, because the plant changed between the two visits — a fouled heat exchanger, a worn valve trim, a different feed.

The practical consequence is that a loop should be tuned for the worst condition it will actually meet, not the cleanest one available on commissioning day, and that the tuning should be recorded along with the date and the plant condition it was done under. A tuning constant without that context is a number without a meaning.
