Tuning of PID Controllers in Open and Closed Loop

Wolfgang Altmann · IDC Technologies (Phiên bản 3.02)

Chapter 8 · 31 source pages · 10 figures · companion notes
Key terms. Reaction curve — the recorded PV response to a step in the controller output, taken in MANUAL. · Effective lag L — the time from the step to the first visible change in PV. · N — the maximum slope of PV, in per cent per minute. · LTC — loop time constant. · P-u, K-u — the ultimate period and ultimate gain, measured at continuous cycling. · Decay ratio P — the ratio of two successive peak amplitudes of a damped oscillation.

This is the longest chapter in the control book and the most practical one, and it answers the question every technician has to answer at least once: how do you arrive at the three numbers you type into the controller?

Altmann presents four methods and, notably, does not say which one is correct. Each needs something different from you — a reaction curve, a point of inflection, a period of oscillation — and each returns a slightly different set of numbers. The technician's job is to know what evidence is in hand and to accept the uncertainty that comes with it.

What tuning is actually for

The chapter opens with a list of objectives rather than a list of formulas, and the list is worth reading as a set of competing claims rather than as a checklist. Minimising the integral of the error asks for speed. Minimum wear asks for stillness. No overshoot at start-up asks for caution. You cannot have all three, and every tuning method in the chapter is a different answer to which one to sacrifice.

The distinction between IAE and ISE is the clearest example of the trade being made explicit. IAE counts every per cent of error for the same amount, however long it lasts. ISE counts a large error far more heavily than a small one, so a controller tuned to ISE will accept a long slow tail in exchange for never being badly wrong at the start. Modern self-tuning controllers work on ISE precisely because a plant cares much more about a two-metre excursion than about a two-hour one.

The reaction curve is an experiment, not a measurement

The open-loop methods all rest on one recording: put the controller in manual, step the output, and watch the process answer. It is worth being explicit that this is an experiment with consequences. The step must be large enough to be visible above noise and small enough not to upset the plant, and the chapter's instruction to record it on a chart calibrated in minutes and fractions of minutes is not pedantry — a strip chart calibrated in seconds makes the effective lag look ten times larger than it is, and the integral time that follows is wrong by the same factor.

Three numbers come off that recording and they divide the labour cleanly. The effective lag L sets the integral time, because L is the delay the integral has to survive. The maximum slope N sets the gain, because N is the process's own sensitivity expressed in the controller's units. And because derivative exists to offset what integral destabilises, the derivative time is always defined as a fraction of the integral time — a quarter of it, in every Ziegler-Nichols variant in this chapter.

Method (2), and why the tangent matters

The second open-loop variant is the one to use when the final control element is a valve, because it works from a valve step rather than an output step. The tangent through the point of inflection is extended back to the vertical axis, and where it lands gives ∆C — the apparent change the process would have made if it had responded at its maximum rate from the start. Dividing the valve step by that apparent change gives PG-u, a gain that already contains the valve's own characteristic.

The gain ratios in Table 8-2 are the same 1 : 0.9 : 1.2 as in method (1), which is the chapter's quiet argument that these are not independent discoveries but one relationship expressed three ways.

The first-order curve and the loop time constant

The third open-loop variant is the only one that asks the process to actually be first order. That assumption buys a great deal of simplicity: the curve reaches 63.2 % of its final change in one time constant, and 63.2 % of what remains in each one after that. The tangent at the origin reaches 100 % in exactly one time constant, which is why the time constant can be read off the chart without solving anything.

The LTC method then sets the three gains directly: proportional gain is the reciprocal of the process gain, integral gain equals the loop time constant, derivative gain is a quarter of it. It is the least fashionable of the four methods and the fastest to apply, and on a genuinely first-order loop it is as good as anything else in the chapter.

Hysteresis: when the process answers differently in each direction

Section 8.7 is two pages long and saves more plant time than any other section. If you step the output up and then step it back down, most real processes will not retrace the same path. A valve with stiction, a heat exchanger with fouling on one side, a boiler whose heating and cooling lags differ — all produce a loop that behaves differently in each direction.

The instruction is to do both directions and compare. Under five or six per cent difference, average the two. Above that, tuning to either direction means the loop is mistuned for the other half of its life, and no amount of PID tuning will fix it — the fix belongs in the process, which is why the chapter sends the reader forward to the feedforward discussion in Chapter 11.

Closed loop: finding the edge of stability on purpose

The continuous cycling method is the one everybody knows and the one that frightens everybody, because it deliberately drives the loop to the edge of instability and measures the period there. The gain that produces sustained oscillation is K-u; the period is P-u. Everything else is arithmetic.

Two details in the procedure are load-bearing. First, integral and derivative must be disabled — T-int at 999, T-der at zero — because both add phase shift and the whole point is to measure the process's phase, not the controller's. Second, the instruction after a growing oscillation is to stop immediately and reduce the gain, not to watch how far it goes. A loop that is amplifying is a loop whose gain is above one, and the amplitude grows until something mechanical gives.

Damped cycling: the same measurement without the danger

Where continuous cycling is unacceptable — a column, a reactor, anything with a trip — the damped variant gets the same information from a decaying oscillation instead. You record the gain that produced a decay, K-d, and the ratio of the first two peaks, and from those you recover the ultimate gain and period by calculation rather than by observation.

The two charts that carry the arithmetic are worth understanding rather than just using. Figure 8-9 is the exact second-order relation between overshoot and damping ratio; a decay ratio of one half corresponds to δ near 0.22, and the usual working target of δ around 0.5 corresponds to about 16 % overshoot. Figure 8-10 converts the same information into the ratio of ultimate to damped period, which is what the tuning formulas actually need.

Pessen: buying no-overshoot with gain

Pessen's two variants are not new measurements, only different fractions of the same K-u and P-u. What they buy is a guarantee at start-up, which is the one moment when a loop transferring from manual to automatic can be arbitrarily far from its set point. The cost is visible in the table: gain drops from 0.6 to 0.2 of K-u, and the loop becomes slow enough that disturbances during normal running are corrected lazily.

The chapter's note that start-up is not the same as a set-point change is the reason these formulas exist at all. A set-point step produces a step in the error and therefore a derivative kick; a manual-to-auto transfer does not, because the error was already there. Tuning for start-up is therefore tuning for a large standing error, not for a transient.

Dependent and independent gains: the trap at the keyboard

The last section looks like housekeeping and is in fact where a great many tuning exercises go wrong. The ISA form has one gain multiplying the whole bracket, so changing it changes all three actions at once. The independent form has three separate gains, and changing one changes only that action. The same physical loop described in the two forms needs two different sets of numbers.

The conversion is simple arithmetic — K-p equals K-c, K-i equals K-c over T-int in seconds, K-d equals K-c times T-der in seconds — but the arithmetic is only correct if you know which form the controller is expecting. Enter a Ziegler-Nichols set, which is derived for the dependent form, into a controller expecting independent gains, and the loop will run; it just will not behave like anything the method predicted. The usual conclusion at that point is that the method does not work, when the method was never given a chance.
