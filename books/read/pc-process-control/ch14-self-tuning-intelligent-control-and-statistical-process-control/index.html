Self-Tuning Intelligent Control and SPC

Wolfgang Altmann · IDC Technologies (Phiên bản 3.02)

Chapter 14 · 18 source pages · 11 figures · companion notes
Key terms. Self-tuning — a controller that recomputes its own parameters. · System identifier — the block that estimates the process parameters. · Controller synthesiser — the block that computes a new parameter set from the identified ones. · Gain scheduling — changing the gain against a measured auxiliary variable. · Subgroup — a sample of n values taken at one instant. · Controlled variation — caused by chance alone. · Assignable cause — a specific cause that puts a point outside the limits. · SPC — statistical process control.

Thirteen chapters have taught how to choose and tune a controller. This last one asks a different question: what if the controller tuned itself, and how would you know whether the whole loop is doing well?

The two questions sound unrelated but they are two halves of one problem. Self-tuning belongs to the controller: it measures the process response and corrects its own parameters. SPC belongs to whoever owns the process: it looks at the run of product and says whether the process is still stable or has drifted. The first asks “how much should I adjust”; the second asks “is adjusting even the right response”. And SPC's answer is often: no, the problem is not in the controller.

Self-tuning is a slow outer loop, and that is the whole trick § 14.2

Figure 14-1 shows the structure and it is worth reading as two loops running at two speeds. The inner loop is the ordinary control loop. The outer loop watches the MV and the PV, identifies the process, and writes new parameters back into the controller.

The speeds must differ by orders of magnitude, and the chapter does not say so explicitly. The inner loop runs in seconds. The outer loop has to observe several oscillations before it can conclude anything about period or damping, so it runs in minutes or hours. If the outer loop ran at anything like the inner loop's rate, it would be chasing the oscillations it had just created — a feedback loop around a feedback loop, with the same stability question one level up.

Section 14.4's threshold is the practical expression of the same idea. The updating algorithms stay dormant until the error exceeds about 1 per cent. That is not a technical constant; it is a judgement that below 1 per cent the measurement noise is the same size as the correction, so adjusting makes the process worse more often than better.

Gain scheduling: open-loop adaptation, and honest about it § 14.3

Figure 14-2 draws gain scheduling as what it actually is: a table of gains indexed by a measured auxiliary variable, approximating a continuous requirement with a staircase. The approximation error between breakpoints is the price; the absence of any model or learning is the benefit.

The chapter is unusually candid about the weakness — it calls it “an OPEN LOOP adaptation with no real learning or intelligence”. That is exactly right, and it is worth spelling out what it means in practice: the schedule does not know whether it is still correct. It looks up a table and trusts the table. When the plant changes underneath it — a fouled exchanger, a deactivated catalyst — the table is consulted as before and is silently wrong.

That is not an argument against gain scheduling. It is the most robust of the adaptive methods precisely because it has no feedback to go unstable. It is an argument for scheduling against a variable that tracks the actual change in dynamics, and for reviewing the table when the plant is modified.

The three numbers a self-tuner reads off the response § 14.4

Figure 14-3 names them: overshoot, period of oscillation, and damping — the ratio of successive peaks. Three numbers, three unknowns. Overshoot and damping together fix the closed-loop damping ratio; period fixes the natural frequency; from those two the gain and the integral time follow. It is the Ziegler–Nichols idea generalised: instead of driving the loop to sustained oscillation and measuring the ultimate gain, the self-tuner reads the same information off an ordinary transient.

Shewhart's distinction is the most useful idea in the chapter § 14.5–14.6

Every process varies; some vary in a controlled way and some do not. That sentence, from Shewhart at Bell Labs in the early 1920s, is the foundation of everything that follows, and it is a distinction about causes, not about sizes.

Controlled variation comes from many small causes acting together — machine, material, operator, method — each too small to identify. It is stable because it is the sum of many independent contributions, and its shape is therefore predictable even though no individual value is. Uncontrolled variation comes from an assignable cause: a specific, findable thing that has changed.

The two demand opposite responses, and getting them the wrong way round is the most common error in process plants. Faced with controlled variation and a product that is off target, people adjust the controller — which is not the cause, and adjusting it only adds variation. Faced with an assignable cause, people redesign the procedure — when tightening one bolt would have done.

Every process displays variation; some processes display controlled variation, others display uncontrolled variation.

W. A. Shewhart, Bell Laboratories, early 1920s

Two variables, because mean and spread move independently § 14.7

Figures 14-4 and 14-6 are the same four hours seen twice: once for a stable process, where the four bells sit on top of each other, and once for a moving one, where they shift right, then left, then widen. The widening is the important case, because it is invisible in the average.

Figures 14-5 and 14-7 are the corresponding charts. Figure 14-7 is the one that earns the method: at 09:00 the average breaks the upper limit, at 10:00 it breaks the lower, and at 11:00 the average returns to normal while the range breaks its own upper limit. For the first three hours the spread never moved. An operator watching only the range — or only the product, which averages the two — would have missed two excursions entirely.

That is the argument for plotting both variables rather than one, and it is worth stating plainly because it is the reason the chapter introduces subgroups at all. A subgroup of n gives you two numbers per sample instead of one, and they answer different questions: where is the process, and how consistent is it.

Why the average chart is more sensitive than the product § 14.7.6

Figure 14-9 contains the statistical reason the method works, and it is one line of algebra: SD(x̄) = SD(X)/√n. The average of four measurements varies half as much as a single measurement; the average of nine, a third as much. Plotting averages therefore compresses the random noise by √n while leaving a genuine shift in the mean untouched — which is why a shift that is invisible in the individual readings stands out on the chart.

(14.2)SD(x̄) = SD(X) / √n

The range distribution behaves differently: it is skewed, not normal, which is why it is converted through d2 and d3 rather than used directly. Figure 14-8 shows all three histograms from the chapter's own data table, drawn to the same scale — the measurements wide and flat, the averages narrow and tall, the ranges skewed hard right.

Two routes to the same limits, and why they agree § 14.8

The chapter computes the limits twice — once structurally, through the estimated standard deviation, and once by formula, through A2, D3 and D4. Both give 1.811 to 7.715 for the averages and 0 to about 9.24 for the ranges. They must agree, because the tabulated constants were derived from d2 and d3 in the first place: A2 = 3/(d2√n), D4 = 1 + 3d3/d2.

(14.5)X̄ ± A2·R̄ = 4.763 ± 0.729 × 4.05 = 1.811 to 7.715

Figure 14-10 is that calculation applied to the chapter's own twenty subgroups. All twenty averages fall between the limits and all twenty ranges fall below the upper one, so the process is judged stable. Note the lower range limit is 0, not the −1.143 the arithmetic produces: a range cannot be negative, and a limit that cannot be reached is not a limit.

A note on the source. The original prints the upper range limit as 9.423. The correct value is 9.243 — 4.05 + 3(1.731) — and D4·R̄ gives 9.242; two digits have been transposed. The original also numbers the worked example 14.8.5 while placing it between 14.7.4 and 14.7.6, then cross-refers to it by that number; this translation renumbers it 14.7.5 and updates the cross-references.

What the chart is really telling you § 14.9

Figure 14-11 is the decision the chart supports, and the two branches are different in kind. Inside the limits means the process is doing what it always does — which is not the same as doing well. If the product is still off target, the problem is in the design of the process, and no amount of operator attention will fix it.

Outside the limits means something specific has changed, and it can be found. That is a much more hopeful diagnosis, and a much cheaper one. The discipline SPC imposes is simply to ask which of the two situations you are in before touching anything.

Closing the book

Fourteen chapters have gone round a full circle. Chapter 2 was sensors — how to know where the process is. Chapter 3 was valves — how to act on it. Chapters 4 to 12 were the theory: stability, control modes, PID, tuning, cascade, feedforward, dead time. Chapters 13 and 14 supplied tools for the problems that theory does not reach.

What is worth keeping is not the formulas — every controller on the market already contains them. It is a habit: before adjusting anything, establish where the process is actually wrong. A sticking valve, a drifting transmitter, a long dead time, or simply a process that was always this variable and is not broken at all. That is the difference between an engineer and someone who knows which knob to turn.
