Concepts and Applications of Feedforward Control

Wolfgang Altmann · IDC Technologies (Phiên bản 3.02)

Chapter 10 · 8 source pages · 9 figures · companion notes
Key terms. Feedforward — acting on the manipulated variable from a measured disturbance, before any error exists. · Measured disturbance — a disturbing quantity the plant has an instrument to read directly. · Lead/lag compensator — a block with transfer function (1 + Tleads)/(1 + αTleads), used to make two reaction speeds equal. · Time matching — making the control action's response coincide with the disturbance's. · Residual offset — the error left after compensation, which is never zero. · Math function — the calculation that turns the block's input into its output.

Feedforward is the only strategy in this book that acts before an error exists. Every controller from chapter 4 onwards has followed the same logic: measure the PV, compare it with the SP, take the difference, act. The error has to exist first. Feedforward inverts that order — it reads the quantity that is about to cause the error and acts on it directly. Get it right and the PV never moves, so the feedback loop has nothing to correct.

That inversion is also why the chapter is hard. To act in advance you have to know in advance: which disturbances are coming, how large they will be, and how the process will respond to each. The book states the price plainly — a sound knowledge is required both of the process and the nature of all relevant disturbances — and it is worth taking seriously, because a feedforward block tuned from a wrong model is worse than no block at all.

The definition, unpacked § 10.2

The chapter's own definition is a single sentence, and it rewards being taken apart. Feedforward control does not take the process variable into account; it reacts to the measurement of known or suspected disturbances, making it a compensating and matching control that makes the impact of the disturbance and of the feedback control equal.

Three claims sit inside that sentence. The block does not see the PV — if it did, it would be a feedback element wearing a different name. It does see the disturbance. And what it does with the disturbance is make two effects equal: the effect the disturbance is about to have on the PV, and the effect the controller has in opposing it. Equal in magnitude and coincident in time. That is the whole specification, and both halves of it matter.

Figure 10-1 is the argument in one picture. The same step disturbance, the same process, the same controller. Blue has feedback only: the PV dives, and the controller then hauls it back. Red has a feedforward block as well: the block reads the disturbance as it arrives and compensates immediately, so the PV barely moves. Nothing about the controller changed between the two traces. What changed is when the information was available.

Manual feedforward: the concept with the loop removed § 10.3

The book's first example is a human being. An operator notices a disturbance, estimates it from process knowledge, and moves the manipulated variable by an amount that will minimise its effect. Figure 10-2 draws this honestly, including the gap: there is no feedback path in the diagram, and the line from PV down to the operator box is an eye, not a loop.

What makes this example more than a teaching device is the failure mode it exposes. The operator sees the PV but nothing compels them to act, and nothing checks whether the action was right. If they misjudge the disturbance, the error stays. Every feedforward block, automatic or manual, is a prediction, and every prediction is wrong by some amount. Without a mechanism that detects the error in the prediction, that error becomes a permanent offset in the process. Section 10.6 is where the book confronts this, and it is the most important passage in the chapter.

Automatic feedforward: the same idea with a calculation in place of a person § 10.4

Figure 10-3 replaces the operator with a block, and adds the detail the manual picture could not show: the disturbance signal splits. One branch goes into the process — that is the physical disturbance, and nothing stops it. The other goes into the feedforward block, is converted into a compensating quantity, and is summed into the controller's output. The two branches have to arrive at the same instant and with opposite signs. Compensation that arrives early is a new disturbance; compensation that arrives late is decoration.

This is where the book's warning about the designer and the operator both needing to understand the mathematics comes from, and it is why feedforward is reserved for important or critical loops. The cost of a feedforward block is not the block. It is the instrument on the disturbance, the model that relates the disturbance to the PV, and the person who can maintain both.

Four parts, and nothing else § 10.5

The chapter's list of what a feedforward block must contain is short: a recognisable input derived from the measured disturbance, a set point or point of origin, a math function operating on the two, and an output which is the result. Figure 10-4 draws it.

The claim that follows — that the controller's action can be described purely by the mathematical function it performs — is not rhetorical. It has a practical consequence. If the block's action is fully determined by its function, then designing the block is choosing the function, and testing the block is checking that the function returns the right answer over a known range of inputs. There is no hidden state, no accumulated integral, no windup. A pure feedforward block is a function, not a dynamical system.

That is precisely why four parts are enough and why there is no fifth part of the kind a PID controller needs. A PID has state because it remembers; a feedforward block has none because it does not. The same fact makes the block testable offline, which is a considerable engineering advantage: sweep the disturbance input, read the output, compare against an independent calculation.

Time matching: when equal magnitude is not enough § 10.6

A heater does not heat and cool at the same rate. Figure 10-5 shows the consequence: the reaction curve for adding heat has one time constant, the curve for losing it has another, and a compensation sized for one is mistimed for the other. The book's phrasing is exact — if the reaction curve of the disturbance is not equal to the control action, it has to be made equal.

The tool for making them equal is the lead/lag block, and its step response explains why it works:

(10.1)G(s) = (1 + Tlead·s) / (1 + α·Tlead·s)
(10.2)y(t) = 1 + (1/α − 1)·e−t/(α·Tlead)

With α below one the block overshoots to 1/α at the instant of the step and then decays back to one. That is the shape in Figure 10-6: a spike, then a settling. Physically it is an extra push delivered immediately, exactly enough to keep pace with a fast reaction, withdrawn gradually as the slower reaction catches up. Choose α and you have chosen the size of the push; with α = 0.4 the block pushes 150 per cent harder for an instant, which is also why a lead term needs a filter or a clean measurement in front of it.

The drift that no amount of care removes § 10.6

The chapter's most valuable paragraph is the shortest. We can be as careful as we want with our evaluation of the disturbances, but we never reach absolute perfect compensation; there are always factors not accounted for. The PV drifts, and the drift has to be corrected by hand or by adding feedback.

Figure 10-8 is that sentence as a graph. Red is feedforward alone: it removes most of the disturbance and leaves a residue that never goes away. Blue is the same block with a slow feedback loop added. The feedback does not need to be fast, because the feedforward block already handled the fast part; it only needs to be patient enough to collect the residue. That combination — fast feedforward, slow feedback — is the architecture of essentially every industrial feedforward scheme, and it is why pure feedforward is rare in practice.

The worked example: a feed heater § 10.6

Figure 10-7 is the chapter's one fully engineered case, and it repays slow reading because it encodes two design rules. Inlet flow is measured, converted straight into a heat demand, and that demand becomes the set point of a fuel flow loop:

(10.3)Qrequired = ṁ · cp · (Tset − Tin)

The first rule is in the formula. Heat demand depends on the temperature difference, not on the set point alone, so the inlet temperature has to be measured too. A feedforward block that reads only the flow is correct only while the inlet temperature happens to be constant. That is why the temperature transmitter appears in the diagram at all.

The second rule is in the topology. The calculation's output does not drive the fuel valve; it drives the set point of a fuel flow controller, which drives the valve. Fuel supply pressure is a second disturbance, unmeasured and unmeasurable in any useful place, and the flow loop absorbs it without the feedforward block ever knowing it exists.

Each feedforward block should compensate exactly one disturbance. A second disturbance belongs to a fast inner feedback loop, not to the same block.

Trying to compensate both with one block produces a math function nobody can verify and nobody can tune. Splitting them produces two elements that can each be tested on their own — which is the whole reason the four-part structure of section 10.5 is worth insisting on.

What the chapter is really teaching Closing

The chapter's closing comparison fits in a sentence: feedback corrects what has already gone wrong, feedforward prevents what is about to. Neither substitutes for the other. A good system has both — feedforward to keep the PV from moving, feedback to erase the residue that feedforward can never erase.

The cost side deserves equal billing. Feedforward demands an instrument for every disturbance you want to compensate, a model for every disturbance-to-PV relationship, and a person who understands both well enough to tune and maintain them. It also demands that the model be updated when the process changes: a block tuned for a clean heat exchanger compensates wrongly once the exchanger fouls, and it will compensate wrongly in a way that is invisible, because the feedback loop keeps the PV on set point and hides the error. In exchange, it is the only mechanism in this book capable of holding the PV still while a disturbance is acting.
