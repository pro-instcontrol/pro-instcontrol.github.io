Digital Control Principles

Wolfgang Altmann · IDC Technologies (Phiên bản 3.02)

Chapter 6 · 16 source pages · 6 figures · companion notes
Key terms. Sampler — the block that replaces the continuous controller and runs the discrete algorithm once per scan. · Scan time — the interval between successive calculations. · Incremental algorithm — computes only the change in output, never its absolute value. · Initialization — loading the output from the downstream block so there is no bump. · Lead — filtered derivative, used instead of ideal differentiation.

This is the shortest chapter in the control book and the one that dates fastest, yet it contains the idea everything else depends on: a digital controller does not compute an output, it computes how far the output should move.

That distinction sounds like bookkeeping. It is not. Once the output is an accumulated sum of increments rather than a value owned by one algorithm, several things become possible at once: initialisation from a downstream block, bumpless transfer between manual and automatic, cascade where the secondary's set point drives the primary's output, and a restart that does not disturb the plant. All of them fall out of the same decision.

The two controller forms

The chapter draws the same controller twice. Figure 6-1 is the field controller: proportional, integral, and a derivative term that is not a differentiator but a lead — a differentiator followed by a first-order lag with a time constant α times smaller.

Figure 6-2 is the ideal form, the one in the textbooks, where the three terms sit in parallel and the derivative is exact. The difference matters because an exact differentiator has unbounded gain at high frequency: it will differentiate the noise on a 4–20 mA signal just as faithfully as the process variable, and the result is a valve that hunts.

Figure 6-4 is the same controller written in the Laplace domain with the lead made explicit, and it is the form to keep: not an ideal differentiator clipped by a filter added afterwards, but a controller whose derivative branch is a lead from the start. Everything the rest of the chapter says about α, about scan time and about initialisation follows from reading that one block diagram correctly.

The lead filter, and why α is 8 to 12

A lead block is a differentiator in series with a lag. Above the lag's corner frequency the differentiation stops and the gain flattens out at α. That single number — 8 to 12 in industrial equipment — is the whole design of derivative action in a real controller. It says: differentiate fast enough to see the process move, but stop differentiating before you reach the noise.

Put the other way round: α is the ratio of how much derivative you would like to how much derivative you can afford. A controller with α = 8 gives up a factor of eight of high-frequency gain in exchange for a derivative term that is usable on real wiring.

Scan time is a unit, not a delay

The most practically important section is the shortest. Every time constant in the discrete algorithm has to be expressed in units of the scan, not in seconds: T_INT in scans is T_INT in minutes times 60 divided by the scan in seconds. A 1.5-minute integral time on a 0.5-second scan is 180 — not 90, and not 1.5.

The second rule is a design constraint: every time constant must be far greater than the scan time, or the difference equation stops approximating the differential equation it came from. A rule of thumb that appears throughout the literature is ten samples per time constant; below that, the discrete controller's phase lag starts to eat the stability margin the tuning was supposed to provide.

This is also where fixed-scan and variable-scan controllers part company. A modulating controller runs a fixed scan, so the constants can be pre-computed once. A PLC's scan varies with program length and I/O count, so the constants must be recomputed every scan — and a chapter that assumes a fixed scan will mislead anyone implementing PID in a PLC.

Increments, and the figures that follow

Figure 6-3 shows what the incremental form actually computes: at each scan the controller looks at how much the error changed since the last scan, multiplies by the gain, and adds the result to whatever the output already is. The output never appears as an absolute number inside the algorithm. It is read from the destination, nudged, and written back.

The Siemens S5 example in Figure 6-6 is worth reading as an implementation rather than as arithmetic. Three differences are formed — first difference for proportional, second difference for derivative, the sample itself for integral — each scaled and summed into one increment. The controller holds three samples of the input and nothing else. That is why it can be restarted mid-shift without a bump: there is no accumulated state to lose.

What has changed since 1999

The arithmetic has not. What has changed is where it runs. In 1999 the incremental form was the native idiom of a DCS or a PLC function block, and the ideal parallel form was what you got in a standalone controller. Today almost every loop is executed inside a PLC or a DCS with a fixed task, and the ideal form is routinely implemented with an internal anti-windup and a derivative filter — which is to say, the field controller of Figure 6-1 has won, and the textbook form of Figure 6-2 survives mainly as a way of explaining it.
