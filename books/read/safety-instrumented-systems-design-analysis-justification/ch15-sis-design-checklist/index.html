Books · Safety Instrumented Systems — Design, Analysis, and Justification · Chapter 15

SIS Design Checklist

Paul Gruhn & Harry Cheddie · ISA (2006)

Chapter 15 · 22 source pages · companion notes
How to use this chapter. This is not a chapter to read from end to end; it is a list to work through. Every line is a question, and the last column offers three answers: Y, N and N/A. In the printed book that column is three boxes to circle; here it is collapsed into one cell. · SRS — safety requirements specification. · Proven-in-use — the route to acceptance for equipment that carries no certificate. · Proof test — the periodic test that reveals hidden dangerous failures. · Diagnostics — the system's own ability to detect its failures.

This chapter is a checklist, and a checklist is a strange thing to translate. It has no argument, no worked example, no narrative. It has 179 questions arranged in twenty groups, and its value depends entirely on somebody actually answering them.

So the useful English companion is not a summary of the questions. It is an answer to the question the checklist itself raises and does not answer: why does a list of questions prevent accidents at all?

A checklist is a memory, not a method

The chapter opens with the honest disclaimer: a checklist does not make a system safer, any more than a HAZOP makes a plant safer if nobody implements the recommendations. That sounds like modesty, but it is precise. A checklist has no power to change anything. It has only the power to remind.

Which means its value is exactly the value of the things people forget. And Figure 15-1 tells you what those things are. Of 34 accidents caused by control and safety system failures, 44 percent traced back to incorrect or incomplete specifications and 20 percent to changes made after commissioning. Sixty-four percent. Nearly two thirds of the failures happened before the equipment was ever stressed, in documents and decisions.

The two failure modes this checklist actually targets

Those two numbers are not equally distributed across the twenty sections. Sections 1 to 4 — management, SRS, conceptual design, detailed design — are the 44 percent. Section 19, management of change, is the 20 percent. Everything else, all sixteen remaining sections, addresses the 36 percent that is left.

That distribution should change how the list is used. A project that works through sections 5 to 18 thoroughly and skips the front matter has done the easy 36 percent and left the hard 64 percent undone. And it is easy to see why: sections 5 to 18 have answers that are checkable — is the power supply redundant, are the field devices calibrated, is there a fault reporting system. Sections 1 to 4 have answers that are judgements — is the specification complete, is the hazard assessment systematic, is the safe state defined for every operating state.

A judgement cannot be ticked. That is the whole difficulty, and it is why the specification failure keeps recurring across decades of accident reports.

Why "N/A" is the most dangerous column

Three options are offered for every line: yes, no, not applicable. The first two are honest. The third is where a checklist quietly dies, because "not applicable" is the answer that requires no work and provokes no argument.

Some lines genuinely are N/A — a pneumatic logic system has no software coding section. But the pattern to watch for is a section where every line is N/A. That is rarely a fact about the system. It is usually a fact about the person filling it in: nobody on the team owned that phase, so nobody could answer, so the whole section was closed with N/A and the project moved on. Item 1.1 asks whether the responsible persons have been identified, and if the answer to that is no, then the N/As further down are not answers at all.

A useful discipline: require a one-line justification for every N/A, exactly as item 3.1 does for its own question ("If not, what is the justification?"). The book asks that question in about a dozen places. It could profitably ask it everywhere.

The questions that are really one question

Read across the twenty sections and one theme repeats in almost every one: has somebody other than the person who did the work checked the work?

Item 15.4 asks whether testing is carried out by persons other than those producing the code. Item 14.11 asks whether the final code is checked against requirements by persons other than those who wrote it. Item 16.2 asks about independence between those carrying out the work and those inspecting it. Item 18.4 through 18.10 ask whether tests happen, whether they are recorded, and who conducted them.

That is not bureaucratic duplication. It is the recognition that the person who wrote the logic is the worst possible person to confirm that the logic does what the specification said, because they will test what they meant rather than what they wrote. Chapter 13 makes the same point about MOC checkout with a specific instruction to use a separate crew. The checklist generalises it: independence is not a virtue, it is a control.

How to use it without pretending

Three practical notes for anyone who actually runs this list on a project.

  1. Answer sections 1 to 4 first, and do not let them be answered by the same person who will later be measured on the schedule. They are the 44 percent, and they are the sections most likely to be compressed when the project is late — which is exactly when compressing them does the most damage.
  2. Treat section 19 as a living control, not a one-time answer. The 20 percent of accidents that came from post-commissioning changes did not come from changes that were reviewed and rejected; they came from changes that were never put through the process at all.
  3. Keep the completed list. It is the only document that records what the project believed about itself at the time, and it is the first thing an investigator will ask for after an incident. A list with honest N's in it is worth more than a list with all Y's, because the N's are where the risk actually was.

And a closing observation about the checklist's own claim that it is not final or complete. That is not false modesty either. The twenty sections map onto a lifecycle that assumes a conventional project: specify, design, build, install, operate. Projects that do not look like that — a software update to a twenty-year-old SIS, a change of feedstock, a plant mothballed and restarted — will find sections that do not fit and will have to invent the questions. The list gives you the habit; it cannot give you the answers.
