Choosing a Technology

Paul Gruhn & Harry Cheddie · ISA (2006)

Chapter 7 · 20 source pages · companion notes
Key terms. PLC — the industrial computer that scans a program in a loop, invented in 1969 to replace relays. · Diagnostics — the self-test circuits and software that find failures the eye cannot see. · Diagnostic coverage — the fraction of failures the system detects on its own. · FMEDA — failure modes, effects and diagnostic analysis: a table of every component, every way it fails, its failure rate, and whether that failure is detectable. · TMR — three redundant channels voting two-out-of-three. · 1oo2D — two channels, each self-diagnosing, either one sufficient to act. · Prior use — the evidence route that substitutes for certification, built on the user's own failure history.

Four technologies can close a valve on demand: compressed air, a relay, a solid-state module, or a programmable logic controller. None of them is best in general, and the choice is not really technical. It is a judgement about how much diagnostic machinery you are willing to pay for, and about what happens to the plant when that machinery is wrong.

The relay panel is the honest baseline

It is fashionable to treat relay logic as obsolete. Read section 7.2 again and it looks more like a specification than a relic. A relay is simple, cheap, immune to most electrical interference, needs no software, and fails in a known direction — de-energised. Its limitations are all consequences of being physical: it cannot tell you anything, it cannot be tested without being wired for it, and every logic change is a change to copper.

The 700-I/O panel in the chapter, with fifty-seven signal handoffs between sensor and final element and a satellite in the middle, is not a joke about bad engineering. It is what happens when a technology is scaled past the size at which its virtues hold. Relays are excellent below about fifteen I/O and become unmanageable somewhere before a hundred. The number is not a standard; it is the point at which nobody can trace a cause to an effect any more.

Diagnostics are the whole difference

Every technology in this chapter delivers the same thing when it works: a discrete output. What separates them is what they can tell you about themselves while nothing is happening. A general-purpose PLC has almost no answer to that question, because it was designed for active control, where a failed input announces itself by making the process misbehave. In a safety system the process is quiet by design, so a failed input announces nothing at all.

This is why the chapter's horror stories are all the same story. The demo system with the CPU chip removed that kept every output energised; the four out of seven systems that failed a site acceptance test nobody had thought to run; the backup CPU cable that was never installed and was not detected for eighteen months. In each case the hardware was working. The lights were green. Nothing was measuring the thing that mattered.

The FMEDA is where a vendor becomes accountable

Table 7-1 is worth reading as a document rather than as data. Every component appears twice, once for each failure mode, because a resistor that fails short and a resistor that fails open do different things to the loop. Each row is classified safe or dangerous, given a FIT rate, and marked as detectable or not. The totals at the bottom — 111 FIT, 88.75 safe, 22 dangerous, 97.9 % safe coverage, 100 % dangerous coverage — are the vendor's claim in its most falsifiable form.

Two things about that table repay attention. First, the dangerous modes are a minority of the total but a majority of the risk: 22 FIT out of 111, all of them detected, which is exactly the arrangement a safety circuit is supposed to have. Second, the coverage figures are only achievable because the circuit compares two channels. A single-channel version of the same schematic would have the same failure rates and almost none of the coverage — the hardware would be identical and the SIL claim would be two bands lower.

Redundancy architectures are arguments about disagreement

Hot back-up, active parallel, TMR and 1oo2D are usually presented as a ladder of increasing integrity. They are better understood as four different answers to one question: when the channels disagree, who decides? A hot back-up never has to answer, because only one CPU is running; it pays for that by being unable to detect its own failure. Active parallel has to answer every time and has no basis on which to do it. TMR answers by majority, which works until two channels fail the same way. 1oo2D answers by diagnostics rather than by voting, which is why it needs only two channels — and why its integrity depends entirely on the diagnostic coverage it claims.

The common-cause problem runs underneath all four. Redundancy counts independent failures; software bugs, shared power supplies, shared impulse lines and shared maintenance procedures are not independent. A triple system with a single shared root cause is a single system with three times the hardware.

Centralisation buys convenience and sells blast radius

Section 7.5 is the shortest in the chapter and the most consequential. Consolidating a dozen small shutdown systems into one large one is almost always the right commercial decision: one spare-parts list, one maintenance team, one programming environment, one screen. It is also a decision to convert a set of independent single points of failure into one shared single point of failure, and to convert a local trip into a plant trip.

The mitigation is redundancy, and the trap is that redundancy is exactly what makes the system complex enough to need more of it. Every hot back-up adds a switch that can fail; every switch adds a diagnostic that must itself be tested; every test adds a procedure that can be performed wrongly. Simplicity is not an aesthetic preference in a safety system. It is the only property that reduces the number of things that must be got right.

Prior use is a claim about your own history, not the vendor's brochure

The chapter ends on certification, and the honest observation that most field devices are never going to be certified. The prior-use route is the alternative, and it is frequently misused. It requires a quality programme you have actually audited, a sample size large enough to mean something, and an environment similar enough that the failure data transfers. It does not mean “we have used these for years and nothing has gone wrong” — which is a statement about luck, not about failure rate.

The distinction the chapter draws between simple field devices and complex logic solvers is the practical one. A pressure switch has a small enough failure space that field history can characterise it. A safety PLC has a failure space dominated by software, where field history tells you almost nothing about the failure you have not had yet.
