Books · Safety Instrumented Systems — Design, Analysis, and Justification · Chapter 13

Managing Changes to a System

Paul Gruhn & Harry Cheddie · ISA (2006)

Chapter 13 · 9 source pages · 3 figures · companion notes
Key terms. MOC (management of change) — the procedure that initiates, documents, reviews, implements and approves every change. · Replacement in kind — for safety equipment this means like for like: same failure modes and failure rate, not merely the same electrical ratings. · Original safety design basis — the assumptions, constraints and design features maintainers must know in order not to delete them by accident. · First affected phase — the point in the lifecycle to which every change returns. · PSAT — pre-startup acceptance testing. · Decommissioning — taking a system out of service, which also needs MOC.

The only constant in any organisation is change. This chapter is about what that sentence costs when it is applied to a safety system and nobody is standing at the gate.

Flixborough is not a story about a pipe

Section 13.2 spends a page on a temporary pipe, and it is worth reading slowly, because every detail is a decision that a procedure would have caught. A reactor is removed for repair. A bypass link is installed to keep production running. No qualified engineer is consulted. The only drawing of the change is a full-scale chalk outline on the workshop floor. The assembly is pressure-tested once — pneumatically rather than hydraulically, and to 127 psig rather than the 156 psig of the relief valve.

Twenty-eight people died two months later. The pipe held until a pressure transient below the relief valve setting twisted it, tore the flexible connection and left two 28-inch openings. Between forty and fifty tonnes of cyclohexane found an ignition source.

The lesson the chapter draws is Trevor Kletz's line: they did not know what they did not know. That is not a comment about intelligence; it is a comment about scope. The people installing the pipe knew how to install a pipe. They did not know that the flexible bellows had a pressure rating, that the design pressure came from the relief valve, that a pneumatic test stores far more energy than a hydraulic one, or that a temporary modification needs the same review as a permanent one. Nobody in the loop was responsible for knowing any of it.

Replacement in kind is a phrase that has killed people

Section 13.4 contains the most useful paragraph in the chapter, and it is the one most often skimmed. For an electric motor, replacement in kind means same power, voltage, frequency, speed and frame size — interchangeability is a physical property, and any motor that fits will do. For a transmitter it is not. Two transmitters with identical ranges, identical outputs and identical mounting can have failure rates that differ by a factor of five, and different proportions of detected versus undetected dangerous failures.

That difference goes straight into the PFD calculation that justified the SIL. A swap that looks like a like-for-like replacement on the datasheet can quietly move the loop out of its target integrity level, and nothing on the plant will look any different. This is why the chapter insists on "like for like" and on pre-approved replacements rather than interchangeability.

The last line wins

The offshore anecdote in section 13.5 is the best illustration in the book of why testing the revision is not testing the system. A shutdown coil already sat at the end of a rung that had no room left. The programmer added a second rung and repeated the coil, which is exactly what you would do if you were thinking of the diagram as a drawing rather than as an execution order.

Ladder logic does not write to the output during the scan. Values accumulate in memory and are written at the end, so the later rung overwrites the earlier one: the original rung calls for a shutdown, the new rung resets the bit to normal, and the valve never moves. The revision worked exactly as designed. The original logic was never re-tested, so nobody found out until a government inspector asked for a demonstration.

Two general rules come out of it, and both are cheap. Never write to the same output in more than one place — if you need a second condition, put it in series on the same rung or use an intermediate bit. And when you change logic, re-test the whole logic, not the diff. The second rule costs an afternoon; the first one would have cost ten minutes.

A change to software is a change to design

Section 13.5 applies the lifecycle rule to software without softening it: a modification returns you to the first affected phase, and every later phase must be re-run, including re-verification and documentation. That is inconvenient, because software changes feel like edits rather than engineering. The programmer in the anecdote made a ten-minute change and treated it as one.

The standard's six software requirements are worth reading as a checklist rather than as prose, because each one maps to a failure mode: analyse the effect before you modify; plan the modification and the re-verification; follow the plan; plan the conditions during modification and testing; update every affected document; keep a log. Skip the first and you change something whose purpose you did not understand. Skip the second and you test what you remember rather than what you specified.

Here is the twelve-item table the standard lays out for an MOC procedure, in English, because the list is the part of this chapter a plant actually implements:

ItemNotes
How a change is initiated or requestedApproval forms must be built and put into the MOC procedure. The technical basis for the change should be recorded on the form. The form is usually shared across every change on the site. It must cover both temporary and permanent changes.
Approval of the change requestAn authorisation and approval procedure must be established for the change.
ResponsibilitiesIdentify the individual(s) responsible for approving the change request, for recognising that the MOC procedure must be applied, and for ensuring the procedure is followed.
Hazard assessmentThe people taking part in the hazard assessment may not be the group that reviewed the change originally, and must be identified. More operations and maintenance staff usually take part than in the original review. The techniques used must be chosen from the nature, size and effect of the change.
DocumentationWhich documents must be updated, and how will the update be carried out?
Review and sign-offWho reviews the updated documents and signs them off?
TrainingEveryone affected by the change must be informed and trained.
ConstructionWhen and how will the change be implemented in the field?
CheckoutA separate crew should be responsible for checkout, working from fully documented procedures.
StartupThe change must be approved by operations before startup.
Final completionA final report must be signed off and issued after a successful startup.
Audit and verificationAn audit and verification system must exist to confirm that the procedures are being followed.

Table 13-1. The twelve items of an MOC procedure. The last two rows are the ones most often dropped: a procedure with no audit leaves nobody knowing whether it is followed, and a procedure with no completion report leaves nobody knowing whether the change ended.

MOC is a gate, not a form

The twelve-row table in 13.6 is often implemented as a form to be filled in, and that is where it stops working. Read the rows as a sequence of gates instead. Hazard assessment is a gate: somebody qualified decides whether the change touches a protected function. Check out is a gate, and the chapter is specific that it should be a separate crew — the people who installed the change are the worst possible people to confirm that it works. Startup is a gate: operations approves before the change goes live.

The last two rows are the ones that distinguish a procedure from a ritual. Auditing and verification asks whether the procedure is actually being followed, which is a question nobody asks if the form is the deliverable. Final completion asks for a signed report, which is the only artefact that tells a future reader that the change is finished rather than abandoned halfway.

And the reason all of this matters is in section 13.2: decision makers need to know why the safety features were put there originally, so that they do not inadvertently undo them. A safety system is a set of arguments made concrete. A change is an edit to one of those arguments, and an argument edited by someone who never read it is not an argument any more.
