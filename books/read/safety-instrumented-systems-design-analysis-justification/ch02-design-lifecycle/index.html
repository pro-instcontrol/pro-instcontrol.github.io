Design Lifecycle

Paul Gruhn, P.E., CFSE & Harry L. Cheddie, P.Eng., CFSE

"If I had 8 hours to cut down a tree, I would spend 6 hours sharpening the axe." - A. Lincoln. Designing a safety instrumented system is beyond any single person: it demands a multi-discipline team. This chapter shows why - from hindsight vs. foresight and the HSE findings on accident causes (44% traced to wrong or incomplete specifications) through the eight steps of the SIS design lifecycle described in IEC 61511 / ANSI-ISA-84.

Hindsight is the wrong tool for the job

Section 2.1 makes a distinction that sounds like a word game and is not. Hindsight asks what went wrong, after the fact, with the evidence in front of you. Foresight asks what could go wrong, before the fact, with the evidence you have been able to gather. Engineering is supposed to run on the second, and it mostly runs on the first, because the first is cheap and the second is not.

The practical consequence is that the industry's knowledge is stored in the wrong place. It sits in incident reports, insurance claims and coroners' findings — all of which are produced after the money has been spent and the plant has been built. A design team starting a new project has almost no access to that store, and even less incentive to read it, because the schedule has already been agreed.

The 44 % finding is about documents, not engineers

The Health and Safety Executive's finding — that 44 % of accidents in control and safety systems trace back to an incorrect or incomplete specification — is quoted so often that it has stopped being read. It does not say that 44 % of engineers are incompetent. It says that in nearly half of the cases, the system did exactly what it was asked to do, and what it was asked to do was wrong.

That reframing matters because it moves the fix. If the problem were competence, the remedy would be training and more checking of the same documents. If the problem is that the specification was never written down completely enough to be checked, the remedy is structural: a document that states the function, the integrity target, the proof-test interval, the response time and the safe state, in a form that somebody who was not in the meeting can audit.

Add the 20 % attributed to changes made after commissioning and the number reaches 64 %. Nearly two thirds of the failures happen before the equipment is ever stressed — in the specification and in the modification — which is the same distribution that chapter 15's checklist is built around, and the reason sections 1 to 4 and section 19 of that checklist carry most of its value.

Eight steps, and the loop that closes them

Section 2.3 lays out the design lifecycle that IEC 61511 and ANSI/ISA-84.00.01 both adopt: hazard and risk analysis, allocation of safety functions to protection layers, development of the safety requirements specification, SIS design and engineering, installation and commissioning and validation, operations and maintenance, modifications, and decommissioning. Eight steps, in that order, and the order is the argument.

Each step produces the input to the next, which is why skipping one is not a shortcut but a debt. An allocation made without a hazard study assigns a SIL to the wrong scenario. A specification written without an allocation states a target integrity that nobody derived. A design built from an incomplete specification will pass its factory test perfectly and fail its first demand, because the factory test checks the design against the specification and the specification is what was wrong.

The eighth step is the one that makes it a lifecycle rather than a sequence. Decommissioning is not the end of the system; it is the point at which the next hazard study begins, and the information gathered in steps six and seven — what actually failed, how often, and what the proof tests found — is the only field data the next project will ever get. A plant that throws that away at decommissioning has spent twenty years generating evidence and then deleted it.

There is also a backward loop that the diagram does not draw. Operations feeds modifications, and modifications feed back into the hazard analysis, which is why chapter 13 treats a change as a return to the first affected phase rather than as an edit. The lifecycle is not a line with eight boxes on it; it is a line with a return path, and most of the failures in this book live on the return path.
