Determining the Safety Integrity Level (SIL)

Paul Gruhn & Harry Cheddie · ISA (2006)

Chapter 6 · 26 source pages · companion notes
Key terms. SIL — a measure of the performance of a whole system, never of a single device. · PFD — the probability that the system fails to act when demanded. · RRF — the reciprocal of PFD, and the easier number to picture. · FAR — fatalities per hundred million person-hours of exposure. · ALARP — “as low as reasonably practicable”, a legal concept rather than a technical number. · IPL — an independent protection layer, countable only if it is specific, independent, dependable and auditable. · LOPA — the semi-quantitative method that multiplies the PFD of each layer.

This chapter is about a number that cannot be measured directly, chosen by a committee, defended in court, and then used to size a safety system. Everything in it — the fatality statistics, the matrices, the risk graphs, the LOPA arithmetic — is scaffolding around one uncomfortable fact: the tolerable level of risk is a decision, not a measurement. The engineering starts after the decision has been made.

The number the standard will not give you

A performance-based standard tells you that greater risk demands a better safety system. It does not tell you what “greater” means, what “better” means, or where the line sits. That abstention is deliberate and it is also the source of most of the difficulty in this chapter. A prescriptive standard can be checked: the valve is either redundant or it is not. A performance standard has to be interpreted, and interpretation needs a starting point — a tolerable risk target — that no standard supplies.

So the first task in any SIL determination is not technical at all. It is to find out what target the organisation has already committed to, in writing, somewhere else: a corporate risk matrix, a jurisdiction's published fatality rate, an insurance condition. If no such target exists, the team is not doing a SIL study. It is inventing policy, and it should say so before it starts, because the number it invents will be quoted back at it for the life of the plant.

Why the statistics in section 6.5.7 feel wrong and are still useful

The Russian roulette passage is the most quoted part of the chapter and also the least reliable arithmetically — the barrel count does not match the annual rate it is supposed to represent, and the parenthetical 40 % is never derived. But the passage earns its place because it demonstrates something the arithmetic obscures: a rate and a probability are different objects, and the public, the regulator and the lawyer all reason in the second while the engineer computes in the first.

One in 2,300 per year is not “safe for 2,300 years”. It is a 25-in-2,300 chance over a twenty-five year plant life — about 1 %. The reciprocal is a unit error that survives because it is rhetorically convenient: it turns a small but real probability into an apparently infinite deferral. Any safety case that uses the reciprocal form should be read as advocacy rather than analysis.

Three ways to reach the same SIL, and why that is not a scandal

The chapter presents ALARP, a risk matrix, a risk graph and LOPA as four methods, and then says plainly that none is more correct than another. Engineers find this unsatisfying. It is worth being precise about why the situation is tolerable. The methods do not disagree about physics; they disagree about how much resolution to buy. A qualitative matrix collapses a continuous frequency axis into five boxes and therefore rounds upwards, because rounding up is the safe direction. LOPA keeps the axis continuous and therefore rounds less. The difference between a SIL 2 and a SIL 1 answer is usually the difference between two resolutions, not between two truths.

What matters is that the method is fixed before the answers are known, applied consistently across the plant, and documented well enough that a second team would reach the same box. A site that uses a matrix for one unit and LOPA for the next has not bought accuracy; it has bought an inconsistency that will be very hard to defend when the two units are compared.

The third axis is where the argument actually happens

Adding a Z axis for “quantity and/or effectiveness of additional layers” is presented as a refinement of the risk matrix. In practice it is the whole negotiation. Every claim that a relief valve, a dike, a blast wall or a trained operator counts as a layer lowers the SIL requirement, and every such claim is a claim about independence that nobody can verify quantitatively.

The four IPL criteria — specificity, independence, dependability, auditability — are the discipline that keeps the negotiation honest. Independence is the one that fails most often, and it fails quietly. A relief valve sharing a nozzle with the instrument tapping is not independent of the pressure transmitter. A dike that is also the bund for the adjacent tank is not specific to this scenario. Neither failure shows up on a P&ID; both are found only by walking the plant.

The human layer has a ceiling, and the ceiling is low

Section 6.10.3 is unusually blunt: even with documented procedures, trained personnel, fifteen minutes to act and an audited compliance record, a probability cap of 1 × 10⁻¹ is the most that may be claimed for a human response. Nine times out of ten, in an emergency, under stress. Anyone who has watched an operator respond to a real trip will recognise the number as generous rather than conservative.

The consequence is structural. A human layer can never deliver better than one decade of risk reduction, which means it can never on its own satisfy a SIL 2 requirement. This is the arithmetic reason behind a design rule that is often stated as a preference: if the hazard needs more than one order of magnitude, an instrumented function has to do the work, and the operator is there to back it up rather than to carry it.

LOPA is arithmetic, and arithmetic is auditable

The worked example is worth reading as a template rather than a case. Four numbers, three multiplications, two comparisons: 10⁻³ against a 10⁻⁴ target, 2.5 × 10⁻⁴ against 10⁻⁵. The shortfalls are ten and twenty-five, the binding one is twenty-five, and twenty-five sits inside the SIL 1 band. Nothing in that chain is a judgement call except the inputs — and the inputs are exactly where the study earns or loses its value.

Note what the example does with the operator. Alarms and operator action are excluded because the control system is itself the initiating event, so no alarm would be generated. That single sentence is the difference between an honest LOPA and a flattering one. It is always possible to add a layer and lower the answer; it is only sometimes true that the layer would still be there when the demand arrives.

SIL is a property of a loop, never of a box

The distinction the chapter insists on — that a logic solver may be rated for SIL 3 while the loop it sits in achieves SIL 1 — is the one most often lost in procurement. Vendors sell SIL capability; systems achieve SIL. A transmitter with a certified failure rate, wired to a certified solver, driving a valve that is stroked once every five years, is a SIL 1 loop with three SIL 3 certificates attached to it.

This is why the phrase “we have a SIL 3 plant” is not merely imprecise but misleading: it suggests a property of the facility when the property belongs to individual functions, each with its own demand rate, its own proof-test interval and its own weak link. The correct unit of conversation is the safety instrumented function, and the correct question is not what it is rated for but what it will actually do on the day it is needed.
