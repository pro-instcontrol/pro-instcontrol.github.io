Issues Relating to Field Devices

Paul Gruhn & Harry Cheddie · ISA (2006)

Chapter 9 · 24 source pages · 3 figures · companion notes
Key terms. Field devices — sensors, impulse lines, final elements, field wiring and everything else landing on the I/O terminals of the logic solver. · Energize to trip — the circuit is normally live, so losing power is the trip command. · Supervised circuit — a loop with an end-of-line resistor, so the logic solver can tell a broken wire from an open contact. · HART — the digital protocol riding on top of the 4–20 mA signal, carrying diagnostic data. · Smart valve positioner — a positioner with diagnostics and partial-stroke capability. · Inferential measurement — inferring one variable from another. · Diverse redundancy — two channels of different technology, maker and firmware, to cut common cause.

Ninety percent of the problems in a safety instrumented system are in the ten percent of the budget that nobody argues about. The logic solver gets the meetings, the certification and the architecture diagrams; the solenoid gets whatever is left in the procurement budget. This chapter is the correction.

One table, and the argument is over

Table 9-1 is four rows long and settles most of the debate in this book. A pressure sensor, four relays, a solenoid and a shutdown valve, tested once a year, come to a total PFD of 0.06 — RRF 16, barely SIL 1. The logic is 8 % of that. The two valves are 50 %. No choice of PLC, no TMR architecture and no certification will move a number whose eighth part is the only part you were negotiating about.

The solenoid deserves particular attention because it is the cheapest item on the list and the second largest contributor. It is a coil of wire in a weatherproof box, asked to sit energised for years in a plant where the ambient temperature swings forty degrees, and it fails by burning out — which is a nuisance trip, not a dangerous failure, which is why nobody counts it as a safety problem until the trips become frequent enough that somebody bypasses it.

Diagnostics are worth more than redundancy

The 1oo1D example in section 9.5.1.1 makes the arithmetic plain. Adding 60 % diagnostic coverage to a single transmitter takes PFD from 0.01 to 0.004 — a factor of 2.5 — for the price of a HART monitor and no additional process connection. A second transmitter in a 1oo2 arrangement buys a much larger factor and costs a tap, a root valve, an impulse line, a cable, an input card and a maintenance procedure. Neither is wrong; the point is that the cheap option is usually tried last.

There is a second, quieter benefit. A transmitter that can tell you it has failed lets you bypass the failed signal and keep producing, instead of tripping the plant on a sensor that is merely broken. That decision — alarm versus trip on a detected fault — is the whole of Table 9-2, and it is a process decision, not an instrument one.

The same idea applies to discrete inputs, where the mechanism is different and just as cheap. A plain contact tells the card whether it is open or closed — two states, one of which is indistinguishable from a broken wire. Fit an end-of-line resistor and the same circuit reports four states, because the card can now see the difference between a closed contact, an open contact, a short and a break. The price is a resistor that can itself fail, and a healthy-state current that the loop must tolerate.

Why the field device is hard to fix

A logic solver is one box from one vendor with one failure rate. A loop is a tap, a root valve, an impulse line, a transmitter, a cable, a junction box, an input card, a solenoid, an air line, an actuator, a stem, a seat and a spring. Nine of those thirteen items are mechanical, most are outdoors, and several are maintained by people who never see the safety requirement specification. This is why the 90 % figure is stable across industries and decades: it is a property of the topology, not of any particular product.

It is also why partial stroke testing spread so quickly. It is the only technique in this book that improves the largest contributor without stopping the process, and section 8.13.1 shows the arithmetic: RRF 80 to RRF 300 for a monthly test at 80 % coverage.

The shared-sensor argument, and when to break it

Section 9.3 gives three reasons not to share a sensor with the BPCS, and the first is the strongest: the SIS exists partly because BPCS components fail, so building it on a BPCS sensor means building it on the thing it is supposed to protect you from. The other two — stricter maintenance procedures and the impossibility of online testing — are practical rather than fundamental.

The exception in section 9.4.1 is worth reading carefully, because it is how most real plants actually operate. Where a separate shutdown valve is impractical, a control valve can carry the function provided the solenoid is tubed directly to the actuator, bypassing the positioner, and provided somebody writes a safety case. The safety case is the price of the exception, and it should be a real document, not a paragraph in a design file.

Two sentences that repay rereading

“Diagnostics do not change the overall failure rate, just the percentage of failures that are detected.” This is the most misunderstood sentence in functional safety. Diagnostics move failures from the undetected column to the detected column; they do not remove them. A device with 99 % diagnostic coverage still fails exactly as often as the same device with none — it just tells you sooner, and telling you sooner is what converts a dangerous undetected failure into a nuisance trip you can fix.

The second is the note under Table 9-2: an alarm should always be generated if a transmitter fault occurs. The logic may be configured to trip or to alarm, and both are defensible, but silence is not. A fault that is detected and not annunciated is worse than a fault that was never detected, because it consumes the diagnostic coverage you paid for and returns nothing.
