Case Study

Paul Gruhn & Harry Cheddie · ISA (2006)

Chapter 16 · 20 source pages · companion notes
Key terms. Case study — a worked example that runs the whole lifecycle on one real furnace. · Fired heater — a furnace heated by direct flame; here, the crude unit charge heater. · Pass — one of several parallel tube runs through the furnace, each with its own flow. · Pilot — a small, continuously burning gas stream that keeps the main flame lit. · K.O. drum — knockout drum: removes liquid droplets from a gas stream before it reaches the burners. · Cause and effect diagram — a cross-reference sheet saying which cause closes which valve. · Nuisance trip — a spurious shutdown. · PSAT — pre-startup acceptance test. · Bypass — a valve opened to test the system without shutting the unit down.

Chapters 1 to 15 each handed over one piece of a procedure. This chapter is where the pieces get assembled: a crude unit charge heater, followed from process hazard analysis to a quarterly test procedure, through all eight lifecycle steps. The emphasis is on the method, not on the answer.

The chapter is worth reading as a whole because of something no single chapter could show — how much of the engineering is bookkeeping. Six tables, one P&ID, four block diagrams and a fourteen-step checklist. The safety argument is not won by a clever calculation; it is won by the fact that the calculation, the cause-and-effect sheet and the test procedure all refer to the same four valves by the same names.

The numbers that decide the design

Two of them do most of the work, and both are small. The pilot gas function needs SIL 3 because a pilot flame failure can explode the furnace and kill people; the two pass-flow functions need SIL 2 because the worst credible outcome is a million-dollar tube failure or a million-dollar fire. That single split in Table 16-2 is what produces the asymmetry in the whole design: only the pilot gas group gets 1oo2 transmitters, only it gets a 1oo2 valve pair on both the pilot and the fuel gas.

The second number is the demand rate. Once a year, stated in section 16.3 almost in passing, is what makes every PFD in section 16.9 a simple multiplication. At one demand per year, with a three-month proof test, the average probability of failure on demand is the failure rate times the undetected fraction times the common-cause factor times half the test interval. No Markov model, no numerical integration, no software. The chapter's arithmetic fits on one page because the demand rate made it possible to fit on one page.

What the PFD calculation actually reveals

Follow the three terms. The transmitters contribute 6.25 × 10⁻⁶. The safety PLC contributes 5 × 10⁻⁵. The valves contribute 2.5 × 10⁻⁴. The valves are forty times the PLC and forty times the transmitters put together.

The design response is visible in Figure 16-5 and it is worth naming: the PFD covers four valves, not two, because losing the pilots requires shutting both the pilot gas and the fuel gas. That is a correct and easily missed detail — the function is not 'close XV-30A/B', it is 'close everything that can feed this fire box'. Counting only the pilot valves would have understated the PFD by half and, more importantly, would have described the wrong function on the cause-and-effect sheet.

And the result is 3.06 × 10⁻⁴ against a SIL 3 limit of 1 × 10⁻³. That is a margin of about three, which sounds comfortable until you notice where it comes from: the margin exists because the valves are 1oo2 and tested quarterly. Change the test interval to annual and the valve term becomes 1 × 10⁻³ on its own — the function fails SIL 3 with nothing else altered. The test interval is not a maintenance detail. It is a design parameter, and it is the one that actually holds the SIL up.

The spurious trip budget is the tighter constraint

Section 16.3 sets two requirements, and they pull in opposite directions: no more than one nuisance trip per year, and SIL 3. Everyone remembers the SIL 3. Almost nobody remembers the nuisance trip limit, and it is the harder of the two.

The calculation gives 2.0 years between spurious trips, against a requirement of one per year. That is a factor of two in hand — but look at the terms. The transmitters are 12.5 years, the PLC is 100 years, and the valves are 2.5 years. The valves are again the binding term, and this time the arithmetic is unforgiving: four valves at λs = 0.1 per year give 1/(4 × 0.1) = 2.5 years, and no amount of transmitter redundancy improves it. The only lever is the safe failure rate of the valves themselves.

This is the same lesson Chapter 14 taught with its two cost cases, arriving here from a different direction: the final element dominates both the safety case and the availability case, and it is the component most often left until last. A project that spends its redundancy budget on 2oo3 transmitters and a fault-tolerant PLC, and then buys the cheapest available shutdown valves, has optimised the wrong end of the loop.

Why the SRS table is the most important artefact

Table 16-3 is thirty-odd rows of mostly one-line answers, and it looks like paperwork. It is not. Read down the 'Details' column and notice how many rows say 'Refer to Table 16-4'. The SRS is deliberately refusing to restate the cause-and-effect sheet, and that refusal is the point: there is exactly one place where the trip points live, and every other document points at it.

The rows that are not pointers are the ones that decide whether the system can be tested. De-energise to trip. Manual reset solenoids. Limit switches to confirm closure. Smart transmitters for diagnostics. Bypass valves for on-line testing, with alarms in the BPCS. Five seconds response. Every one of those is a decision about how the plant will be operated for the next twenty years, taken at the specification stage, and each one is much cheaper to take now than to retrofit.

The bypass row deserves particular attention because it is the row that makes the rest of the chapter possible. Without HV-25 and HV-26, the only way to prove the trip works is to trip the furnace — which means it gets proved rarely, or not at all. With them, the proof test is a fourteen-step procedure an operator can run in an afternoon. The bypass is not a concession to convenience; it is what buys the three-month test interval that the PFD calculation depends on.

The test procedure as a safety document

Look at what the fourteen steps actually do. Steps 2 and 3 open the bypasses and confirm the bypass alarms. Step 4 confirms the furnace is still stable. Only then does step 5 vent PT-7. Step 6 checks the annunciator. Steps 7 to 9 restore, reset and confirm. Step 10 repeats the whole thing for PT-24. Steps 11 and 12 close the bypasses and confirm the alarms cleared.

The order is the safety argument. The bypass alarm is verified before the bypass is relied on, and the bypass closure is verified after the test — because a bypass left open is a disabled shutdown function, and section 5.0 of the procedure says so in as many words: 'Failure to close the bypass valves fully upon completing the test can disable the furnace shutdown functions.' That sentence is the whole reason the procedure exists in written form.

There is also a defect worth noticing, because noticing it is a habit worth building. Step 12 closes the fuel gas bypass HV-26 and then asks the operator to confirm that alarm ZA-25 has cleared. ZA-25 is the pilot gas bypass alarm; the fuel gas one is ZA-26. The book has a typo, and an operator following it literally would confirm the wrong alarm and sign the sheet. This translation corrects it to ZA-26 and says so in the translator's note. Procedures are code; typos in code get executed.

What the case study leaves out, and why it matters

Section 16.4 is unusually honest about scope. It lists eight lifecycle steps as in scope and then names four that are not: lifecycle planning, functional safety management, verification activities, and functional safety assessment. The chapter does the engineering and skips the management system that is supposed to surround it.

That omission is deliberate for a textbook and unacceptable in a project. The eight steps produce a design; the four omitted activities produce the evidence that the design was done competently, by people with defined roles and demonstrated competence, with documentation under control, and with an independent assessment before startup. A plant that installs this exact SIS without the four missing activities has the same hardware and none of the assurance.

Which is the right way to read the whole chapter: as a worked example of the technical steps, not as a template for a project. The tables tell you what to produce. They do not tell you who signs, who checks, or who is allowed to change the cause-and-effect sheet after commissioning — and those three questions are where real safety instrumented systems fail.
