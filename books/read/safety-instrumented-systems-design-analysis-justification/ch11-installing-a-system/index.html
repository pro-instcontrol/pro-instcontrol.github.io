Installing a System

Paul Gruhn & Harry Cheddie · ISA (2006)

Chapter 11 · 13 source pages · 3 figures · companion notes
Key terms. FAT (factory acceptance test) — acceptance testing at the vendor's works, before the system ships. · SAT (site acceptance test) — acceptance testing on site, which is most of what validation consists of. · Validation — demonstrating that the installed system meets the safety requirements specification in all respects; once, for the whole system. · Verification — demonstrating that each phase of the lifecycle met the objectives set for that phase; at every phase. · FSA (functional safety assessment) — an evidence-based investigation judging the functional safety actually achieved. · PSSR — in some countries, the name given to the final assessment before startup. · Free issue — material supplied by the client rather than bought by the contractor. · Force — overwriting an I/O value in a programmable system, a bypass that leaves no visible trace.

The chapter is called installing a system, but what it really covers is the most dangerous fortnight in a safety system's life: the gap between the drawings closing and the plant running. Everything that went wrong quietly during design gets its chance to appear at once, at the moment when fixing it costs the most.

Six per cent is not the number to take comfort from

The Health and Safety Executive examined more than thirty accidents and attributed six per cent of them to installation and commissioning. It reads like a small number, and section 11.1 immediately explains why it is not: the figure counts accidents that happened, not hazards that were created. A safety function that was wired to the wrong terminal and tested against the wrong cause-and-effect row is a protection layer that does not exist, and it will sit there looking healthy for years.

The list of failure modes in 11.1 is worth reading as a list of things that are all invisible at the moment they happen. A wrong termination is invisible. A substandard terminal strip is invisible until it corrodes. A jumper left on a terminal block is invisible until somebody needs the trip. An as-built drawing that was never updated is invisible until the first maintenance outage. None of these announce themselves; the system is quiet either way.

Four gates, four different kinds of evidence

Figure 11-1 is the spine of the chapter, and its argument is that the four gates are not interchangeable. FAT proves that the logic solver and its software left the works matching the specification — it says nothing about field wiring, because at that point there is no field. Installation checks prove that the wiring, tagging and calibration match the drawings, and they are deliberately done before power is applied, so they cannot prove the logic runs. SAT proves the installed system meets the safety requirements specification, and it is the first test that uses the real sensors and the real valves. FSA/PSSR is not a test at all; it is an independent judgement about evidence somebody else gathered.

The order matters as much as the content. Each gate is cheap to pass and expensive to fail, and each one narrows what is left to discover. If you skip FAT because the schedule is tight, you do not save the FAT; you move it to the site, where the same defect costs more, takes longer, and is found by people who did not write the code.

Verification is not validation, and the standard is right to insist

Section 11.2 spends a page on four words that are routinely used as synonyms and are not. Verification is per phase: did this phase meet the objectives set for this phase. Validation is per system, once, after installation: does the whole thing meet the safety requirements specification. Figure 11-2 draws the relationship — validation is a subset of verification, not a peer of it. The confusion has a cost: teams that treat SAT as "validation done" skip the phase-by-phase checks, and then discover during SAT that the cause-and-effect diagram they are testing against was never verified against the hazard study.

The FSA is a third thing again, and the standard's requirement that at least one assessor come from outside the design team is the whole point. An assessment conducted by the people who made the decisions is a review, and reviews of one's own work reliably find nothing. The PSSR, where that name is used, is simply the last of these assessments, moved to the moment just before the hazard is introduced.

The FAT bench is a model of the plant, and models lie in specific ways

Figure 11-3 shows the classic bench: switches for discrete inputs, potentiometers for 4–20 mA, thermocouple simulators, lamps for outputs, each labelled as the field device it stands in for. It is genuinely good value — it finds logic errors, addressing errors, scaling errors and configuration errors before anyone books a flight.

What it cannot find is everything that lives between the terminal and the process. Cable capacitance and induced noise. A shield grounded at both ends. A 24 V supply that sags when forty solenoids pick up together. A valve that takes eleven seconds to stroke when the cause-and-effect diagram assumes three. An instrument that reads correctly in a workshop at 21 °C and drifts at 60 °C. The bench tests the logic solver; only the site tests the loop.

The automated version, with a PC running the script and writing the record, is better than the manual version for a reason that has nothing to do with speed: it produces evidence. A manual FAT produces a technician's memory of a long day. If the FAT is going to be used as proof in an assessment, the proof has to exist on paper.

Free issue is where quality goes to die

Section 11.1 and 11.4 both worry about free issue material, and the worry is well placed. The logic solver, the transmitters and the valves are specified to three decimal places and audited by everyone. The terminal strips, the ferrules, the cable glands and the jumpers are bought by whoever is buying, to whatever standard is cheapest, because nobody wrote a specification for them.

These are exactly the components that decide whether the system works in year eight. A terminal that corrodes open is a dangerous undetected failure in a de-energised circuit. A ferrule that is crimped with the wrong tool is a resistance that drifts. The cost of specifying them properly is trivial against the cost of the outage that finds them.

Bypasses outlive the startups that created them

Both section 11.1 and section 11.9 raise the same problem from two directions: temporary bypasses and forces that were needed to get the plant running and were never removed. Section 11.9 adds the detail that makes it dangerous — forcing an output from the PES is the common method, because it needs no jumper wire, leaves no physical evidence, and in many systems survives a download.

The mitigations the chapter asks for are modest and mostly procedural: an alarm on any forced point, a register, a time limit, shift-by-shift renewal, and access control on who can force at all. None of them is technically difficult. All of them fail when the plant is struggling at three in the morning and the person who could remove the force has gone home.

Startup is the only test that uses the real process

Section 11.9 lists six reasons why first startup is the most hazardous phase, and they compound. The plant is not quite the plant on the drawings. Operations are learning. Systematic design and installation failures choose this moment to surface, because it is the first moment the system is asked to do everything at once. And the components are sitting in their infant mortality period, where the failure rate is highest and falling.

The bathtub curve from chapter 8 is not an abstraction here; it is a schedule. The first weeks of operation are when the hardware is most likely to fail and when the people are least able to cope, which is precisely why the chapter insists that validation be complete before the hazard is introduced, and why the assessment team is given the authority to defer the startup.

The handover signature is a liability document

Section 11.8 is short and easy to skim, but the requirement that each safety function be signed off individually is doing real work. A single signature for the whole system means one deficiency anywhere blocks everything, so in practice it gets signed anyway. A signature per function means the deficiencies are enumerated, owned and dated, and the system can go into service with a written, bounded list of what is not yet right.

That is the whole discipline of this chapter in one sentence: a safety system does not have to be perfect to start up, but it does have to be honest about what it is.
