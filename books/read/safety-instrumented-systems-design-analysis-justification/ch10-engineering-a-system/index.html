Books · Safety Instrumented Systems — Design, Analysis, and Justification · Chapter 10

Engineering a System

Paul Gruhn & Harry Cheddie · ISA (2006)

Chapter 10 · 16 source pages · companion notes
Key terms. PIP — the authors' half-serious name for the factors that decide integrity: technology, architecture, failure rates, diagnostics, power, interfaces, security, test interval. · De-energise to trip — normally live; losing power is the trip, the norm for most SIS. · Energise to trip — normally dead; applying power is the trip, usual in machinery protection and fire and gas. · Line monitoring — measuring a trickle current to tell a broken wire from an open contact. · Forcing — writing a value over an I/O point in a PLC, which is a bypass with no visible evidence. · V-model — every design step on the left leg has a matching verification step on the right. · FPL / LVL / FVL — fixed, limited variability and full variability languages. · Emulation / simulation — running the logic on the target processor versus on the development PC.

This is the chapter about everything that does not fit in the model. Panel doors, password stickers, lunch buckets on bus bars, the engineer who left the project in month four. None of it appears in a Markov diagram, and all of it decides whether the system still works in year twelve.

The specification is the failure mode

The 44 % figure from the UK Health and Safety Executive is the most useful number in this chapter, and it is not about hardware at all. Nearly half of system problems trace back to a specification that was wrong, incomplete, or written after the purchase order. Section 10.1.1 describes the mechanism precisely: vague scope plus a deadline that has already passed, which produces scope creep, which produces a renegotiation, which produces a system that meets the contract and not the plant.

The V-model in Figure 10-1 is the structural answer, and its point is often missed. It is not that you test at the end; it is that each test is designed at the same time as the thing it tests. A requirement with no acceptance criterion is not a requirement, it is a wish, and it will be discovered as a defect during commissioning when the fix is most expensive.

Energise to trip versus de-energise to trip

Section 10.2.1 contains a trap that catches people who think they are being clever. A normally energised system fails safe by dropping out: loss of power trips the plant. A normally de-energised system, common in fire and gas and in machinery protection, fails safe by energising — so loss of power means no trip, and an inverted logic solver gives you exactly the wrong behaviour.

Everything else in that section follows from this one fact. The latching solenoids, the alarm fuses, the line monitoring: each exists because in a de-energised system a broken wire and a healthy circuit look identical. That is the price of choosing the architecture that does not trip the plant every time a fuse blows, and it is a price worth paying only if somebody actually installs the monitoring.

Common cause is an installation problem, not a component problem

The nuclear industry figure — a quarter of all failures attributable to common cause — is usually read as an argument for diversity. It is better read as an argument for looking at the room. Two diverse transmitters on one process tap share a plugging failure. Two redundant controllers in one cabinet share a fan, a door and a flood. The Beta factor in chapter 8 quantifies this, but the fix is architectural and it is cheap: separate the taps, separate the cabinets, separate the power feeds.

The authors' line — if someone can, someone inevitably will — is the right frame for the whole chapter. Every bypass that can be left in place will be left in place. Every password that can be shared will be shared. Every forced I/O point that leaves no visible trace will eventually be left forced over a weekend.

Bypass administration is a safety function

The Piper Alpha reference in section 10.2.9 is not rhetorical. A permit-to-work system that fails is a bypass that nobody removes, and 167 people died because a procedure was not followed. The design consequences are concrete: a bypass should be visible at the panel, should have a time limit, should require shift-by-shift renewal, and should not hide the true state of the field device while it is in place.

Forcing is the quiet version of the same problem. It is a bypass with no jumper wire and no evidence, available from a laptop, and in many systems it survives a download. If the system permits it, somebody will do it at three in the morning during a startup.

Software: stop trying to measure it, start trying to specify it

Section 10.3.3 is refreshingly honest. Software does not wear out, so the failure rate model of chapter 8 simply does not apply; the standards committees looked at the dozens of quantification techniques, saw that they disagreed by orders of magnitude, and abandoned the exercise. What they kept was the lifecycle, because a specification defect is a design defect, and a design defect in software is a permanent one.

The ladder-logic warning is worth repeating: reusing the relay system's logic diagram as the PLC program carries forward every requirement error of the original design, and adds a new class of hazard the original never had. The relay was fail-safe by physics. The PLC is fail-safe only by configuration.
