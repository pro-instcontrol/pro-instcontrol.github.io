Justification for a Safety System

Paul Gruhn & Harry Cheddie · ISA (2006)

Chapter 14 · 19 source pages · 4 figures · companion notes
Key terms. Justification — showing, in numbers, that the money spent on a safety system is money well spent. · Risk receptor — what the risk lands on: people, the environment, production, capital equipment, company image. · Nuisance trip / spurious trip — the safety system shuts the plant down when nothing demanded it. · Lifecycle cost — the total cost of owning the system, from specification to disposal. · Present value (PV) — what a stream of future payments is worth today. · RRF — risk reduction factor, equal to 1/PFD. · MTTFspurious — mean time to a spurious trip.

Every chapter of this book up to here has been about making a safety function work: specifying it, calculating its PFD, testing it, changing it safely. This chapter is about the conversation that happens before any of that — the one where somebody asks what the system is worth, and expects an answer in money.

The book's answer is more interesting than a cost-benefit table. It says a safety system has three separate claims on the money: it protects people, it protects the plant, and it avoids shutting the plant down when nothing is wrong. Only the first is easy to defend, and it is the one that most companies have already settled by policy. The argument that actually gets argued is the second and third.

The running example is a vessel with two feeds held in ratio by the BPCS, protected against overpressure by a relief valve. Everything in that arrangement is control: the level controller trimming two flow controllers is doing what a control system is for. The safety function is the separate layer that closes the feeds when the BPCS has already failed — and the whole of this chapter is about what that layer is worth.

Four failure modes, four different arguments

Section 14.2 splits the usual safe/dangerous pair into four modes, and the split is worth taking seriously because each mode attacks a different part of the business case. A dangerous undetected failure attacks the safety case and is invisible until somebody tests for it. A dangerous detected failure attacks availability: the function is gone until somebody repairs it, and the question becomes how long the plant can run without it. A degraded mode attacks nothing, which is exactly why redundancy sells. A nuisance trip attacks production directly and, through the bypasses it provokes, attacks safety indirectly.

That last mechanism is the one worth dwelling on. The chapter quotes 29 CFR 1910.119 making an argument that sounds almost heretical in a safety book: shutting down is itself dangerous, and keeping shutdowns to a minimum promotes safety. The regulation is right, and the practical consequence is that a safety system which trips too often gets bypassed. The bypass is the real failure mode, and it is caused by good reliability engineering done badly — or not done at all.

Why the numbers are smaller than they look

Example 1 is the chapter's most useful reality check. A million-dollar explosion, once every five hundred years, reduced by a factor of a hundred. The annual saving is $1,980. Discounted at six percent over twenty years, the entire justified expenditure is $22,710.

Twenty-two thousand dollars for a safety system that prevents a million-dollar explosion. That number surprises people, and the surprise is the lesson: a risk reduction factor of 100 sounds enormous, but it is applied to an event that almost never happens, and then discounted over twenty years. The arithmetic is not arguing against safety systems. It is arguing that the financial case for a safety system is usually thin, and that pretending otherwise is how a justification gets written that nobody believes.

Which is why the chapter immediately notes what the example leaves out: operation and maintenance costs are assumed negligible. Put them back and the twenty-two thousand shrinks further. The honest conclusion is that most safety systems cannot be justified on equipment damage alone — and that this is a reason to justify them on the right grounds rather than to inflate the grounds that do not hold.

The reliability argument is the strong one

Example 2 makes the opposite point. Seventy thousand dollars to halve the spurious trip rate, against a ten-thousand-dollar annual benefit, borrowed at five percent over twenty years at $5,617 a year. That is a payback of about seven years and a clear yes.

The asymmetry is the whole chapter. Protecting equipment from a rare explosion is hard to justify because the event is rare. Avoiding a nuisance trip is easy to justify because the event is frequent, its cost is known from the production ledger, and the benefit arrives every year. Reliability spending pays back on a schedule a finance department recognises; safety spending pays back in events that never happen, which is a much harder line item to defend.

This is why the chapter insists that justification is usually the control systems engineer's job. The safety case was settled upstream by the SIL determination and the company's risk tolerance. What remains to argue is the increment above the SIL: the extra redundancy, the better diagnostics, the shorter test interval. Those are reliability purchases wearing safety clothing, and they need the reliability arithmetic.

Lifecycle cost turns the argument around

The two cases in section 14.7 are the same plant, the same hazards and the same SIL requirement, solved two ways. Case 1 is single transmitters and relay logic; Case 2 is triplicated 2oo3 transmitters and a fault-tolerant safety PLC. Both meet SIL 1. Case 1 has a PFD of 0.0165 and trips spuriously every three years. Case 2 has a PFD of 0.0100 and trips every five years.

Case 1 is the arrangement almost every plant already has: two single transmitters feeding one relay block that closes two shutdown valves. There is no redundancy anywhere, so a failed transmitter loses one function and a failed relay loses both — and the test interval has to be short enough to compensate.

Case 2 replaces the sensors and the logic but leaves the valves alone, which is where the money is and where the reliability is not.

Case 2 costs $38,000 more to buy and $74,160 less to own. The mechanism is worth spelling out because it is not obvious from the purchase order: the 2oo3 sensors and the safety PLC can be tested every three years instead of every six months, and that single change removes the largest annual cost line in Case 1 — $25,000 of manual testing. The redundancy did not just buy safety margin; it bought test interval, and test interval is where the money is.

There is a sting in the tail that the chapter states plainly and then moves past. The valves are single in both cases, and they dominate both the PFD and the spurious trip rate of Case 2. Doubling the sensor count and buying a fault-tolerant PLC reduced the PFD from 0.0165 to 0.0100 — a factor of 1.65 — because 0.0100 of the 0.0100 is the valves. The expensive redundancy is propping up a number that the cheapest component in the loop controls.

What a justification document should actually contain

Reading sections 14.3 to 14.9 together, a defensible justification has four parts, and most real ones are missing at least two.

  1. A statement of which risk receptor is being protected. Personnel risk is settled by policy; equipment, production and environmental risk are not, and lumping them together hides the fact that only some of them carry a financial case.
  2. The annual loss avoided, not the event loss avoided. A million-dollar event every five hundred years is two thousand dollars a year; presenting the million is how a weak case is made to look strong.
  3. The present value of that annual figure over the asset life, at a stated interest rate. Without discounting, twenty years of savings is overstated by roughly a factor of two at five percent.
  4. The cost side computed the same way — including the annual costs, not just the capital. A justification that compares a discounted benefit against an undiscounted capital cost is not a comparison.

And one thing that is not on the list: a number for a human life. The chapter is explicit that companies avoid publishing or using such figures, and it does not supply one. That reticence is not evasion. A safety system justified by a body count is a safety system that will be re-justified downward the moment somebody finds a cheaper body count, and the regulatory and ethical floor exists precisely so that the calculation never has to be made.

The sentence to remember

Section 14.9 contains the observation that reorganises the whole chapter: for decades people optimised the logic box, and modelling shows the field devices dominate. In the worked example the logic system is 10 percent of the capital cost in Case 1 and 21 percent in Case 2, while the valves alone contribute 61 percent of the PFD in Case 1 and essentially all of it in Case 2. The place where money buys safety is not the place where money is usually spent.

That is also the answer to the question a project manager asks when handed a lifecycle cost analysis: why are we comparing twenty-year totals for a decision being made today? Because the decision being made today is which system to own for twenty years. The purchase order is the smallest part of it.
