Introduction

“Engineering responsibility should not require the stimulation that comes in the wake of catastrophe.” — S. C. Florman. Safety instrumented systems are designed to bring a process, or specific equipment within it, to a safe state when hazardous conditions arise. This chapter defines what an SIS is, who the book is for, why it was written, the confusion that pervades the industry, and the guidelines, standards and regulations that govern the field.

A definition that excludes more than it includes

A safety instrumented system is a combination of sensors, logic solver and final elements that takes the process to a safe state when it is asked to. The definition is narrow on purpose, and the exclusions do most of the work. It excludes the basic process control system, which is there to keep the plant running rather than to stop it. It excludes the relief valve and the dike, which are protection but not instrumentation. It excludes the operator, whose response time cannot be demonstrated. What is left is the one layer in the onion that is designed to be dormant — and dormancy is the property that makes the rest of this book necessary.

That is why the chapter spends so little time on what an SIS is and so much on what it is not. A control loop that stops working announces itself within minutes. A safety function that stops working announces nothing at all, and will go on announcing nothing for years. Everything that follows — the SIL arithmetic, the proof tests, the change control — exists to compensate for a component that is silent by design.

Six confusions with one cause

Section 1.4 lists six areas where the industry argues with itself: technology, redundancy, field devices, test intervals, vendor claims, and certification versus prior use. They look like six separate arguments. They are one argument seen from six angles, and the cause is structural: the field is split between vendors who sell components, engineering contractors who sell systems, and end users who own the consequence — and all three use the same words to mean different things.

A SIL 3 certificate on a transmitter, a SIL 3 claim for a loop, and a SIL 3 requirement in a safety requirements specification are three different statements, and only one of them is about a plant. The vendor's document says what the device can do under stated conditions; the loop claim says what the installation achieves, which is usually less; the requirement says what the hazard needs, which is usually more than either. Most of the confusion in the chapter is somebody quoting one of those three numbers in a conversation about another.

Two of the six deserve singling out because they recur throughout the book. The first is redundancy, which is treated as a property a system has rather than a claim about which failures are independent. Two channels in one cabinet, on one power supply, calibrated by one technician with one procedure, are redundant in the drawing and single in the failure. The second is the field device question, which chapter 9 answers with a table showing that the logic solver is a small fraction of the loop's unreliability while absorbing most of the specification effort.

Two standards, two audiences

The most useful thing in section 1.5 is the distinction between IEC 61508 and IEC 61511, because it explains why the same conversation produces different numbers depending on who is in the room. IEC 61508 is the umbrella standard, written for the makers of devices: it defines what a component must do to be called SIL 2, and it is what a vendor's certificate refers to. IEC 61511 — published in the United States as ANSI/ISA-84.00.01 — is written for the users of those devices: it defines what a plant must do, starting from the hazard rather than from the component.

A vendor cannot certify a plant, and a plant cannot be built from certificates. That asymmetry is the reason the book keeps returning to the safety requirements specification: it is the only document that belongs to the user, and the only one that can connect a hazard to a purchase order.

The standards changed direction, and moved the burden

Section 1.6 describes a shift that took about twenty years and is still incomplete. The older documents were prescriptive: use two sensors, use a redundant logic solver, test annually. The newer ones are performance-based: achieve this probability of failure on demand, and demonstrate that you have. The prescriptive form is easy to audit and impossible to argue with; the performance form is hard to audit and possible to argue with in every direction.

What moved is the burden of proof. Under a prescriptive rule, compliance is the deliverable. Under a performance rule, the deliverable is an argument — and an argument can be made badly, made optimistically, or made by someone who does not know what they are doing. That is not a defect of the newer standards; it is the price of letting a competent engineer design for the actual hazard instead of for the worst case in a committee room. But it does mean that the quality of a safety system now depends on the quality of the reasoning behind it, which is why the rest of the book is about method rather than about equipment.

The failure mode is complacency

Sections 1.7 to 1.9 are short and read like asides. They are the most important pages in the chapter. The argument is that the industry's characteristic failure is not ignorance — the standards are widely read and the equipment is generally good — but confidence. A plant that has run for fifteen years without a serious incident begins to treat that record as evidence about the system rather than as evidence about the absence of demands.

Nothing in this book protects against that. A proof test that is signed without being performed, a bypass that outlives its permit, a cause-and-effect sheet that was correct in 2004 and has been edited four times since: each is invisible in the incident statistics right up to the moment it is the only thing in them. The chapter's closing line is that there is always more to learn, and the reason it is the closing line is that the alternative — deciding you already know — is the specific attitude that the rest of the book is written against.
