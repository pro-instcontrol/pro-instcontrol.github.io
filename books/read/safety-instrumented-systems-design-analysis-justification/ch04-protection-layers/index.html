Protection Layers

Paul Gruhn & Harry Cheddie · ISA (2006)

Chapter 4 · 13 source pages · 3 figures · companion notes
Key terms. Prevention layer — reduces the probability of the event. · Mitigation layer — reduces the consequences after it has happened. · Defense in depth — several independent, diverse layers. · LOPA — layer of protection analysis, the semi-quantitative method that assigns a risk reduction factor to each layer. · Risk reduction factor (RRF) — the inverse of that layer's probability of failing on demand. · Diversification — using different technologies in different layers. · F&G — fire and gas detection. · Inherently safe — safe by virtue of the design itself, with no added devices.

Accidents rarely have a single cause. This chapter retells Bhopal — not for the casualty figures, but because the sequence shows a dozen separate decisions, each reasonable in its own context and each small, lining up. It then sorts the protection layers into prevention and mitigation, and explains why cramming more functions into one layer makes the whole plant less safe rather than more.

Every layer was removed for a good reason

Read the Bhopal sequence as a list of engineering decisions rather than as a tragedy. The refrigeration was switched off to save money. The alarm threshold was raised to match the new storage temperature, which is a perfectly logical thing to do once the refrigeration is off. The scrubber was left idle because the plant was not producing. The flare was down for maintenance, which is when flares are normally maintained. The slip blinds were not fitted because the job was a wash-out, not a vessel entry. The gauges were disbelieved because they were known to be unreliable.

Not one of those decisions was made by someone trying to cause an accident. Each was made by someone optimising the thing in front of them — cost, schedule, production, nuisance alarms. The layers failed not because they were badly designed but because each was individually negotiable, and the negotiation was always won by whoever was closest to the immediate problem. That is the argument for independence in one sentence: a layer that can be switched off by the person it inconveniences is not a layer.

Prevention and mitigation are different businesses

The distinction sounds academic until you try to buy one. A prevention layer competes with production for the same equipment, the same instrument, the same maintenance window; its value is invisible because the accidents it prevents do not appear in any report. A mitigation layer sits outside the production path and is easier to justify, but by definition it only pays out after the plant has already failed.

This is why the onion diagram is drawn as concentric rings rather than as a list. The innermost rings are the ones the operating team touches every day, and they are therefore the ones most exposed to the pressure to keep running. The outermost rings — the dikes, the flare, the evacuation plan — are touched by almost nobody, and they are the ones that still work when everything inside has been compromised. A plant with excellent inner layers and no outer ones is a plant that has never had a bad day.

Alarms are the weakest layer, and the most used

Alarms occupy a strange position: they are cheap, they are easy to add, and they are the layer most often counted on in a hazard study. They are also the layer with the worst record, for a reason that has nothing to do with the instruments. An alarm transfers the problem to a human being under time pressure, and the human being is the component whose behaviour is least predictable and least testable.

The 17,000-message DCS printout is the extreme case of a general rule: an alarm system's value collapses as its count rises. A single high-pressure alarm at three in the morning gets a response. The same alarm among two hundred others in the same hour gets a shrug, because the operator has learned — correctly, from experience — that almost all of them are noise. Alarm rationalisation is therefore not housekeeping; it is the only thing that keeps the layer alive.

The finding that people make the wrong decision 99 % of the time when given under a minute to choose is worth sitting with. It does not mean operators are incompetent. It means that a layer whose success depends on a fast, correct decision under stress has a performance that cannot be claimed with any confidence, and should not be assigned a serious risk reduction factor in a LOPA study.

Why more functions in one box makes the plant less safe

The Volkswagen Beetle analogy in the source is worth keeping because it is quantitative rather than rhetorical. A protection layer's claimed risk reduction factor is a property of the layer, not of the processor it runs on. Moving the fire and gas logic into the BPCS does not add a factor of ten to the BPCS; it subtracts one, because the combined system now shares a failure mode that neither had alone, and because the IEC standards cap the BPCS claim at ten precisely to reflect that.

The same logic explains why the standards are so insistent about field devices. A shared transmitter is a shared failure, and a shared impulse line is a shared failure that no amount of logic redundancy can see. Diversification is not a preference for old technology; it is the recognition that the cheapest way to avoid a common cause failure is to make the two paths physically incapable of sharing one.
