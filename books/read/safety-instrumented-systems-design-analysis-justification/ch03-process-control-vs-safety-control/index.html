Process Control vs. Safety Control

Paul Gruhn & Harry Cheddie · ISA (2006)

Chapter 3 · 15 source pages · companion notes
Key terms. BPCS — the basic process control system, doing the everyday regulating. · SIS — the safety instrumented system, taking the process to a safe state. · SIF — one safety instrumented function inside a SIS. · Revealed failure — a failure that stops the system working, so it is seen at once. · Unrevealed failure — a failure with no symptom until the system is needed. · Common cause — one stressor taking out several items at once. · Beta factor — the share of failures that can hit redundant legs together. · Systematic failure — a design or process error that repeats under the same conditions.

This is a short chapter, but it lays the foundation for the whole book: it answers why the control system and the safety system must be two different systems rather than two programs in one processor. The answer has nothing to do with computer reliability — it lies in the fact that the two systems have different failure modes, different users, and different rules about change.

Two systems, two jobs, two sets of habits

A control system is judged by how well it holds the plant at the set point. A safety system is judged by whether it still works on the day it is needed — which may be a day five years from now, after five years in which it did nothing at all. Those two judgements cannot be satisfied by the same design philosophy, because they reward opposite things.

Control rewards accessibility. The operator must be able to change a set point at three in the morning without waking anybody, put a loop in manual, force an output, tune a controller. Every one of those actions is a normal, desirable, safety-improving act in a control system, and every one of them is a potentially catastrophic act if applied to a safety function. A single system cannot be simultaneously easy to change in one place and impossible to change in another, when both places are the same engineering database.

Safety rewards inertia. Nothing should change, nobody should touch it, and every change should require a signature, a review and a record. The two philosophies are not in tension because one is careless; they are in tension because they are both right, about different things.

Revealed and unrevealed: the asymmetry that decides everything

The deepest difference is not philosophical but statistical. A control loop announces its own failure. If a flow controller dies, the flow stops following the set point, the operator sees it within minutes, and the failure is repaired. The failure is revealed, and the time to repair it is short, and the probability that the loop is dead at some random moment is small.

A safety function announces nothing. It sits at logic one, output energised, valve open, year after year. If its sensor has drifted out of range, or its output card has failed energised, or its logic has been bypassed and never restored, the plant looks exactly the same as it does when everything is healthy. The failure is unrevealed, and the only thing that converts it into a revealed failure is a proof test — or a demand, which is the same test conducted at the worst possible moment.

This asymmetry explains the otherwise puzzling fact that safety systems need far more diagnostics than control systems, even though they are simpler. A control system's diagnostics exist to help maintenance; a safety system's diagnostics exist because without them there is no information at all about whether the system is alive. It also explains why the standards' insistence on separation is not about redundancy. Two systems in one box can be perfectly redundant electrically and still share the failure that matters most: the one nobody can see.

The standards say separate; the reason is human

Every document quoted in this chapter — the HSE two-part volume, the CCPS guidelines, IEC 61508, ISA-84, API 14C and 554, NFPA 85, IEEE 603 — arrives at the same requirement from a different direction, and the wording ranges from the absolute (“shall not be combined”) to the negotiable (“to the extent that…”). It is tempting to read that range as a difference in conviction. It is more accurately a difference in how much each document trusts the management system that will operate the plant.

The prescriptive standards — NFPA 85 in particular — come from industries where the consequence of a mistake is immediate and public, and where the regulator can afford to be blunt. The performance-based ones come from industries where the same plant may be run by a large competent operator or by a small one with three engineers, and where a rule written for the worst case would be unaffordable for the best. Neither approach is wrong; they simply put the burden of judgement in different places.

What every one of them assumes, however, is that human beings will eventually do something they should not. The bypass that is never removed, the alarm that is silenced because it is a nuisance, the interlock deleted during a commissioning push and never restored — the audit story in section 3.5.1 is not an unusual case, it is the normal end state of a system that makes the wrong action easy. Separation is not a technical fix for that; it is the recognition that the fix has to be organisational, and that the organisation needs a physical boundary to defend.

Beta factor: a number that admits it is a guess

The beta factor is one of the few places in functional safety where the arithmetic openly confesses. It says: of all the failures that could take out one leg of a redundant system, this fraction will take out the others too. A beta of ten percent turns a triplicated processor into a single processor with a tenth of the failure rate, which is exactly what it deserves to be called — a model, not a measurement.

The value of the model is that it forces the question. Once you have written down that ten percent of failures are common cause, you have to ask which ten percent, and the answer is almost never in the hardware. It is in the things the legs share: the same power supply, the same cabinet, the same firmware revision, the same technician with the same calibration procedure, the same impulse line, the same fibre route. Redundancy that shares any of those is redundancy on paper only, and the beta factor is the instrument that makes the paper honest.
