Functional Testing

Paul Gruhn & Harry Cheddie · ISA (2006)

Chapter 12 · 14 source pages · 3 figures · companion notes
Key terms. Functional test — the periodic test that finds dangerous failures the system does not reveal by itself. · Proof test interval (TI) — the time between two tests. · Test effectiveness (ET) — the fraction of failures the test actually finds, 0 to 1. · System lifetime (SL) — how long the system exists between complete replacements; a failure the test missed stays latent for all of it. · PFDavg — the average probability that the system fails to respond to a demand. · RRF — risk reduction factor, 1/PFD. · Force — overwriting an I/O value; not a test. · As-found / as-left — the condition recorded before and after repair.

A SIL is a number on paper until somebody actually goes out and tests the loop. This chapter is about that: what to test, how often, who does it, and why testing more often is not the same as being safer.

Everything fails; the only question is when you find out

Section 12.1 puts the argument in five words: everything fails, it is just a matter of when. The whole chapter follows from the second half of that sentence. A control loop is active, so its failures announce themselves — the reading goes wrong, the operator sees it, somebody fixes it. A safety loop is dormant. It sits at its healthy state doing nothing visible, and a dangerous failure leaves it sitting there looking exactly as healthy as before.

That asymmetry is why the first of the three reasons in 12.2 is the important one: safety systems get treated like control systems, and the assumption that failures will reveal themselves is carried across from a system where it happens to be true. It is not true here. A welded-shut relay, a plugged impulse line, a valve that has not moved in four years — none of these produce an alarm. They produce a demand that is not met.

The test interval is a design parameter, not a maintenance preference

Equation 12.1 is one line long and it reorders the priorities of everyone who reads it. PFDavg = λd·TI/2. The failure rate of the hardware and the interval between tests enter the same expression with the same weight. A system built from mediocre devices and tested diligently can beat a system built from excellent devices and tested once a decade, and it will usually cost less.

The corollary is uncomfortable for anyone who treats testing as a maintenance chore. If TI is a design parameter, then it belongs in the safety requirements specification, it belongs in the SIL calculation, and the number in the calculation has to be the number the site actually achieves. A verification that assumes a one-year interval while the plant gets round to it every four years is not a conservative calculation; it is a wrong one, and it is wrong in the dangerous direction.

Test effectiveness is the number nobody measures

Equation 12.2 introduces ET, and it is the most quietly damning term in the chapter. The first term assumes the test finds the failures; the second term assumes it does not, and lets them sit for the whole system lifetime SL. With ET at 95 per cent, a tenth of the dangerous failures are effectively never tested at all — they are only discovered when the system is replaced.

The worked example makes the scale concrete. The same valve, same failure rate, same replacement policy: tested annually it lands in SIL 1 with an RRF of 55, never tested it lands in SIL 0 with an RRF of 8. Nothing about the hardware changed. The seven-fold difference is entirely the test.

The uncomfortable part is that ET is almost never measured. It is asserted in the calculation, then assumed thereafter. The only way to know it is to record as-found conditions honestly and compare them with what the test claims to detect — which is precisely what the five record items in 12.2.1 exist for.

The optimum test interval is not zero

Figure 12-2 is the counter-intuitive one, and it is worth sitting with. The curve is not monotonic. Testing more often improves availability of the function but takes the system out of service for the duration of each test, and at some point the second effect overtakes the first. A simplex system in bypass has an availability of zero, not 99 per cent; a triplicated system in test is a dual system.

So there is a genuine optimum, and it is a calculable number rather than a matter of opinion. For the parameters drawn here it lands near nine months. The practical lesson is not the exact figure — it depends on the loop — but that the curve has two arms with two different causes, and that a test policy justified by "more is better" is as unexamined as a policy justified by "we do it at turnaround".

Forcing is not testing

Section 12.2.2 draws a line that gets crossed constantly in practice: forcing an input or an output in the logic solver is not a test. A force proves that the software responds to a value somebody typed. It proves nothing about the transmitter, the cable, the impulse line, the solenoid or the valve, because none of them is in the circuit.

Forcing is worse than a jumper in one specific way. A jumper on a terminal is at least visible to the next person who opens the cabinet. A force lives in the controller, survives downloads in many systems, and is invisible to anyone not logged in and looking. That is why the chapter wants forcing confined to maintenance and why chapter 11 wants an alarm on every forced point.

One procedure per function, written by the people who will run it

The sample form in 12.6.1 looks bureaucratic until you notice what it asks for in item 2.0 and item 5.0. Item 2.0 wants one named person with overall responsibility — not a department, a person. Item 5.0 wants the hazards of the test itself written down, which is the item most often skipped and the one that gets people hurt: a test that closes a valve on a live process is a hazard in its own right, and it needs to be assessed as one.

Testability, meanwhile, is designed rather than discovered. The loop in Figure 12-3 has an isolation valve so the transmitter can be pressurised, a bypass valve so the process keeps running around the shutdown valve, and two limit switches confirming that the valve actually reached its end position. Three additions, and a unit shutdown becomes a twenty-minute exercise — which is the only reason the interval in the calculation is achievable at all.

The chapter's insistence that operations and maintenance help write the procedure is not politeness. A procedure written by an engineer in an office describes the system as designed. A procedure written with the technician describes the system as it is, including the valve that has to be tapped to seat and the junction box that needs a second person to hold the door.

The record is the deliverable

The five record items in 12.2.1 are short, and the last one carries the weight: results, including as-found and as-left conditions. As-found is the only honest measure of whether the test works and whether the assumed failure rate was right. As-left is the only evidence that the loop went back into service in the condition the calculation assumes.

A test without a record is an anecdote. It cannot be audited, it cannot update a failure rate, and it cannot defend the plant in an inquiry. The chapter closes by asking for periodic audits of the records precisely so that the assumptions made at design time can be compared with what the plant has actually been doing — which is the only feedback loop the safety lifecycle has.
