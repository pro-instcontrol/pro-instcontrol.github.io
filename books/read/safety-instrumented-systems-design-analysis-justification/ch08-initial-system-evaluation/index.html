Initial System Evaluation

Paul Gruhn & Harry Cheddie · ISA (2006)

Chapter 8 · 35 source pages · 8 figures · companion notes
Key terms. Lambda — failure rate, failures per unit time; λ = 1/MTBF. · MTBF — mean time between failures; not the same thing as life. · MTTFspurious — mean time to a nuisance trip. · PFDavg — average probability of failure on demand. · RRF — risk reduction factor, the reciprocal of PFD. · SA — safety availability, 1 − PFD. · Diagnostic coverage — the fraction of failures the system finds by itself. · Beta factor — the fraction of one channel's failures that take out the whole system. · TI / TD — test interval and test duration. · SFF — safe failure fraction, the number you look up in the fault tolerance tables.

This is the chapter that tells you the arithmetic is unreliable, and then makes you do the arithmetic anyway. Both halves matter. The formulas are how you find out which component is killing your safety performance; the caveats are why you must never quote the answer to three significant figures in a meeting.

Safe, dangerous, detected, hidden

Before any formula, the vocabulary. A failure is described by two questions asked independently: does it leave the process in a safe state or remove the protection, and does it announce itself or not. Crossing them gives four cells, and the whole discipline lives in one of them — dangerous and undetected. A dangerous failure that announces itself is a maintenance problem. A dangerous failure that hides is the only thing a proof test actually has to find, and the only thing the PFD formula is counting.

The reason the vocabulary has to be this precise is that “availability” is a word borrowed from the control world, where it is the only thing that matters. A safety system is judged on the opposite axis: it is allowed to be unavailable as often as it likes, provided every one of those occasions is a trip rather than a silence. Two systems with identical availability figures can differ by three orders of magnitude in the number that decides whether the plant is protected.

The four numbers that decide everything

Strip away the vendor literature and a safety instrumented function is four numbers: the failure rate of the sensor, the failure rate of the logic solver, the failure rate of the final element, and how often somebody actually tests all three. Everything else — architecture, diagnostics, certification, the glossy brochure — is a way of moving those four numbers around.

Section 8.10 to 8.13 does this four times in a row, and the sequence is worth reading as a single argument rather than as four worked examples. A relay panel of ten relays lands at RRF 3,300. Replace it with a general-purpose PLC and the number falls to 220 — a full order of magnitude worse, with the same nuisance-trip performance. Replace that with a TMR system and the number becomes 16,000,000, which is nonsense. Add a 1 % common-cause factor and the nuisance-trip figure collapses from 45,000 years to 1,100. Then bolt on eight non-redundant field devices and the whole thing comes back down to RRF 29.

Why the field devices always win

The last step is the one people skip. A logic solver is one box; a loop has a sensor, a cable, a solenoid, a valve body, a stem, a seat and a spring. The valve is the only part of the loop that is asked to move after sitting still for a year in a dirty process. It is unsurprising that it dominates, and yet the purchasing conversation is almost always about the logic solver, because that is where the specification sheet is.

Partial stroke testing is the interesting compromise in section 8.13.1, and the arithmetic explains why it spread so fast in the late 1990s. A monthly partial stroke at 80 % coverage takes a single valve from RRF 80 to RRF 300 — from the bottom of SIL 1 to the middle of SIL 2 — without ever stopping the process. Nothing else in this book buys that much for so little. The catch is the coverage factor: nobody can measure it, the claims run from 60 % to 90 %, and the whole benefit rests on it.

MTBF is not life

The match analogy in section 8.5.1 is the clearest explanation of this in the literature. A match has a very low failure rate per hour of storage and therefore an enormous MTBF — minutes or hours — while its actual life is a few seconds. The two numbers answer different questions. MTBF answers how often one unit in a population fails per unit time; life answers how long one unit lasts. Confusing them produces the classic maintenance plan that replaces every device at the MTBF, which throws away 63 % of the good ones.

The same confusion sits behind the nines argument. Ninety-nine percent availability sounds excellent and can mean a nuisance trip every month with four minutes of downtime each. Ninety-nine point nine nine percent sounds barely different and can mean one trip every ten years. Both are true statements about different plants.

Three ways to draw the same system

Section 8.7 presents three modelling methods, and the choice between them is a choice about what you are willing to assume. A reliability block diagram is the simplest: blocks in series multiply their probabilities of working, blocks in parallel multiply their probabilities of failing, and the diagram makes the weak link visible at a glance. It also assumes every block is independent, which is exactly the assumption that hurts.

A fault tree works the other way round — top-down from the event you are trying to prevent, through the combinations of failures that could cause it, down to individual components. Its advantage is that it can carry the field devices and the process itself in one structure, so the answer is not confined to the logic solver. Its limitation is equally clear: it can only model failures somebody has already thought of.

A Markov model abandons the drawing entirely and describes the system as states and transition rates. That buys the three things the other two methods cannot express — time, repair and the test interval — and it costs a matrix to solve and a set of transition rates nobody measured. It is the right tool when the question is about test intervals; it is the wrong tool when the question is about whether the team understands the plant.

The models disagree with reality for a reason

Section 8.12 is the honest heart of the chapter. The TMR calculation produces a risk reduction factor of sixteen million, and the authors immediately ask whether SIL 7 exists. It does not, and the reason is that the model contains only the failures somebody thought of. Common cause is the largest of the missing terms, and a Beta factor of 1 % — one failure in a hundred taking out all three channels — removes a factor of forty from the answer.

This is why IEC 61511 put the fault tolerance tables in. If the arithmetic alone decided the architecture, a clever engineer with optimistic failure rates could justify a simplex logic solver at SIL 3 and be technically correct. The tables say: no. At SIL 3 you need two channels regardless of what your spreadsheet says, because the spreadsheet cannot see the failure mode that takes out both.

Reading the tables in section 8.14

The two fault tolerance tables look bureaucratic and are actually the most useful pages in the chapter. Read them as a floor, not a target. Table 8-2 tells you the minimum redundancy for a programmable logic solver at each SIL and each band of safe failure fraction; Table 8-3 does the same for field devices and non-programmable solvers, and is stricter because field devices have far less diagnostics.

The trap is that SFF is a property of the device, not of your installation. A transmitter with 95 % diagnostic coverage installed with its impulse line plugged has the diagnostic coverage of a brick. The standard knows this, which is why section 8.14 mentions the conditions that raise the requirement by one — dominant failure mode not to the safe state, dangerous failures undetected — and the ones that lower it.

What to do on Monday

Build the spreadsheet. Not because the answer will be right, but because building it forces you to write down the four numbers, and writing them down is where the arguments happen. Most disagreements about SIL are not disagreements about mathematics; they are disagreements about whether a thirty-year-old valve really has a forty-year MTBF.

Then sanity-check the output against the tables in section 8.14 and against the cookbook in section 8.15. If your model says a simplex system meets SIL 3, one of the two is wrong, and it is not the table.
