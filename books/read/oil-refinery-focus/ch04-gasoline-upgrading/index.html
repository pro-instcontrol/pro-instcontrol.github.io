Gasoline Upgrading: Reforming, Isomerization and Alkylation

A refinery does not make one gasoline. It makes components and then blends them; reformate, isomerate and alkylate each buy octane in a different way.

Sources used. Handbook of Petroleum Processing, ch. on gasoline components and catalytic reforming; Petroleum Refining: Technology and Economics, ch. on catalytic reforming, isomerization and alkylation; Handbook of Petroleum Refining Processes, UOP Platforming and Stratco sulfuric acid alkylation; Chemistry of Petrochemical Processes (Matar, 2nd ed.), ch. on aromatics

What catalytic reforming actually does

In catalytic reforming, naphtha contacts a platinum-containing catalyst at elevated temperature and hydrogen pressures of 345 to 3450 kPa (50 to 500 psig). Low-octane paraffin-rich naphtha becomes a high-octane aromatic-rich liquid, with hydrogen and light hydrocarbons as by-products. Full-range feed typically contains 6 to 12 carbon atoms; BTX reformers prefer mostly naphthenes and paraffins of 6 to 8 carbons.

The desired reactions are dehydrogenation of naphthenes to aromatics, and cyclization plus dehydrogenation of paraffins. Paraffin conversion is the hardest step. Acid-catalyzed reactions combined with the platinum dehydrogenation function drive hydroisomerization; high-octane aromatics, paraffin isomerization and cracking of low-octane material to gas all contribute to the final octane.

Regeneration strategy sets the operating window

The first platinum-based reforming process, UOP Platforming, came on stream in 1949. Early semiregenerative units were followed by bimetallic catalysts that allowed roughly 1380 to 2070 kPa (200 to 300 psig), 95 to 98 octane and typical cycle lengths of about one year. Cyclic reforming let each reactor be removed, regenerated and returned without shutting the unit, reaching octane near 100 at about 200 psig.

Continuous catalyst regeneration changed the picture again. UOP commercialized CCR Platforming in 1971: small amounts of catalyst are continuously withdrawn from the last reactor, regenerated, and returned to the first. CCR permits ultra-low pressure around 345 kPa (50 psig) with product octane as high as 108. More than 95% of new catalytic reformers are designed for continuous regeneration, and many older semiregenerative units have been revamped.

Isomerization and alkylate

Reforming is not the only way to raise octane. Isomerization rearranges normal paraffins into higher-octane branched molecules without sacrificing yield; yield is usually defined as liquid product with five or more carbons. This is especially valuable for the light naphtha fraction that is a poor reforming feed.

Alkylation combines light olefins, mainly C3 and C4, with isobutane over HF or sulfuric acid catalyst to produce alkylate: high octane, low vapor pressure and essentially sulfur-free. The Handbook of Petroleum Refining Processes treats both HF and Stratco H2SO4 alkylation as distinct licensed designs, so the acid choice is a plant-design decision, not merely a laboratory detail.

Octane is a blending property

The research octane number, RON, is measured at low engine speed and the motor octane number, MON, at high speed. In the United States the number posted at the pump is the arithmetic average of MON and RON. RONC means research octane number clear, with no octane-boosting additives such as lead. The scale is anchored by n-heptane at zero and 2,2,4-trimethylpentane, iso-octane, at 100.

Crucially, blending octane is not the same as the octane of the pure component: a hydrocarbon can behave differently when mixed with others. Reformate specifications for octane, vapor pressure and end point are therefore set to produce an optimum blending component, not a stand-alone fuel. That is why blending reappears as a control problem in Unit 6.
