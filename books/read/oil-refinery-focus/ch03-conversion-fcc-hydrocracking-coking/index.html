Conversion: FCC, Hydrocracking and Coking

Distillation separates what exists; conversion manufactures what the market wants. The three main routes differ in how they add hydrogen or reject carbon.

Sources used. Handbook of Petroleum Processing, ch. 6: Fluid catalytic cracking; Handbook of Petroleum Processing, ch. 7: Hydrocracking; Petroleum Refining: Technology and Economics, ch. 4-7: thermal and catalytic conversion; Handbook of Petroleum Refining Processes, KBR FCC and Isocracking process descriptions

FCC: the catalyst carries the heat

Catalytic cracking was first commercialized in 1936 by Eugene Houdry. Fluid catalytic cracking followed, with the first commercial unit starting at Baton Rouge in 1942. In a modern FCCU, hot regenerated catalyst contacts the oil near the base of the reactor; spent catalyst is stripped with steam before entering the regenerator so hydrocarbons do not burn in the regenerator and deactivate the catalyst.

Coke burned from the catalyst in the regenerator supplies the heat required by cracking. The handbook notes that a typical fuels refinery sends about a third of its crude to a standard gas-oil FCC unit. More than two million barrels per day of purpose-built resid crackers have been licensed, with another million barrels per day of FCC capacity processing some resid alongside normal gas oils.

FCC operating levers

Conversion is useful as a measure of severity: higher conversion needs higher catalyst temperature, and lowering product end point at constant conversion also needs higher severity. Conversion is normally controlled by catalyst temperature, while the catalyst-to-oil ratio and slide valves set circulation. For heavier feeds, coke make must be raised to restore the catalyst/oil ratio; dense-bed catalyst coolers become necessary at the heaviest feeds.

Zeolite chemistry explains the catalyst choice. Acidity and activity are associated with hydroxyl groups attached to aluminum atoms in the crystal structure, while the three-dimensional framework limits rapid coking. Deep Catalytic Cracking, commercialized in 1991, deliberately produces much more propylene than a normal FCCU by combining these features with higher-severity operation.

Hydrocracking: treating plus cracking

Hydrocracking uses a bifunctional catalyst. One function is acidic cracking; the other is hydrogenation supplied by metals from group VIA, molybdenum and tungsten, or group VIIIA, cobalt and nickel. Reactions divide into treating and cracking. The metals hydrogenate the feed, making it more reactive for cracking and heteroatom removal while reducing coking; dehydrogenation also forms the olefin intermediates that start cracking.

Operators monitor the weight average bed temperature (WABT). As catalyst deactivates, WABT is gradually raised to hold conversion; the rate of that rise is the deactivation rate and is a key performance indicator. Liquid hourly space velocity, LHSV, is the volumetric feed rate per hour divided by catalyst volume. Increasing fresh feed at constant catalyst volume raises LHSV and demands higher catalyst temperature. Recycle also matters: raising the combined feed ratio reduces the required catalyst temperature at constant fresh-feed conversion.

Resid routes: coking or hydroprocessing

The handbook is direct about feed selection: for high-metals crudes or crudes deficient in hydrogen, coking is the preferred bottoms route, followed by hydroprocessing of the coker gas oils or FCC products. Feed preparation, although costly, greatly improves overall yields where the crude warrants it.

Residue hydrotreating is essentially contaminant removal plus selective hydrogenation of olefins and aromatics. Some cracking occurs but is normally less than 20 vol% of fresh feed. The choice between coking and hydroprocessing is therefore a hydrogen and metals balance as much as a yield calculation.
