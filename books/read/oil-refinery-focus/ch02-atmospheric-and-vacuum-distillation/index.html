Atmospheric and Vacuum Crude Distillation

The crude column is a separator with a heat budget. Desalting protects it, the furnace feeds it, side strippers sharpen it, and vacuum distillation takes over where thermal cracking would begin.

Sources used. Handbook of Petroleum Processing, ch. 3: The atmospheric and vacuum crude distillation units; Handbook of Petroleum Processing, ch. 4: Fractionation equipment and tower design; Petroleum Refining: Technology and Economics, ch. 3: crude distillation

Desalting before the furnace

The handbook gives a practical threshold: when salt content exceeds 10 lb per 1000 bbl expressed as NaCl, the crude requires desalting to minimize fouling and corrosion from salt deposition on heat-transfer surfaces and from acids formed by decomposition. Water emulsified with the crude is also partially rejected, protecting catalysts in downstream catalytic units.

Electric fields of 12,000 to 35,000 volts promote droplet coalescence. Single-stage desalting typically achieves 90 to 95% efficiency; two-stage processes reach 99% or better. Heavier crudes have made efficient desalting more important, and some crudes sent to catalytic resid processing use three stages. Suspended sand and clay are removed as a useful secondary function.

Preheat, furnace and the atmospheric tower

The feed stays liquid through the preheat train, so its enthalpy curve needs only sensible heat. Furnace discharge temperature of 650 to 750°F (343 to 399°C) vaporizes all products withdrawn above the flash zone plus about 10 to 20% of the bottoms product. That overflash is not waste: it provides the internal reflux that keeps the wash section and side draws on specification.

For a typical Middle East crude, full-range naphtha runs from gas to 400°F, kerosene from 400 to 500°F and gas oil from 500 to 650°F. Straight-run naphtha may boil between 150 and 400°F (65 to 200°C). Side draws are steam stripped; the number of stripping trays is usually four with 24-inch tray spacing. The overhead condenser recovers the pentane-and-heavier fraction while lighter gas goes forward.

Why vacuum distillation is a different animal

Lower operating pressure produces a significant increase in the volume of vapor per barrel vaporized. Vacuum towers are therefore much larger in diameter than atmospheric towers; the handbook notes that columns up to 40 feet in diameter are not unusual. The EFV curve, section pressure and corresponding temperature determine how the feed flashes.

Typical vacuum gas oil boils at 750 to 1050°F and vacuum reduced crude at 1050°F and above. VGO is routed to catalytic cracking or hydrocracking; heavier vacuum resid goes to deasphalting or coking. Pushing the atmospheric cut point too far would crack the molecule thermally before the vacuum section ever sees it.

Keeping the tower inside its operating window

Most towers are designed for at least a 50% turndown ratio on the trays. Below that, performance falls off sharply, especially for sieve trays, almost certainly because liquid begins to weep from tray to tray. A short temporary reduction in throughput can be offset by increasing reflux; sustained low load needs a different operating strategy.

At the other extreme, excessive vapor load raises pressure drop until fractionation is no longer possible. Feed rate and heat input must then be reduced. Tower diameter is set by loading for maximum cross-sectional area and by the crude feed cut point together with the degree of overflash; the crude assay is therefore a mechanical design input, not only a process calculation.
