Support Systems, Quality Control and Advanced Control

The processing units get the attention, but the refinery works because of its support systems: control, blending, utilities, safety and the optimization layer above them all.

Sources used. Handbook of Petroleum Processing, ch. 13: Support systems common to most refineries; Handbook of Petroleum Processing, ch. on quality control and laboratory systems; Practical Advances in Petroleum Processing Vol. 2, model-predictive control chapter (Robinson and Cima); Petroleum Refining: Technology and Economics, ch. on product blending and linear programming

Control is part of plant design

The Handbook of Petroleum Processing states that proper operation depends as much on a properly designed control system as on correct equipment design and specification, and that safe operation depends largely on control design. Surge volume is the liquid volume between normal liquid level, NLL, and the high and low levels, HLL and LLL. The larger the surge range, the larger the vessel.

Selection of a level scheme depends on how many outlet streams leave the surge vessel and which streams cannot tolerate complete shut-off before available surge is exhausted. Two general rules follow: if the product outflow rate may vary, use level control with relatively small surge capacity; if downstream demand for steady flow dominates, provide a larger surge volume and a different control arrangement.

Blending: recipe, valves and analyzers

Blending combines two or more components into a desired product. Batch blending routes components separately into a single tank; in-line blending routes them through automatically operated flow valves to a finished-product tank. With modern computerized control, in-line blending is increasingly common for gasoline and lube oil, while middle distillates and residuum are still often blended in batches because fewer components are handled.

An in-line blender is essentially a multiple-stream controller with feedback. The controller is a computer holding the recipe; it starts pumps and drives component flow valves, and it receives data from on-line analyzers in the rundown line. Proprietary recipes are usually implemented with linear programming. For good flexibility, the ratio of component tankage to finished-product tankage should be about four or five to one. Process, instrument and computer engineers all contribute to the design.

Model-predictive control

Practical Advances in Petroleum Processing defines model-predictive control, MPC, as the use of process models derived from past behavior to predict future behavior, then controlling units dynamically at optimum steady-state targets. Applications may include inferred product properties from analyzers and process calculations. MPC controllers almost always handle multiple independent variables; the constrained, multivariable form is the predominant advanced-control application in refining.

Benefits come from operating close to constraints, tracking optimization targets and moving smoothly between sets of targets. The largest gains are reported for crude distillation units and gasoline blenders with high throughput, and for FCC and other conversion units. After instrumentation infrastructure is in place, projects on major units can be completed in two to four months, with typical payback of four to twelve months.

Optimization, safety and the envelope around them

Linear programming builds a mathematical model whose main expression is the objective function: maximize profit or minimize cost subject to constraints. The handbook notes considerable development in using linear programming on line to optimize unit operation, with submodels linked by linear relationships. Planning, blend recipes and MPC targets form one hierarchy rather than three unrelated tools.

Around that hierarchy sit the support systems: storage, product blending, road and rail loading, jetty facilities, waste disposal and effluent-water treating. Many refineries treat the flare as part of the safety system because relief valves, process vents and other vent systems discharge to it. Advanced control can push against operating constraints, but relief and shutdown paths remain independent of the control loop.
