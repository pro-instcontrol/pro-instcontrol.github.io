Crude Oil, Assays and Refinery Configuration

Distillation can only separate molecules that are already in the crude. The assay tells you what is there; the refinery configuration decides how far you are willing to rearrange it.

Sources used. Handbook of Petroleum Processing, ch. 1: An introduction to crude oil and its processing; Handbook of Petroleum Processing, ch. 2: Refinery products and configuration; Practical Advances in Petroleum Processing Vol. 1, pp. 30-50: crude evaluation; Petroleum Refining: Technology and Economics, ch. 1-2: products and refinery types

Reading the crude assay

The Handbook of Petroleum Processing treats the crude assay as the starting datum for every downstream calculation. API gravity expresses density relative to water at 60°F (15.6°C); specific gravity, sulfur, nitrogen, viscosity and carbon residue complete the picture. Carbon residue is determined by distilling to a coke residue in the absence of air and is reported as weight percent by the Ramsbottom (RCR) or Conradson (CCR) test. In most cases the lower the carbon residue, the more valuable the crude.

Assay data are not an academic exercise. The handbook notes that selecting a crude slate requires careful examination of the various crude assays and modeling of refinery operation to set the crude-oil mix and its operating parameters. A lighter, sweeter crude gives a very different product slate from a heavy, high-sulfur one, even before a single conversion unit is considered.

TBP, ASTM and the meaning of a cut point

A true boiling point (TBP) curve plots the boiling points of the near-pure components contained in the crude. The ASTM distillation curve is the routine measurement, but its separation is poorer: the initial boiling point (IBP) is higher than the corresponding TBP point and the final boiling point (FBP) is lower. Correlations are therefore needed to move between the two.

A cut point is the temperature on the whole-crude TBP curve that marks the upper and lower limits of a fraction. In refinery calculations the TBP curve is divided into mid-boiling or mid-volume pseudo-components, each assigned molecular weight and other properties. The handbook gives correlations that relate ASTM end point to TBP cut point so a product distillation curve can be predicted from its cut range alone.

From assay to product slate

The worked example in the handbook for a typical Middle East crude sets the residue cut point first, then reads each fraction as a percentage of whole crude from the TBP curve: naphtha from gas to 400°F is 30% vol, kerosene from 400 to 500°F is 10.5% vol, gas oil from 500 to 650°F is 14.5% vol, and residue above 650°F is 45.0% vol.

Those four lines already explain why a refinery needs more than a distillation tower. Nearly half the barrel arrives as residue, while market demand pulls the other way toward light transport fuels. Everything discussed in Units 3 and 4 exists to move carbon from the residue side to the fuel side.

The configuration ladder

Petroleum Refining: Technology and Economics lays out the ladder from a simple topping plant, which only distils crude, through hydroskimming, which adds catalytic reforming and hydrotreating, to conversion and deep-conversion refineries built around FCC, hydrocracking or coking. Product blending and sulfur recovery appear in almost every high-conversion layout, even when a simplified diagram omits them.

The choice is not a matter of prestige. It follows from the crude slate, product demand and hydrogen balance. Lube-oil processing and petrochemical integration appear only in a minority of sites; the handbook notes, for example, that aromatic extraction using a sulfolane-like process exists in only about 20 of the largest North American refineries.
