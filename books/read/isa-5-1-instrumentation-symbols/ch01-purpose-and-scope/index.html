Purpose and scope

ANSI/ISA-5.1-1984 (R1992) · ISA

Chapter 1 · 9 source pages · companion notes
Key terms. Consensus standard — a standard developed by agreement among interested parties rather than imposed by regulation. · Identification — the sequence of letters and/or digits that designates one instrument or loop. · Symbol — the graphic that depicts an instrument or function on a diagram. · Loop — two or more instruments or functions passing signals between them to measure and/or control a variable.

ANSI/ISA-5.1 is the standard that gives process instrumentation its common language. This chapter sets out its purpose, its scope, and how the document positions itself: a tool for communication, not a mandatory regulation.

About this standard The standard and its background

ANSI/ISA-5.1-1984 (R1992) — Instrumentation Symbols and Identification was reaffirmed on 13 July 1992; it was formerly designated ANSI/ISA-S5.1-1984 (R1992). It was prepared by ISA Committee SP5.1, chaired by R. Mulley. The preface, footnotes and appendices are included for information only and are not part of the standard.

ISA prepared the standard in pursuit of uniformity in instrumentation. Communication presupposes a common language — or is at least greatly helped by one — and the standard supplies its foundation. Integrated into a system, its symbols and designations form a concise, dedicated language that communicates concepts, facts, intent, instructions and knowledge about measurement and control systems.

A consensus standard. This is a consensus standard, not a mandatory one. Its strength is broad, interdisciplinary applicability; its weakness is that it is often not specific enough for the special requirements of a particular interest group. The symbols and designations serve as conceptualising aids, design tools, teaching devices and a concise means of communication on technical, engineering, procurement, construction and maintenance documents.

1 · Purpose Purpose of the standard

The purpose of the standard is to establish a uniform means of designating instruments and instrumentation systems used for measurement and control. To that end it presents a designation system comprising two parts: symbols and an identification code.

This revision adds symbols, identification and definitions for concepts not previously described — notably shared display/control, distributed control and programmable control. Definitions were broadened because, in newer systems, equivalent functions are frequently no longer tied to a uniquely identifiable instrument yet still have to be conceptualised and named. The SAMA method of functional diagramming was adopted for function blocks and function designators; new binary line symbols and the first-letter Y (an initiating variable classed as an event, presence or state) were introduced for the batch-processing industries.

2 · Scope Scope and limits

2.1 · General Different user needs

The procedural needs of different users differ. The standard recognises those needs, where consistent with its objectives, by providing alternative symbolism methods, together with examples of adding information or simplifying the symbolism as desired.

Process-equipment symbols are not part of this standard; they appear only to illustrate applications of instrumentation symbols.

2.2 · Application to industries Suitable industries

The standard is suitable for the chemical, petroleum, power-generation, air-conditioning and metal-refining industries, and numerous other process industries. Fields such as astronomy, navigation and medicine use highly specialised instruments quite unlike conventional industrial process instruments. No specific effort was made to meet their requirements, but the standard is expected to be flexible enough for many of their needs.

2.3 · Application to work activities Which documents use it

The standard applies whenever any reference to an instrument or control-system function is required for symbolisation and identification. Typical uses:

  • Design sketches; teaching examples.
  • Technical papers, literature and discussions.
  • Instrumentation system diagrams, loop diagrams, logic diagrams.
  • Functional descriptions.
  • Flow diagrams: process, mechanical, engineering, systems, piping and instrumentation.
  • Construction drawings; specifications, purchase orders, manifests and other lists.
  • Identification (tagging) of instruments and control functions.
  • Installation, operating and maintenance instructions, drawings and records.

The intent is that anyone with a reasonable amount of process knowledge who reviews a document depicting process measurement and control can understand how the process is measured and controlled — without the detailed knowledge of an instrumentation specialist.

2.4–2.6 · Extent of detail How far identification goes

  • 2.4 · Applicable to all classes of process measurement and control instrumentation — not only discrete instruments but also the analogous functions of systems termed shared display, shared control, distributed control and computer control.
  • 2.5 · The standard identifies and symbolises the key functions of an instrument. Finer detail belongs in a specification, data sheet or other document intended for those who need it.
  • 2.6 · The standard covers the identification of an instrument and of all other instruments or control functions associated with it in a loop. The user is free to add identification by serial number, unit, area, plant or other means.
