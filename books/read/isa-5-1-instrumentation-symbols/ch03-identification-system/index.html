Outline of the identification system

ANSI/ISA-5.1-1984 (R1992) · §4

Chapter 3 · 4 source pages · 1 figures · companion notes
Key terms. Tag number — the alphanumeric code identifying one instrument. · Functional identification — the letter group, one first-letter plus succeeding-letters. · Loop identification — a first-letter plus a loop number. · Modifying letters — D (differential), F (ratio), Q (integrate), K (rate of change), H/L/M (high/low/middle).

Clause 4 is the backbone of the whole standard: it explains how a tag number is assembled, how identification letters are chosen, how loops are numbered and how symbols are used on drawings.

4.1 · General The tag number and its parts

Each instrument or function to be identified is designated by an alphanumeric code or tag number as shown in Figure 1. The loop-identification part of the tag number is generally common to all instruments or functions of the loop. A prefix or suffix may be added to complete the identification.

TIC 103 parses as: T = measured variable (temperature), IC = indicating and control, 103 = loop number. In expanded form, 10-PAH-5A has prefix 10 (area/type code), functional identification PAH (high pressure alarm), loop number 5 and optional suffix A. Hyphens are optional separators.

  • 4.1.2 · The loop number may include coded information such as a plant-area designation. Specific number series may also be set aside for special functions — for instance 900–999 for loops whose primary function is safety-related.
  • 4.1.3 · Each instrument may be represented on diagrams by a symbol, and the symbol may be accompanied by a tag number.

4.2 · Functional identification Rules for choosing letters

Functional identification consists of letters from Table 1: exactly one first-letter designating the measured or initiating variable, plus one or more succeeding-letters identifying the functions performed (4.2.1). The foundation rule is that identification follows function, not construction (4.2.2).

CaseCorrect designationWhy
A differential-pressure recorder used for flow measurementFRIts function is flow measurement, whatever its construction.
A pressure indicator on the output of a pneumatic level transmitterLIThe measured variable is level, not the signal pressure.
A pressure-actuated switch on that same transmitter outputLSThe first-letter still follows the level variable.
A valve varying flow on the command of a level controllerLV, not FVThe first-letter follows the measured variable (level), not the manipulated one (flow).

Four classic examples from clauses 4.2.2–4.2.3 of identification by function.

4.2.4 · Modifying letters. Succeeding-letters designate readout or passive functions and/or output functions. A modifying-letter may be added, and may modify either a first-letter or succeeding-letters. Thus TDAL contains two modifiers: D turns the measured variable T into a new variable, «differential temperature», while L restricts the readout function A (alarm) to a low alarm only.

4.2.5 · Letter order. The sequence starts with a first-letter from Table 1; readout/passive letters follow in any order, then output letters in any sequence — except that output letter C (control) precedes output letter V (valve), e.g. PCV, a self-actuated control valve. Modifying letters are always placed immediately after the letters they modify.

  • 4.2.6 · A multiple-function device may be drawn with as many bubbles as it has measured variables, outputs and functions. A temperature controller with a switch may be two tangent bubbles TIC-3 and TSH-3, written jointly as TIC/TSH-3.
  • 4.2.7 · Keep the number of functional letters to a minimum: no more than four letters in one group. Use subgroups, or omit I (indicate) when an instrument both indicates and records the same variable.
  • 4.2.8 · All letters of the functional identification are uppercase.

4.3 · Loop identification Parallel and serial numbering

Loop identification consists of a first-letter and a number. Every instrument in a loop carries the same loop number — and, with parallel numbering, the same first-letter. Each loop has a unique identification. An instrument common to two or more loops carries the identification of the loop considered predominant (4.3.1).

SchemeExampleCharacteristic
ParallelTIC-100, FRC-100, LIC-100, AI-100A new numerical sequence starts for each first-letter.
SerialTIC-100, FRC-101, LIC-102, AI-103One sequence for the project regardless of the first-letter.

4.3.2 · Numbering may begin at 1 or any convenient number such as 001, 301 or 1201; coded information is allowed but simplicity is recommended.

  1. 4.3.3 · Suffixes. When a loop contains more than one instrument with the same functional identification, append a suffix: FV-2A, FV-2B, FV-2C or TE-25-1, TE-25-2. Use uppercase letters; for a multipoint temperature recorder, number primary elements after the point identification; subdivisions may alternate letters and numbers.
  2. 4.3.4 · Multiple functions. A device performing two or more functions may be designated by all of them: flow recorder FR-2 with a pressure pen PR-4 becomes FR-2/PR-4; a two-pen pressure recorder is PR-7/8; a common annunciator window for high and low temperature alarms is TAHL-21. The slash is unnecessary when no distinct separate devices exist.
  3. 4.3.5 · Accessories. Purge meters, air sets and seal pots not shown on the diagram are still tagged by function, using the same loop identification as the instrument they serve: an orifice flange union with FE-7 is FX-7 (or «FE-7 FLANGES»); a thermowell with TI-9 is TW-9. The rule need not apply to bulk-purchased items.

4.4 · Using symbols on drawings Twelve practical rules

The standard's examples illustrate typical usage; they do not restrict the applications or designations, and the order in which alternative symbols appear implies no preference (4.4.1).

  • 4.4.2 · A bubble may tag a distinctive symbol such as a control valve; the connecting line is then drawn close to, but not touching, the symbol. Elsewhere the bubble itself represents the instrument.
  • 4.4.3 · A distinctive symbol whose relationship to the loop is obvious need not be individually tagged (an orifice flange, a control valve). Showing a primary element symbol connected to another instrument is optional.
  • 4.4.4 · A brief notation may be added adjacent to a symbol or line: 3–9 psig and 9–15 psig beside two split-range valves, together with failure-mode symbols, make the intent completely clear.
  • 4.4.5–4.4.6 · Bubble sizes are recommendations only, depending on drawing reduction and tag length. Symbols may be drawn in any orientation and signal lines may enter or leave at any angle; but Table 3 function-block designators and tag numbers are always horizontal. Add arrowheads where information flow needs clarifying.
  • 4.4.7–4.4.8 · Electrical, pneumatic or other power supplies are not shown unless essential to understanding. In general one signal line suffices between two instruments even if physically connected by several.
  • 4.4.9 · The connection sequence on a diagram should reflect functional logic / information flow, not necessarily the wiring order. Analog voltage loops need parallel wiring and current loops series wiring, yet both should be drawn as if all wiring were parallel so the functional relationships stay clear.
  • 4.4.10 · The degree of detail is entirely at the user's discretion. Sketches and papers are usually highly simplified; process flow diagrams are less detailed than engineering flow diagrams, which may show all in-line components. What matters is consistency within each application. The terms simplified / conceptual / detailed in clause 6.12 merely sample typical usage.
  • 4.4.11 · Engineering flow diagrams commonly omit interlock hardware that a working system actually needs (auxiliary relays, current transformers…), especially for electrical interlocks; such detail belongs in other documents.
  • 4.4.12 · The distinction between shared display/shared control and computer functions is sometimes blurred; the choice of symbol must rely on manufacturers' definitions, industry usage and personal judgement.
