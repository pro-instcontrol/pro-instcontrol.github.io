Overview: which method for which stage Tổng quan: phương pháp nào cho giai đoạn nào

Several safety analysis methods have been developed already. Some of them are internationally known and proved, some have been used and developed more or less inside companies. Information requirements of the methods are different, also the results produced vary. Thus different safety methods are suitable for different stages of process development, design and operation (see Figure 3 in Chapter 3).

Process industry has used the Dow Fire and Explosion Hazard Index (DOW, 1987) and the Mond Index (ICI, 1985) for many years. These indices deal with fire and explosion hazard rating of process plants; they are rapid hazard-assessment methods for use on chemical plant, during process and plant development, and in the design of plant layout. They are best suited to later design stages when process equipment, chemical substances and process conditions are known.

Another widely used method is Hazop (Kletz, 1992), but conventional Hazop was developed to identify probable process disturbances when complete process and instrumentation diagrams are available, so it is not very applicable to conceptual process design. Kletz has also mentioned a Hazop of a flowsheet usable in preliminary process design, but it is not widely used. A more usable method in preliminary process design is PIIS (Edwards and Lawrence, 1993), developed to select safe process routes.

Five other preliminary methods Năm phương pháp phân tích sơ bộ khác

Other possible preliminary safety analysis methods are concept safety review (CSR), critical examination of system safety (CE), concept hazard analysis (CHA), preliminary consequence analysis (PCA) and preliminary hazard analysis (PHA) (Wells et al., 1993). These methods are meant to be carried out from the time of the concept safety review until such time as reasonably firm process flow diagrams or early P&IDs are available.

The four methods discussed here in more detail are the Dow Fire and Explosion Hazard Index, the Mond Index, Hazop and PIIS; their elements are also presented in Table 1 at the end of this chapter.

4.1 Dow Fire and Explosion Hazard Index 4.1 Chỉ số cháy và nổ Dow

The purpose of the Dow Fire and Explosion Hazard Index (Dow, 1987) is to: 1) quantify the expected damage of potential fire and explosion incidents in realistic terms, 2) identify equipment that would be likely to contribute to the creation or escalation of an incident and 3) communicate the fire and explosion risk potential to management. The Dow Index is the product of the Unit Hazard Factor and the Material Factor (Table 1).

The material factor MF for the process unit is taken of the most hazardous substance present, which leads to the analysis of the worst case that could actually occur. MF denotes the intensity of energy release from the most hazardous material or mixture of materials present in significant quantity in the process; it is obtained from the flammability and reactivity of the substances. The process is divided into units and the material factor is calculated for each unit separately. Dow (1987) has listed a number of chemical compounds and materials with their MFs.

The Unit Hazard Factor for a process unit is the product of general and special process hazards. Penalties of general process hazards deal with different exothermic chemical reactions, endothermic processes, material handling and transfer, enclosed or indoor process units, access to the area, and drainage and spill control. Special process hazards contain the factors for toxic materials, sub-atmospheric pressure, operation in or near flammable range, dust explosion, relief pressure, low temperature, quantity of flammable and unstable materials, corrosion and erosion, leakage in the cases of joints and packing, use of fired heaters, hot oil systems and rotating equipment.

The values of the factors are determined on the basis of Dow's Fire & Explosion Hazard Index Classification Guide (Dow, 1987). The Guide includes rules and tables which cover well the most chemical substances and unit operations.

4.2 Mond Index 4.2 Chỉ số Mond

The Mond Index (ICI, 1985) has been developed from the 1973 version of the Dow F&E Index. The principal modifications to the Dow method include (Lees, 1996): 1) a wider range of processes and storage installations can be studied, 2) it covers processing of chemicals having explosive properties, 3) improved hazard consideration for hydrogen, 4) additional special process hazards, and 5) toxicity is included into the assessment.

In the Mond Index the plant is divided into individual units on the basis of the feasibility of creating separating barriers; one of the factors taken into account is therefore plant layout. The potential hazard is expressed in terms of the initial value of a set of indices for fire, explosion and toxicity. A hazard factor review is then carried out to see if design changes reduce the hazard, and intermediate values of the indices are determined. Offsetting factors for preventative and protective features are applied and the final values of the indices, or offset indices, are calculated. The elements of the Mond method are listed in Table 1.

4.3 Hazard and Operability Analysis (Hazop) 4.3 Phân tích mối nguy và khả năng vận hành (Hazop)

Hazard and Operability Analysis (Hazop) (Kletz, 1992) is one of the most used safety analysis methods in the process industry and one of the simplest approaches to hazard identification. Hazop involves a vessel to vessel and a pipe to pipe review of a plant: for each vessel and pipe the possible disturbances and their potential consequences are identified.

Hazop is based on guide words such as no, more, less, reverse, other than, which should be asked for every pipe and vessel (Table 1). The intention of the guide words is to stimulate the imagination, and the method relies very much on the expertise of the persons performing the analysis. The idea behind the questions is that any disturbance in a chemical plant can be described in terms of physical state variables. Hazop can be used in different stages of process design but in restricted mode; a complete Hazop study requires final process plannings with flow sheets and P&IDs.

Kletz (1991) pointed out an important difference between a conventional Hazop of a line diagram (P&ID) and a Hazop of a flowsheet (the process concept): in a conventional Hazop deviations from design conditions are assumed to be undesirable and ways of preventing them are looked for, whereas in a flowsheet Hazop deviations are generated but are actually looked for to find new process alternatives. Although many detailed accounts of conventional Hazops have been published, little or nothing has appeared concerning the detailed results of a flowsheet Hazop (compare Figure 3 in Chapter 3, which considers Hazop as rarely used in conceptual design). Still there is a growing interest in flowsheet Hazop thanks to the ability to link a computerized Hazop to computer aided design systems, which allows a preliminary Hazop to be done during design.

Even if the conventional Hazop is a powerful technique for identifying hazards and operating problems, it comes too late for major changes to be made. In the forthcoming Hazop standard (IEC 61882, 1999) Hazop studies are recommended to be carried out throughout the life cycle of a system — but for the concept and definition phase other basic methods are suggested (see Figure 3).

4.4 Prototype Index of Inherent Safety (PIIS) 4.4 Chỉ số nguyên mẫu an toàn nội tại (PIIS)

Edwards and Lawrence (1993) developed a Prototype Index of Inherent Safety (PIIS) for process design. The inherent safety index is intended for analysing the choice of process route — the raw materials used and the sequence of the reaction steps. This method is very reaction oriented and does not consider properly the other parts of the process even though they usually represent the majority of equipment.

The PIIS is calculated as a total score, the sum of a chemical score and a process score (Table 1). The chemical score consists of inventory, flammability, explosiveness and toxicity; the process score includes temperature, pressure and yield. Some of the scores are based on similar tables in the Dow and Mond Indices; others have been constructed by dividing the domain of values of a parameter into ranges and assigning a score to each range. They are supposed to be modified in the future.

It has been argued that an overall inherent safety index such as the PIIS incorporates some kind of built-in judgement of the relative importance of the various types of hazards. The user has to defer to the judgement of the developer of the index or modify it to incorporate his own judgement — in the latter case the results are no longer comparable with other users (Hendershot, 1997). The PIIS may also be used as such or the factors may be weighted by the user. Hendershot (1997) prefers a system where contributory factors are evaluated by known indices such as the Dow F&E Index and the alternatives are compared e.g. by the Kepner-Tregoe method (Kepner and Tregoe, 1981). We should keep in mind, however, that even the Dow F&E Index includes built-in judgement on the importance of terms.

The PIIS has some clear advantages over some other numerical indices in early design stages, because it can be used when most of the detailed process information is still lacking.
