Introduction

Anna-Mari Heikkilä · VTT Publications 384 (1999)

Chapter 1 · 3 source pages · 1 figures · companion notes
Key terms. inherent safety — avoiding hazards by the intrinsic properties of the process · external (add-on) safety — controlling hazards with protective systems · hazard / risk · SHE — safety, health, environment · ISI — Inherent Safety Index · ISBL / OSBL — inside / offsite battery limits · conceptual process design.

The aim of process design is to create a process which is profitable, economic, safe, environmentally benign and user friendly. This is achieved by the optimization of process alternatives according to economic and functional criteria. It is required that the safety of a process plant fulfills a certain required level — because of general legal requirements, company image, and also for economic reasons, since an unsafe plant cannot be profitable due to losses of production and capital.

Inherent safety and external safety An toàn nội tại và an toàn bên ngoài

The safety of a chemical process can be achieved through internal (inherent) and external means. Inherent safety (Kletz, 1984) is related to the intrinsic properties of the process — e.g. the use of safer chemicals and operations. The essence of inherent safety is to avoid and remove hazards rather than to control them by added-on protective systems, which is the principle of external safety. The largest payoffs are achieved by verifying that inherent safety has been considered early and often in the process and engineering design (Lutz, 1997).

The concept of the inherently safer plant has been with us for many years. But in spite of its clear potential benefits related to safety, health and the environment (SHE), as well as costs, there have been few applications in chemical plant design. As Kletz (1996) has written, there are hurdles to overcome: inherently safer design requires a basic change in approach. Instead of assuming that we can keep large quantities of hazardous materials under control, we have to try and remove them. Changes in belief and the corresponding actions do not come easily.

The add-on habit Thói quen dựa vào hệ thống bổ sung

The traditional attitude in plant design is to rely much on added-on safety systems. Reactions from industry can be expressed by two questions (Gowland, 1996): “How do I know if my process is designed according to inherently safe principles?” and “Can the influence of a process change on the inherent safety of a plant be measured?”. Plants are designed on a tight time schedule using standards and so-called sound engineering practice. Lutz (1997) realized that inherent safety alternatives have become a requirement in companies that understand that inherently safer plants have lower lifetime costs and therefore are more profitable.

The chemical process industry in general overlooks the simplicity of designing to eliminate the hazard at the earliest opportunity. The result is controls being engineered near the end-point of the design and capitalization process: with this approach add-on systems become the only opportunities for process safety and pollution control. Systems added late in design require continual staffing and maintenance throughout the life of the plant, greatly adding to lifetime costs as well as repetitive training and documentation upkeep.

Hazards are inextricably linked Các mối nguy liên kết chặt với nhau

There is no general answer to the question of which process is inherently safer. One problem is how to minimize simultaneously the risk associated with all of the process hazards: in the real world the various hazards are not independent of each other but are inextricably linked together (Hendershot, 1995). A process modification which reduces one hazard will always have some impact, positive or negative, on the risk resulting from another hazard. The advantages and disadvantages of each option must be compared for a particular case and the choice made based on the specific details of the process and materials.

Hendershot (1995) points to current concerns about the adverse environmental effects of chlorofluorocarbons (CFCs). It is easy to forget that these materials were originally introduced as inherently safer replacements for the more hazardous refrigerants then in use. While the alternative materials are inherently safer with respect to long-term environmental damage, they are often more hazardous with respect to flammability and acute toxicity.

Accident statistics: a rising trend Thống kê tai nạn: xu hướng tăng

One way of assessing the efficiency of existing safety policies is to look at accident statistics in industry. They show (Anon., 1997) that in the USA about 23,000 accidents involving toxic chemicals took place in the period 1993–1995 — an average of 7,700 accidents per year — resulting in about 60 deaths and the evacuation of 41,000 people. In 1988–1992 the yearly average was 6,900 accidents: the trend is increasing. It seems that the traditional approach of reducing risks is not enough and new types of actions are needed. This calls for more preventative strategies such as inherent-safety plant design.

Aim of the thesis Mục tiêu của luận văn

To implement inherent safety in practice, a method to estimate the inherent safety of different design alternatives is needed. Methods such as the Dow and Mond Indices are commonly used in the chemical industry, but their point is mostly fire and explosion hazards, and they often need detailed information on the process while at the beginning of design only scarce knowledge is available. This study presents an Inherent Safety Index to solve the safety evaluation problems of the conceptual engineering phase — when the major decisions on the chemical process are made — built on the knowledge available at the preliminary design stage.

Per the Abstract (pp. 3–4), the total index is divided into the Chemical Inherent Safety Index (reaction heats, flammability, explosiveness, toxicity, corrosiveness, chemical interaction) and the Process Inherent Safety Index (inventory, process temperature, pressure, equipment safety, process structure). The equipment safety subindex was developed from accident statistics and layout data separately for ISBL and OSBL areas. The process structure subindex describes safety from the system engineering point of view, evaluated by case-based reasoning on a database of good and bad design cases — experience-based information on recommended process configurations and accident data — allowing the reuse of existing design experience, which is often neglected. Finally the index is used as a fitness function in the optimization of the process structure by an algorithm combining a genetic algorithm and case-based reasoning, with two case studies on the synthesis of inherently safer processes.

About the source Về nguồn tài liệu

The work was carried out at the Helsinki University of Technology, Laboratory of Chemical Engineering and Plant Design, from April 1993 to July 1998 (Preface, p. 5); the author is Anna-Mari Heikkilä, Tampere, March 1999. Published as VTT Publications 384 of the Technical Research Centre of Finland, 129 pages, one PDF file; keywords: inherent safety, process plant design, safety analysis, case-based reasoning, genetic algorithm.

Central idea: inherent safety removes hazards through the choice of chemicals, inventory and process structure at the earliest design stage, instead of controlling them with protective systems added late. This chapter sets the problem and the aim of the Inherent Safety Index; later chapters review existing evaluation methods, build the index and its subindices, and apply them to a case study and to computerized process synthesis.
