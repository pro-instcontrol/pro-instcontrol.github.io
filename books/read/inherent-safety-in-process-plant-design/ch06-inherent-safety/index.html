The best way of dealing with a hazard is to remove it completely. The provision of means to control the hazard is very much the second solution. As Lees (1996) has said, the aim should be to design the process and plant so that they are inherently safer.

"Inherent" is defined by the American College Dictionary as "existing in something as a permanent and inseparable element, quality or attribute". Thus an inherently safer chemical process is safer because of its essential characteristics — those which belong to the process by its very nature. An inherently safer design is one that avoids hazards instead of controlling them, particularly by removing or reducing the amount of hazardous material in the plant or the number of hazardous operations.

Inherent safety was first widely expressed in the late 1970s by Trevor Kletz. The basic principles are common sense: avoid the use of hazardous materials, minimise the inventories of hazardous materials and aim for simpler processes with more benign and moderate process alternatives (Kletz, 1984).

While the basic principle of inherently safer design is generally accepted, it is not always easy to put into practice. Inherently safer design has been advocated since the explosion at Flixborough in 1974. Progress has been real, but nevertheless the concept has not been adopted nearly as rapidly as quantitative risk assessment, which was introduced into the chemical industry only a few years earlier (Kletz, 1996).

It has been commented that methods developed to date have largely been for evaluating the safety of some proposed design. In the future, safety experts expect to see a greater emphasis on the use of knowledge to synthesize a safe plant design in the first place. In their opinion the value of inherent safety has been recognised, but there is still room for better awareness and practice. The concern expressed by inherent safety experts is that best practice is not being adopted quickly enough by the potential practitioners (Preston, 1998).

Mansfield (1994) has pointed out that in industry there is an increasing need to address — and sometimes balance — the overall lifecycle health, safety and environmental aspects of performance. This shows that in real life we should talk about Inherent SHE (Safety, Environment and Health) instead of plain inherent safety. Especially in the management of safety, health and environmental protection, integration of legislation (e.g. SEVESO II, OSHA Process Safety Management) and systems such as EMAS, ISO 14000 and Responsible Care is needed (Turney, 1998). Otherwise it is possible to create conflict situations: for example, environmentally and economically important energy savings may lead to inherently less safe process solutions (see Figure 9 in the following chapter).

6.1 The Principles of Inherent Safety 6.1 Nguyên tắc của an toàn nội tại

Inherent safety is the pursuit of designing hazards out of a process, as opposed to using engineering or procedural controls to mitigate risk. Inherent safety therefore strives to avoid and remove hazards rather than to control them by added-on systems. It is best considered in the initial stages of design, when the choice of process route and concept is made.

Kletz (1984, 1991) has given the Basic Principles of Inherent Safety as follows:

Intensification Cường tập hóa

"What you don't have, can't leak." Small inventories of hazardous materials reduce the consequences of leaks. Inventories can often be reduced in almost all unit operations as well as storage. This also brings reductions in cost, since less material needs smaller vessels, structures and foundations.

Substitution Thay thế

If intensification is not possible, an alternative is substitution. It may be possible to replace flammable refrigerants and heat transfer media with non-flammable ones, hazardous products with safer ones, and processes that use hazardous raw materials or intermediates with processes that do not. Using a safer material in place of a hazardous one decreases the need for added-on protective equipment and thus decreases plant cost and complexity.

Attenuation Suy yếu

If intensification and substitution are not possible or practicable, an alternative is attenuation: carrying out a hazardous reaction under less hazardous conditions, or storing or transporting a hazardous material in a less hazardous form. Attenuation is sometimes the reverse of intensification, because less extreme reaction conditions may lead to a longer residence time.

Limitation of Effects Giới hạn hệ quả

If it is not possible to make plants safer by intensification, substitution or attenuation, the effects of a failure should be limited. For instance, equipment is designed so that it can leak only at a low rate that is easy to stop or control, and gaskets are chosen to minimise leak rates. Limitation of effects should be achieved by equipment design or a change in reaction conditions rather than by adding on protective equipment.

Simplification Đơn giản hóa

Simpler plants are inherently safer than complex plants, because they provide fewer opportunities for error and contain less equipment that can go wrong. Simpler plants are usually also cheaper and more user friendly.

Change Early Thay đổi sớm

Change Early means identification of hazards as early as possible in the process design. The payback for early hazard identification can make or break the capital budget of a new process. This can be achieved by dedicated safety evaluation methodologies designed for preliminary process design purposes.

Avoiding Knock-On Effects Tránh hiệu ứng dây chuyền

Safer plants are designed so that incidents which do occur do not produce knock-on or domino effects. For example, safer plants are provided with fire breaks between sections to restrict the spread of fire, or — where flammable materials are handled — the plant is built out-of-doors so that leaks can be dispersed by natural ventilation.

Making Status Clear Làm rõ trạng thái

Equipment should be chosen so that it can easily be seen whether it has been installed correctly or whether it is in the open or shut position. This refers to the ergonomics of the plant. A clear explanation of the chemistry involved in the process also helps operating personnel identify possible hazards.

Making Incorrect Assembly Impossible Không thể lắp sai

Safe plants are designed so that incorrect assembly is difficult or impossible. Assembled components must meet their design requirements: a loss of containment may result from using, for example, the wrong type of gaskets.

Tolerance Chịu lỗi

Equipment should tolerate maloperation, poor installation or maintenance without failure. Expansion loops in pipework, for example, are more tolerant of poor installation than bellows. Construction materials should be resistant to corrosion and to the physical conditions; for most applications metal is safer than glass or plastic.

Ease of Control Dễ điều khiển

A process should be controlled by the use of physical principles rather than added-on control equipment — that is, the dynamics of the process should be favourable. If a process is difficult to control, one should look for ways of changing the process or the principles of control before investing in a complex control system.

Administrative Controls / Procedures Kiểm soát hành chính / quy trình

Human error is the most frequent cause of loss of containment. Training and certification of personnel on critical procedures are permanent considerations. Other inherent safety principles — ease of control, making status clear, tolerance and making incorrect assembly impossible — also come into play here.

An inherently safe design should contain the principles mentioned above, and they should cover the whole design process. In the early stages of process design these principles help to choose the safest materials, process conditions and even process technology. The present difficulty is the lack of routines to implement these inherent safety principles into reality.

6.2 Inherent Safety in Preliminary Process Design 6.2 An toàn nội tại trong thiết kế sơ bộ quá trình

The possibility of affecting the inherent safety of a process decreases as the design proceeds and more and more engineering and financial decisions have been made (Figure 4). It is much easier to affect the process configuration and inherent safety in the conceptual design phase than in the later phases. For instance, the process route selection is made in conceptual design, and it is many times difficult and expensive to change the route later. Time and money are also saved when fewer expensive safety modifications are needed and fewer added-on safety devices are included in the final solution.

In the early design phases the available information is limited to products, by-products and raw materials, capacity, main process equipment and a rough range of process conditions such as temperature and pressure. Of the steps of Suokas and Kakko (1993) in Figure 2, only hazard identification can partly be done in preliminary process design. In the early phases of a plant design, however, changes for safety are the most profitable: nothing has been built or ordered yet, so no expensive modifications are needed.

6.3 Evaluation of Inherent Safety 6.3 Đánh giá an toàn nội tại

As Hendershot (1995) has pointed out, most process options will be inherently safer with respect to one type of hazard, but may be less safe from a different viewpoint. In some cases the overall balance is readily apparent and it is easy to reach general agreement on which option offers the safest overall balance. In other cases the balance is less apparent, and more sophisticated tools are needed — qualitative ranking schemes, quantitative risk analysis and formal decision-making tools.

Several authors have put forward ideas for methods to be used in inherently safer design. Tools range from systematic open methods such as What-If analysis (CCPS, 1992) and critical examination (Wells et al., 1993), through to detailed checklists (CCPS, 1992; Hendershot, 1991; Englund, 1994; Lutz, 1994). Hendershot (1994) has proposed decision analysis techniques to help address the economic, engineering and SHE factors that arise when optimising route selection and plant design; similar approaches are being considered or used by other leading companies.

Indices such as the Dow Fire and Explosion Hazard Index and the Mond Index have been suggested to measure the degree of inherent SHE of a process. Rushton et al. (1994) pointed out that these indices can be used for the assessment of existing plants or at the detailed design stages: they require detailed plant specifications such as the plot plan, equipment sizes, material inventories and flows. Checklists, interaction matrices, Hazop and other hazard identification tools are also usable for the evaluation, because all hazards must be identified and their potential consequences understood. Hazop, for example, can be used at different stages of process design but in a restricted mode: a complete Hazop study requires final process plans with flow sheets and P&IDs.

The P.I.I.S. developed by Edwards and Lawrence (1993) is intended for estimating the inherent safety of reaction hazards at the conceptual design stage — analysing the choice of process route, i.e. the raw materials and the sequence of the reaction steps (see Chapter 4).

The INSIDE project (INSIDE, 1997) developed a toolkit called InSPIRE — a hyperbook providing chemists and engineers with the tools and methods to systematically identify, evaluate, optimise and select inherently SHE chemical processes and designs. Safety, health and environmental hazards are treated in an integrated way so that conflicts and synergies between these aspects are recognised and effectively managed. The toolkit especially addresses the key early stages of a project, where almost all of the main decisions that determine the SHE performance of the plant are taken. The InSPIRE hyperbook is no longer readily available, and at the end of the project many of the tools merited further development or validation.

The chapter's conclusion: there seem to be plenty of evaluation methods for inherent safety, but they are not directly suitable safety analysis tools for use with novel design systems in preliminary process design. Most existing methods need detailed process information and are not directly applicable in the early design stages; on the other hand, not all methods suit computerised use with optimisation and simulation tools. This gap leads to the Inherent Safety Index presented from Chapter 8 onwards.
