14. Application of Inherent Safety Index for Computerized Process Synthesis 14. Ứng dụng chỉ số an toàn nội tại trong tổng hợp quá trình

Classically, synthesis of chemical processes is based only on economic and engineering aspects. There is, however, an increasing need to evaluate the safety of processes in the early stage of process design, because of government regulations and for practical engineering reasons. It is much easier to affect the process configuration and inherent safety in the conceptual design phase than in the later phases. Consequently, safety aspects should be included in computerized process synthesis. The presented ISI can be used as a tool to evaluate synthesized alternatives or as an objective function in synthesis. The existing safety estimation methods (e.g. Dow F&E Index, computerized Hazop) are mostly intended for the more detailed engineering phases or for existing plants, and are not directly applicable in their full mode in the conceptual phase since they require detailed information on process equipment; other methods such as checklists are not easily changed into function form to be integrated into other design tools.

Process synthesis is a task of formulating the process configuration for a purpose by defining which operations or equipment are used and how they are connected together. There are two basic approaches: 1) classical process synthesis, analysis and evaluation, and 2) optimization of the process structure using a suitable objective function.

14.1 Classical Process Synthesis 14.1 Tổng hợp quá trình cổ điển

Classical process synthesis consists of the synthesis of the alternatives, their analysis and final evaluation. Hurme and Järveläinen (1995) presented a combined process synthesis and simulation system consisting of an interactive rule-based system used for generating process alternatives (Figure 10); the alternatives are simulated, costed and evaluated through profitability analysis. The system concept combines process synthesis, simulation and costing with uncertainty estimation.

Heikkilä et al. (1996) expanded the work of Hurme and Järveläinen (1995) with environmental and safety aspects (Figure 11). The alternatives are simulated to determine the material and heat balances and to estimate the physical properties; then the alternatives are assessed in economic terms using the internal rate of return. The environmental effects are estimated by an equivalent amount of pollutant taking into consideration the harmfulness of the different effluent substances; environmental risks also include occupational-health aspects, to choose an inherently healthier process. The inherent safety is estimated in terms of the inherent safety index as described later.

The final decision on the best alternative is not a clear choice, since the three parameters — cost-efficiency, environmental aspects and inherent safety — cannot be merged into a single figure in a unique way: process designers may emphasize these parameters differently depending on the company policy and the goal of the problem. Consequently, the alternatives have to be compared based on all the criteria, using for example the decision-chart method (Kepner and Tregoe, 1981) or the pairwise-comparison matrix presented by Saaty (1977).

Safety aspects are considered in two phases (Figure 11). In the rule-based synthesis, some safety-related rules are applied in process-concept selections — such as "separate corrosive or hazardous components first" or "avoid using chemically incompatible substances in the same process". Not all safety-related matters — such as the selection of raw materials — are considered in the synthesis phase; they are given by the user. Also, generating universal synthesis rules considering safety is not easy. Therefore it is important to analyse the alternative designs by inherent safety indices, which describe e.g. flammability, toxicity and process conditions.

The conceptual design phase is the most critical when designing inherently safer plants, since the alternative process concepts are created and analysed in this phase. This emphasizes the need to introduce safety-evaluation tools into preliminary process design: time and money are saved when fewer expensive safety modifications are needed during the later stages of design. The safety evaluation has to be closely integrated into the existing preliminary process design environments to be readily available during design; the safety tools also benefit from the existing databanks and simulation programs for physical-property and phase-condition calculations. The ISI allows the evaluation of the inherent safety of process alternatives in a computerized process synthesis environment — a flexible and interactive comparison of alternatives in the conceptual design phase.

14.2 Process Synthesis by Optimization 14.2 Tổng hợp quá trình bằng tối ưu hóa

Process synthesis can be considered as an optimization task. The problem is that the model to be optimized changes at the same time as the process configuration alters. Therefore the possible optimization approaches are MINLP (mixed integer nonlinear programming) and genetic algorithms. The MINLP approach has been used in many articles (e.g. Grossmann and Kravanja, 1995): the method is rigorous but requires a dedicated mathematical algorithm, some of which are still under development. In genetic optimization the solution is searched in an evolutionary manner, which as such is quite familiar to a practising engineer; the problems are the requirement of coding the problem as genes and the slowness of the algorithm (many unnecessary cases are calculated due to mutations and crossovers), and the method does not guarantee that an optimal solution is reached. GA has already been used for separation-process synthesis (Hurme, 1996).

14.3 Genetic Optimization 14.3 Tối ưu hóa di truyền

Genetic algorithms (GA) are computational models of natural evolution, which use operations related to genetics to guide an optimizing process in a complex search space. GAs work with sets of individuals which, when properly coded, represent potential solutions. Populations are processed iteratively, starting from a random population, following the phases of evaluation, selection, mating, crossover and mutation (Figure 12). Selection is based on the evaluation of the individuals of a given population by means of a fitness function. The cycle is repeated until a termination condition, such as an error tolerance, is reached. It is characteristic of GA that individuals characterized by a common feature and exhibiting a high fitness grow exponentially. Even though convergence is not always guaranteed, GAs have been successful in solving difficult optimization problems; GAs do not pose special requirements on the problem (such as continuity), except that a proper coding of individuals and an appropriate objective function for evaluating the fitness of each individual must be possible. The proper choice of the fitness function is important since it guides the selection and optimization procedure (Goldberg, 1989).

Hurme (1996) used GA to solve the synthesis problem of separating a mixture of hydrocarbons, and compared GA with a pure random version in which the crossover and mutation operations were replaced by a random generation of new solutions: there was no difference during the first generations, but it became significant after some generations — GA reached the solution after ten generations with about 1100 different possible solutions, while the random version required tens of generations. GA seems both fast compared to random optimization and not too computationally intensive.

Hurme and Heikkilä (1998) expanded the GA approach of Hurme (1996) with a safety function. Because the model is uncontinuous, ordinary optimization methods cannot be used, but a genetic algorithm is employed instead: the structure of the process is represented as a string of integers, which describes the operations required and how they are connected together, and an inherent safety index is used as the objective function (i.e. fitness). The index has been developed for safety estimations in the early stages of process design; most of its subindices are quite simple to estimate, except the subindex for safe process structure, which is estimated by case-based reasoning using a database of good and bad design cases. This index can also be used as an objective function to be optimized, if the inherent process safety is to be maximized in a systematic way.

14.4 Principle of the Method 14.4 Nguyên lý của phương pháp

In the approach by Hurme and Heikkilä (1998), inherent safety is the only objective function considered in the process synthesis. It is estimated by the Inherent Safety Index (Heikkilä et al., 1996), developed for inherent-safety estimations in conceptual process design. The structure of the process is represented as a string of integers (a gene) representing the required operations, their types and how they are connected; the inherent safety index is the fitness function. The basic steps of the genetic optimization algorithm are:

  1. Generation of an initial population of random separation sequences — the sequences describe both which equipment items are used and how they are connected.
  2. Random selection of sequences and generation of new solutions using a crossover procedure (the crossover location is also chosen randomly); since the crossover may result in an impossible sequence, a checking and revision procedure is required — its type depends on the type of process synthesized.
  3. Mutation of sequences at a randomly chosen location.
  4. Evaluation of the sequences using the inherent-safety fitness function.
  5. Selection of the best sequences and removal of the worst and redundant sequences, so that the population size stays constant.
  6. Repetition of steps 2–6 until the improvement is below the given tolerance.

14.4.1 Case Study; Separation Process 14.4.1 Nghiên cứu tình huống — quá trình tách

A synthesis problem on the purification of butenes is discussed (Hurme and Heikkilä, 1998): the aim is to synthesize the separation process by maximizing the inherent safety, measured as the Inherent Safety Index. In the process, a mixture of propane, 1-butene, n-butane, 2-butenes and n-pentane is separated to produce technically pure component streams; both ordinary distillation (method 1) and extractive distillations (methods 2 and 3) are used. Separation-synthesis problems are highly combinatorial when the number of components and separation methods is large: in this simple case of five components and three separation methods there are 1134 different solutions (Hurme, 1996); two of them are shown in Figure 13 as an example. The column vapor loads (vapor flow rates up the columns) are calculated to determine the approximate equipment sizes; an approximate method for calculating the vapor flow V in a column is (Hurme, 1996):

((11))V = D(RF(α − 1) + 1)

where RF = R / Rmin, V is the vapor flow, D the distillate flow rate, F the feed flow rate, α the relative volatility, R the reflux rate and Rmin the minimum reflux rate.

The steps of the genetic algorithm in this problem: 1) an initial population of random separation sequences is generated — the sequences describe both the order in which components are separated and the separation method used; e.g. the left-hand sequence in Figure 13 is described by the string 23 12 14 11 — the first integer is the separation method and the second the heavy-key component of the split in the column; 2) crossover — the crossover length is fixed to two components; since the crossover may result in a sequence containing some components twice, a checking and revision procedure is implemented (all components must be included; if not, the original instance is changed to the missing component); 3) mutation of both the separation method and the sequence at a randomly chosen location; 4) evaluation of the sequences by the inherent-safety fitness function; 5) selection of the best sequences and removal of the worst and redundant ones so that the population size stays constant; 6) steps 2–6 are repeated until the improvement is under the defined tolerance.

The initial population size was 5 sequences; the crossover population was 2 sequences and the mutated population 2 sequences per generation — 4 new sequences per generation in total; after selection the population size was kept at 5. The fitness function was based on the inherent safety index, simplified: there are only minor differences in the safety properties of the compounds, so most subindices are the same for all configurations; the equipment type (distillation) is the same, so the equipment-safety subindex is constant too; the process structures are quite similar, so the process-structure subindex was not evaluated and case-based reasoning was not needed. However, the number of columns changes if extractive distillation is used, so the fluid inventory becomes a major safety parameter; the inventory depends on the column sizes. It was assumed that all columns are designed for the same superficial vapor velocity, so the column area is directly proportional to the vapor flow rate, and the liquid hold-up proportional to the column area; therefore the total fluid inventory is considered directly proportional to the total vapor flow of the columns — calculated by Equation 11 for each column.

The genetic algorithm reached the solution usually in ten generations for this problem of more than 1100 solutions; a random optimization would require tens of generations. The best solution found is the first configuration (23 12 14 11) in Figure 13. The results with the inherent-safety objective function are somewhat different from those with an economic objective function (Hurme, 1996): with the inherent-safety function, simple distillations were favoured more; the exceptions are cases where extractive distillation could improve the separation very dramatically. The reason: in simple distillations only one column is required per split, but in extractive distillation two columns are needed since the solvent must also be separated — causing a larger fluid inventory, especially since the extraction solvent is highly flammable. The results are well justified by common sense: one of the principles of inherent safety is to use simpler designs and reduce inventories to enhance safety.

14.4.2 Case Study; Emulsion Polymerization Process 14.4.2 Nghiên cứu tình huống — quá trình trùng hợp nhũ

As another case study, a process synthesis of an emulsion polymerization process is given (Hurme and Heikkilä, 1998). In emulsion polymerization, unsaturated monomers or their solutions are dispersed in a continuous phase with the aid of an emulsifier and polymerized; the product is a dispersion of polymers called a latex. The raw materials are highly flammable unsaturated hydrocarbons and the reaction is exothermic — both cause a risk. Information on the safety properties, accidents, design recommendations and existing designs of the emulsion polymerization process was gathered from the literature; only the phases marked with an asterisk (*) in Table 31 were considered. About 50 design recommendations or cases on the topic were found; a case base was formed of this information together with some general design recommendations, and the cases were evaluated based on the safe process structure subindex, which was included into the cases in the database.

The main variables in the selected process phases were chosen and coded with integers for the synthesis procedure — including the operation and equipment types used and how they are connected. The 14 main variables were: 1) premixing; 2) preheating; 3) reactor type and mode of operation; 4) number of reactors; 5) reactor construction material; 6) reactor mixing; 7) baffles in the reactor; 8) reactor heat transfer system; 9) method of liquid transfers; 10) relief system location; 11) relief equipment type; 12) reaction stopping method; 13) relief recovery system; 14) vent treatment equipment.

The genetic optimization was started with an initial population size of five, generated randomly; the algorithm included crossover of two randomly selected sequences and random mutations on two sequences (the number of mutations per sequence varied from four at the beginning to one at the end). The steps: 1) a random initial population of 5; 2) crossover of two randomly selected sequences; 3) mutation of two sequences; 4) case-based reasoning on the safe process structure subindex; 5) calculation of the fitness using the inherent safety index — only inventory, equipment safety, heat of reaction and process-configuration subindices were considered; 6) selection of the best sequences so that the population size stays at five; 7) repetition of steps 2–7 until the fitness improvement is below the tolerance. The procedure converged in less than 20 generations; the optimum configuration had an Inherent Safety Index value of 16 (the optimization typically started from a value of 28–30). The inherently safest synthesized alternative is shown in Figure 14.

The process with the lowest inherent-safety index value contains the following features: a semibatch (instead of batch) reactor, resulting in a low monomer inventory in the reactor since the reaction starts immediately when the first monomers are fed; no premixing tank for the monomers, which would increase the inventory; only one large reactor instead of several smaller, to make the system simpler (an inherently safer process structure); two mixers on one shaft to increase the mixing efficiency as the liquid level changes; baffles for the same purpose; cooling by both the jacket and a reflux condenser; polished steel reactor construction, which has better heat-transfer properties than a glass-lined vessel; liquid transfers to and from the reactor by elevation to reduce the number of pumps (equipment safety); a relief system including a rupture disk, safer than a relief valve alone in fouling conditions; the relief led to a quench tank containing quench liquid to stop the reaction and separate the liquid phase from the relief — safer than an ordinary knock-out drum or cyclone (safe process structure); the vent led to a flare system after the quench tank; an ordinary flare is used, since it has a larger capacity than a controlled collection system or a scrubber; an inhibitor addition to the reactor is also included to stop the reaction chemically — a simple but possibly not always reliable method (Figure 14).

Lessons from the two case studies: the ISI works as a fitness function in genetic optimization — usually simplified since many subindices are constant across the alternatives; the inherently safer alternatives typically coincide with simpler designs and lower inventories.
