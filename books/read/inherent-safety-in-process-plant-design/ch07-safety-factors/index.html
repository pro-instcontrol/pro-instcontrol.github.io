As mentioned earlier, the lack of information complicates the safety considerations in preliminary process plant design. It is therefore important to utilize all the available details that may affect the inherent safety of the plant. To make this possible, the inherent safety characteristics should be evaluated systematically during process design. For this purpose, a dedicated methodology for estimating inherent safety using the scarce information available is required.

Most of the principles of inherent safety are useful already in the preliminary design phase, even though most process details are still missing. This is represented in Table 4, which shows at which project stage each feature of friendly design should be considered. In fact, the opportunities for installing inherent safety features decrease as the design progresses (Kletz, 1991). In this thesis an index-based method was selected, since inherent safety is formed of many interrelated subfactors. It is therefore more straightforward to represent these factors as subindices weighted by score means, as discussed later in Chapter 9.

The formation of the inherent safety indices is based on the following principle (Figure 5): the basic principles of inherent safety (Section 6.1) are first described as parameters (Table 5), and the most important of these parameters are selected to be implemented as inherent safety subindices.

Edwards and Lawrence (1993) presented a list of sixteen chemical and physical properties and process parameters available at the process-route selection stage (Table 5). Seven of these sixteen parameters were included in their index method (PIIS). Their selected parameters concentrate very much on the chemical process route and the chemistry. They also tested their selection by expert judgement, which gave support to their work (Edwards et al., 1996).

Intrinsic safety, however, is affected by both the process equipment and the properties of the chemical substances present. Therefore the index should reflect this fact. Parameters representing the process aspects of inherent safety were added to the list (Table 5): the type of equipment involved and the safety of the process structure, which describe the process configuration from a system point of view. A third parameter describing the interaction (reactivity) of the chemicals present was included as well, since this is an obvious source of risk.

Inventory, for example, was chosen by both groups: it is relative to the capacity of a process and to residence times (hold-ups) in vessels; it has a large effect on the degree of hazard and should be kept small by intensification. Phase is a release property which can be expressed by inventory and was therefore excluded. Reaction yield also expresses inventory, since higher yields decrease recycling and thus decrease reactor size.

Both groups chose temperature and pressure: temperature is a direct measure of the heat energy available at release; pressure is both a measure of the energy available at release and the energy available to cause a release. The heat of reaction (Heikkilä et al., 1996) measures the energy available from the reaction — a high heat of reaction may generate higher temperatures and dangerous runaway reactions. Another parameter to consider is reaction rate, but it does not directly express the hazardousness of a reaction (e.g. when the heat of reaction is low) and was therefore excluded.

Chemical interaction together with the heat of the side reaction expresses the potential reactiveness present in the process. Unwanted side reactions may be, for example, rapid polymerization, heat formation, and formation of flammable or toxic gas. Side reactions and possible reaction risks in storage systems are taken into account by the heat of reaction and chemical interaction. Both groups chose flammability, explosiveness and toxicity as the hazardous properties of substances; Heikkilä et al. (1996) also included corrosiveness, because in corrosive environments the right choice of construction materials is essential to inherent safety.

Viscosity has not been found meaningful: the hot-spot effect in poorly mixed viscous reactors can be included in the heat-of-reaction parameter. New phase generation and catalysts were not chosen either, because both parameters are considered through the hazardous properties of the substances (chemical interaction, explosiveness etc.); the same applies to the waste-products parameter.

The chosen meaningful parameters are: heat of reaction, flammability, explosiveness, toxicity, corrosiveness, chemical interaction, inventory, process temperature and pressure, equipment safety and safe process structure (Table 5). This does not mean that other factors affecting inherent safety are meaningless — on the contrary, they should be considered in more detail in further design stages.

From Table 6 it can be seen how the selected parameters connect to the basic principles of inherent safety. For instance, the equipment-safety and safe-process-structure subindices contain several inherent safety characteristics at once, such as limitation of effects or tolerance to maloperation. It is practical to include several characteristics in few parameters, since the inherent safety principles are both very broad and overlapping; the philosophy behind them cannot be described by just one process parameter. The selected parameters are discussed in more detail in the following sections.

7.1 Heat of Reaction 7.1 Nhiệt phản ứng

A reaction is exothermic if heat is generated. Reactions in which large quantities of heat or gas are released are potentially hazardous, particularly during fast decomposition or complete oxidation.

The main clue to the possible violence of any reaction lies in the heat liberated, the temperature that may be reached, and the volume and nature of any gases and vapours formed. Chemical characteristics that increase the potential of an explosion are high decomposition or reaction enthalpies and high rates of energy generation. Broadly speaking, any reaction that can lead to a temperature rise of 300°C or to the production of a significant amount of gas or vapour may pose a significant hazard (King, 1990; IChemE, 1987).

Substances with a positive enthalpy of formation release energy during decomposition. Energetic substances can in general be identified by hazardous molecular structures such as peroxide, nitro and azo groups and double and triple bonds. The presence of these groups does not necessarily imply that the substance is hazardous (IChemE, 1987); on the other hand, the initial absence of unstable groups is no guarantee of long-term stability. King (1990) has pointed out that the rates of chemical reactions often bear little or no relation to the heat they generate: some very fast reactions (e.g. ionic reactions in aqueous solution) produce little or no heat, while others that develop a great deal of heat proceed very slowly in the absence of a catalyst. Thus, instead of reaction rate or the presence of unstable structures, the reaction heat has been chosen as the criterion.

The energy change (ΔUr) during the reaction equals the difference between the energies of formation of the products and the reactants:

((1))ΔUr = Σ(Uf)products − Σ(Uf)reactants

If the reaction occurs under isobaric conditions (constant pressure), energy can be replaced by enthalpy (ΔHr) and the equation can be written for constant-volume conditions as follows:

((2))ΔHr = Σ(Hf)products − Σ(Hf)reactants

7.2 Hazardous Substances 7.2 Chất nguy hiểm

Hazardous substances present in the process are identified on the basis of flammability, explosiveness and toxicity. The flammability of gases and of vapours of flammable liquids is a great concern in the process industries: the result of an ignition can be a fire, an explosion, or both. Accidental fires and explosions of flammable mixtures with air often follow the escape of combustible materials or the inlet of air into process equipment.

7.2.1 Flammability 7.2.1 Khả cháy

Flammability means the ease with which a material burns in air (King, 1990); it applies to gases, liquids and solids. It is very important to know, for instance, in cases of leaks. A liquid whose flash point is below the processing or storage temperature can give rise to a flammable mixture and is generally considered hazardous.

The flammability of liquids depends on the lower flammability limit of the material and its vapour pressure at the prevailing temperature. The flash point is the lowest temperature at which a liquid gives off enough flammable vapour at or near its surface to ignite in an intimate mixture with air given a spark or a flame (i.e. the vapour pressure is high enough for the gas concentration to correspond to the lower flammability limit). The flash point is therefore the main parameter in the hazard classification of liquids, and government regulations are based on it (Lees, 1996). The boiling point may be taken as an indication of volatility; for a flammable liquid it can be a direct measure of the hazard involved in its use (Sax, 1979).

The Finnish legislation (Pyötsiä, 1994) classifies the flammability of chemical substances on the basis of their flash and boiling points — similar to the European Union directives on hazardous substances. Similar approaches are used by the Dow Fire and Explosion Index (1987) and by Edwards and Lawrence (1993).

7.2.2 Explosiveness 7.2.2 Tính nổ

Explosiveness here means the tendency of chemicals to form an explosive mixture in air. When an explosion occurs in the atmosphere, energy is released in a short time and in a small volume, generating an audible pressure wave. An explosion is a sudden and violent release of energy, deriving either from physical or from chemical energy.

Dangers from physical energy (pressure) are considered by the pressure subindex, so only chemical energy is discussed here. The explosive chemicals may be either high explosives or flammable gases: high explosives contain their own oxygen and need no external oxygen to explode — they are considered by the heat-of-reaction parameter; flammable (usually organic) gases explode in excess air to yield a large volume of CO2 and H2O and are discussed by the explosiveness parameter.

The explosive nature of chemical substances is described with the upper and lower explosion limits. The explosiveness of a vapour cloud depends especially on the lower explosion limit (LEL) — the concentration of vapour at which the cloud can be ignited. The wider the range between the explosion limits, the more probable it is that the cloud lies in the flammable region, i.e. the higher the tendency for explosion; Edwards and Lawrence (1993) used explosion limits to determine the explosiveness of substances. Using industrial chemicals with less explosive potential makes the process more intrinsically safe. The most dangerous explosions come from large clouds of flammable material finding an ignition source — Flixborough (Lees, 1996) is an example of the destruction caused by such an incident.

7.2.3 Toxic Exposure 7.2.3 Phơi nhiễm độc tính

Marshall (1987) and Wells (1980) have described toxicity as a property of a substance which destroys life or injures health when introduced into or absorbed by a living organism. The toxic hazard is a measure of the likelihood of such damage occurring, determined by the frequency and duration of exposure and the concentration of the chemical.

The estimation of toxicity is based on animal experimentation. Toxicity depends on the physical and biological characteristics of the substance, the way of entry (swallowing, through the skin, breathing) and the size of the dose. Persistent exposure of workers to relatively low levels of many industrial chemicals can produce chronic disease leading to serious disability or premature death (King, 1990). The extreme health hazards of process materials are carcinogenicity, teratology and mutagenity; special precautions are needed when handling substances possessing these properties (Fawsett, 1982).

According to Wells (1980), probably the most commonly used toxicity term is the Threshold Limit Value (TLV): the concentration in air which can be breathed without harmful effect for five consecutive 8-hour working days. TLVs are based on effects ranging from irritation to physiological damage; in the industrial context they are the most usable toxicity values, since their aim is to protect employees at work. The threshold of odour is important when the TLV is lower than the concentration needed for an odour to appear (Siegell, 1996) — a crucial factor in emergency planning. Because legislation is usually made to protect people in general, e.g. the Finnish legislation (Pyötsiä, 1994) uses LD50 and LC50 for toxicity, while irritation and other effects are dealt with separately. LD50 is the dose administered orally or by skin absorption causing the death of 50% of the test group within a 14-day observation period; LC50 is the concentration in air to which exposure for 24 hours or less would cause the death of 50% of a test group.

7.3 Corrosiveness 7.3 Ăn mòn

All metals corrode under certain conditions. Internal corrosion is caused by galvanic corrosion, pitting, corrosion fatigue, stress corrosion cracking, stray currents, etc. Corrosion reduces the reliability and integrity of the plant: it reduces the strength of materials and causes leaks; corrosion products affect process materials, moving parts and process efficiency and cause fouling. Corrosion proceeds slowly and is usually more the concern of engineers than of safety professionals — yet it has caused catastrophic failures with heavy loss of life (King, 1990).

The safety problems caused by the corrosive properties of the process streams can be prevented by a proper choice of construction materials. The mechanical design is based on process design values (temperature and pressure) beyond which operation is not allowed since it can cause hazardous effects. The mechanical strength of equipment is reduced during the life of the process by corrosion; corrosion is usually measured as corrosion rates (mm/a). In equipment design, corrosion is taken into account by the selection of material and the corresponding corrosion allowance — the material is selected so that the allowance is not exceeded during the lifetime of the equipment. Corrosion rates are not always known during predesign, but a rough type of construction material is often anticipated. Since the need for a better material most often indicates more corrosive conditions, a classification based on the type of construction material can be justified.

The Dow Fire and Explosion Index (1987) also considers corrosion risks, but the penalties are given through unacceptable corrosion rates. Design standards also include advice on acceptable corrosion rates (Uhlig and Reviev, 1985).

7.4 Chemical Interaction 7.4 Tương tác hóa học

The various chemical substances present in a process plant may, under favourable conditions, react with each other or with air or water, causing safety or technical problems. This chemical interaction is based on the chemical reactivity of each substance with the other substances present in the plant. As a potential process hazard, the chemical reactivity of any substance should be considered in the following contexts:

  • reactivity with the elements and compounds with which it is required to react in the process
  • reactivity with atmospheric oxygen
  • reactivity with water
  • reactivity with itself — its propensity to polymerise, condense, decompose and explode
  • reactivity with other materials with which it may come into contact unintentionally in process, storage or transport
  • reactivity with materials of construction — its corrosivity (see Section 7.3)

In a process, chemical interaction is either intended or unintended. The wanted reactions are under control, e.g. in the reactor. Unwanted chemical interaction can lead to unpleasant surprises: heat formation, fire, formation of harmless non-flammable gas, formation of toxic gas, formation of flammable gas, explosion, rapid polymerization, or soluble toxic chemicals.

The United States Environmental Protection Agency (Hatayama et al., 1980) has provided a matrix for determining the compatibility of hazardous wastes: the interactivity of substances is presented with the consequences of the reaction, such as heat formation, fire, rapid polymerization and formation of flammable gases. Similar matrices also exist, e.g. Chempat (Leggett, 1997).

7.5 Inventory 7.5 Tồn kho

"Any material when … present in large quantity may be classified as hazardous." — Wells (1980)

The total quantity of material to be stored is set initially by process engineering, commercial and political considerations, although subsequent hazard considerations may reduce the quantity or lead to improved layout or deconcentration of storage facilities. In general, large inventories in one place are unfavourable in the cases of fire or rupture of a vessel. Potential severity can be reduced by keeping inventories low, by minimizing the reactor size and by avoiding storage of potentially hazardous materials in the synthesis train (CCPS, 1995a). For instance, large quantities of very toxic gases and volatile liquids were one of the major mistakes in the Bhopal accident (King, 1990). After Bhopal and several other accidents the authorities have tightened the limits for the inventories of flammable gases and liquids.

The amount of a substance present in the plant has a large effect on the degree of hazard. It is advised to use a minimum storage inventory of hazardous materials and to construct process and storage areas away from residential — or potentially residential — areas.

7.6 Temperature 7.6 Nhiệt độ

Operation in extreme conditions has its own problems and hazards; extreme conditions usually mean very low or high temperatures or pressures. Temperature is a direct measure of the heat energy available at release (Edwards and Lawrence, 1993). Temperature is the most important factor influencing reaction rate, as shown by the Arrhenius equation: in practice, an increase of 10°C increases a specific reaction rate by two to four times, depending on the energy of activation (CCPS, 1995a).

High temperatures combined with high pressures greatly increase the amount of energy stored in the plant. High-temperature plants have severe problems with materials of construction: high temperature implies thermal stresses, particularly during start-up and shut-down. Low-temperature plants are subject to thermal stresses for the same reason; these stresses need to be allowed for and, as far as possible, avoided.

Low-temperature plants contain large amounts of fluids kept in the liquid state only by pressure and temperature; if for any reason the plant cannot be kept cold, the liquids begin to vapourize. Another hazard in low-temperature plants is possible impurities in the fluids, liable to come out of solution as solids: deposited solids may cause not only blockage but, in some cases, an explosion. A materials problem in low-temperature plants is low-temperature embrittlement. The material requirements are well understood; the problems arise from the installation of incorrect materials or from the flow of low-temperature fluids to sections constructed in mild steel — both refer to inherent safety problems.

7.7 Pressure 7.7 Áp suất

High pressure greatly increases the amount of energy available in the plant: in an atmospheric plant the stored energy is mainly chemical, while in a high-pressure plant there is in addition the energy of compressed permanent gases and of fluids kept liquid only by pressure. Although high pressures in themselves do not pose serious materials problems, the combination with high temperatures, low temperatures or aggressive materials does. The problem is to obtain the material strength required by high-pressure operation despite these factors.

With high-pressure operation the problem of leaks becomes much more serious: the amount of fluid which can leak through a given hole is greater on account of the pressure difference, and the fluid may be a liquid which flashes off as the pressure is reduced. Low (subatmospheric) pressures are not in general as hazardous as the other extreme conditions; but in low-pressure plants handling flammables, the ingress of air with consequent formation of a flammable mixture is a real hazard, and steam explosions may take place if a volatile material (e.g. water) is fed to a low-pressure system, causing consequent vapourization.

It is claimed that it is safer to design for total containment (Englund, 1990): the processing equipment is designed to withstand the maximum pressure expected from a runaway or another hazardous incident. This requires detailed knowledge of the process and of the possible overpressure — best obtained from experimental data combined with theoretical analysis. Unfortunately, in many cases this information is not at hand when comparing process alternatives. In real life it is not often practical to construct for an emergency pressure which can be very much higher than the normal operating pressure; therefore a relief system is nearly always required, together with added-on protective equipment such as a flare or a gas scrubber.

7.8 Equipment Safety 7.8 An toàn thiết bị

Equipment safety tries to measure the possibility that a piece of equipment is unsafe (Heikkilä and Hurme, 1998a). Here equipment includes all major pieces such as pumps and vessels, but not piping, valves or instruments as separate entities. Equipment safety considers the safety of the equipment as such, without interactions through the process with other equipment — that latter aspect is described by the safe process structure (Section 7.9). However, interactions through layout, such as a furnace being a source of ignition for a leak from another piece of equipment, are considered by equipment safety.

The comparison of the safety of equipment is not straightforward: it depends on several features of both the process and the equipment themselves. It can be evaluated from quantitative accident and failure data and from engineering practice and recommendations. Experience has been used for layout recommendations and for the development of safety analysis methods such as the Dow E&F Index (Dow, 1987). Statistics contain details, causes and rates of failures of equipment, and data on equipment involved in large losses.

Data on equipment involved in large losses has been collected, for instance, by Mahoney (1997) and Instone (1989). Mahoney analysed the 170 largest losses in refineries, petrochemical and gas processing plants from 1966 to 1996 — nearly all involved fires or explosions. Instone analysed some 2,000 large loss claims of the hydrocarbon industry at Cigna Insurance. The difference is that Mahoney analysed which equipment items were the primary causes of losses, whereas Instone listed the equipment involved in the losses.

During plant design the safety of process equipment is also recognized by layout: the objectives are to minimize risk to personnel, to minimize escalation (within the plant and to adjacent plants) and to ensure adequate emergency access, as well as adequate access for maintenance and operations. Plant layout is a crucial safety factor because of segregation of risks, containment of accidents and limitation of exposure. Safe layout is designed on the basis of design standards and local regulations; appropriate spacing of unit operations within a process is also considered an inherently safer solution (Bollinger et al., 1996).

Spacing recommendations have been presented in the literature as matrices and lists of typical minimum distances between process items (Industrial Risk Insurers, 1991; Bausbacher and Hunt, 1993; Prugh, 1982). The distance to another item depends mostly on the safety properties of the items; maintenance and access clearances are usually shorter than safety clearances. Therefore it can be assumed that the average of the recommended spacings mostly reflects the general unsafety of a specific process item.

The frequently used Dow Fire & Explosion Hazard Index (1987) gives penalties for fired equipment and certain specified rotating equipment — part of the Special Process Hazards term. There is also a certain amount of statistical information on the failures of process system components: Arulanantham and Lees (1981) studied pressure vessel and fired heater failures in plants such as olefins plants, defining failure as a condition in which a crack, leak or other defect has developed to the extent that repair or replacement is required — including some potentially dangerous as well as all catastrophic failures. Failure rates are related to some extent to the safety of process items: equipment with a long history of failures may cause safety problems in the future, so another piece of equipment should be considered. It should be remembered that not all reliability information expresses safety directly, since not all failures are dangerous and not all accidents are due to equipment failures.

7.9 Safe Process Structure 7.9 Cấu trúc quá trình an toàn

Safe process structure describes the inherent safety of the process configurations: which operations are involved in the process and how they are connected together. It describes the safety of the process from a system-engineering point of view: how well certain unit operations or other items work together, how they should be connected and controlled, and how auxiliary systems such as cooling, heating or relief systems should be configured and connected to the main process (Heikkilä et al., 1998). The importance of this subindex is increasing as processes become more integrated through heat and mass-transfer networks.

Most factors affecting inherent safety are quite straightforward to estimate, being based on the physical and chemical properties of the compounds present. An inherently safe process structure cannot be defined by explicit rules; one has to rely on standards, recommendations and accident reports — information based on the experience gained in the operation practice of different processes (Lees, 1996). Accident reports give valuable information about possible weaknesses in different process solutions, and extensive databases have been collected from them (Anon, 1996); from this data a database of good and bad designs can be built.

Since an inherently safe process structure cannot be described by explicit rules, the reasoning has to be based on analogies: the current design is compared against known safe or unsafe design cases in the database. This is the same approach practising engineers use mentally to generate new process designs; when computerized it is called case-based reasoning (Gonzalez and Dankel, 1993).
