11. Subindices for Process Inherent Safety Index 11. Các chỉ số thành phần của chỉ số an toàn nội tại quá trình

The Process Inherent Safety Index expresses the inherent safety of the process itself, including the equipment and the operating parameters. The Index has been divided into subindices for inventory, temperature, pressure, equipment safety and safe process structure.

11.1 Inventory Subindex 11.1 Chỉ số tồn kho

An exact calculation of inventory is difficult in the conceptual-design phase, since the size of equipment is not usually known. The mass flows in the process are, however, known from the design capacity. Therefore it is practical to base the estimation of inventory on mass flows and an estimated residence time: inventory is included in the ISI as a mass flow in the ISBL equipment (including recycles) with a one-hour nominal residence time for each process vessel (reactor, distillation column, etc.). For large storage tanks the size should be estimated. The total inventory is the sum of the inventories of all process vessels.

In the OSBL area the tank sizes are normally not known in conceptual design, which means that the OSBL inventory cannot readily be calculated — it depends not only on the ISBL process type but also on local conditions, logistics, etc. For the OSBL inventory, values based on the Mond Index (ICI, 1985) were used. These were also used for ISBL by Edwards et al. (1993), but the experts criticized this, since the relevant inventory scale in ISBL is much smaller; also, due to a tighter layout, the same inventory in ISBL is more dangerous than in OSBL. Therefore a new scale was developed by scaling the Mond values using the expert recommendations in Lawrence's work (1996) — see Table 15.

11.2 Process Temperature Subindex 11.2 Chỉ số nhiệt độ quá trình

Temperature is an indicator of the heat energy in the system. The hazard increases at higher temperatures because of the energy content itself and also because the strength of materials becomes weaker at high or very low temperatures. Great temperature changes between ambient (shutdown) and operating temperatures also cause thermal stress, which may increase the hazard of a loss of containment.

Process temperature in the ISI is determined on the basis of the maximum temperature in the process area under investigation — feasible, since preliminary estimates of temperatures and pressures are available in the early stage of process design. Because the hazards in the low-temperature range increase due to mechanical problems and freezing if water is present, temperatures below 0 °C are also included in the index. When there are many temperature levels in the area under study, the highest temperature subindex value is applied. The temperature ranges have been chosen on the basis of the danger to humans and of material strength as a function of temperature: 0–70 °C is harmless to people in general; 70–150 °C is the typical range of mild-temperature processes; the next limit is 300 °C, beyond which the strength of carbon steel is decreased considerably compared to room temperature; higher temperatures require materials with better heat resistance. Subzero temperatures cause problems as explained in Section 7.6.

11.3 Process Pressure Subindex 11.3 Chỉ số áp suất quá trình

Pressure is an indicator of potential energy which affects the leak rates in the case of loss of containment. Higher pressures also pose stricter requirements on vessel strength. Leaks in vacuum equipment may cause inlet of air and a consequent explosion. In the ISI the process pressure is determined on the basis of the maximum pressure in the process area under normal operation; the pressure limits in Table 17 are based on the Dow F&E Index (Dow, 1987).

11.4 Equipment Safety Subindex 11.4 Chỉ số an toàn thiết bị

The Equipment Safety Subindex has been included in the ISI to get a better view of total inherent safety by discussing also the selection and type of equipment used (Heikkilä and Hurme, 1998a and 1998b). Equipment safety tries to measure the possibility that a piece of equipment is unsafe. Here equipment includes all major pieces such as pumps and vessels, but not piping, valves or instruments — these have not been designed yet in the early design stages. The equipment safety index considers the safety of the equipment as such, without interactions through the process with other equipment — this latter aspect is described by the safe process structure subindex. However, interactions through layout, such as a furnace being a source of ignition for a leak from another piece of equipment, are considered by the equipment safety index.

The main failure of equipment is a loss of process containment. The consequences depend on the properties and the amount of the leaking material and the conditions inside and outside the equipment. Pumps and compressors (Marshall, 1987) are perhaps the most vulnerable items of pressurised systems: they contain moving parts and are subject to erosion and cavitation; they also produce vibration which may lead to fatigue failure. Both seals and bearings are liable to fail. Agitator systems present difficulties due to mechanical stresses, though they operate at much lower speeds than pumps.

11.4.1 Evaluation of Equipment Safety 11.4.1 Đánh giá an toàn thiết bị

The comparison of the safety of equipment is not straightforward: it depends on the equipment themselves and on the process conditions. Equipment safety can be evaluated from quantitative accident and failure data and from engineering practice and recommendations. Experience-based information is found in layout recommendations and safety analysis methods such as the Dow F&E Index (1987); quantitative data in accident and operational statistics. It should be remembered that not all reliability or failure information expresses safety directly: not all failures are dangerous and not all accidents are due to equipment failures.

For the Equipment Safety Subindex the process plant area is divided into two areas with different safety properties. The ISBL area is where the raw materials are converted into products — characterised by a large number of equipment and piping located in a concentrated way in a small area. The rest of the plant is the OSBL area — characterised by large inventories of fluids, which are often flammable and/or toxic; the number of equipment items is smaller but their size larger, and the layout is much more scattered, to enhance safety.

11.4.2 Equipment Layout 11.4.2 Bố trí thiết bị

During plant design the safety of process equipment is also recognised by layout: the objectives are to minimise risk to personnel, to minimise escalation (both within the plant and to adjacent plants), and to ensure adequate emergency access, as well as access for maintenance and operations. Safe plant layout is designed on the basis of design standards and local regulations, often expressed as minimum clearances between equipment. Safety distances can in principle be calculated by estimating the size of possible leaks, the probability of ignition and explosion and their effects; since this approach is full of difficulties, experience-based minimum distances are used instead (Wells, 1980). Even though process layouts are not available in preliminary design, the spacing recommendations give an idea of the risk of certain pieces of equipment to their environment.

Layout spacings are also affected by other factors than safety: the space requirements of maintenance, repair works and proper performing of process operations; enough room for pipes, supports and foundations. The spacing recommendations have been presented in the literature as matrices and lists of typical minimum distances. In this work the equipment spacing matrices of Industrial Risk Insurers (1991), Bausbacher and Hunt (1993), Prugh (1982), Mecklenburgh (1985) and the Institut Français du Petrole have been compared. A suitable distance to another process item depends mostly on the safety properties of the process items; clearances required for maintenance and access are usually shorter than safety clearances. Therefore it was assumed that the average of the recommended equipment spacings is mostly related to the general unsafety of a specific process item, not to maintenance aspects.

The evaluation of the layout spacing data was done by calculating the averages of the recommended spacings; where operational and maintenance-access spacings were not included in some matrices, a minimum distance of one metre was used. It was noticed that the order of the process items according to their average spacing requirements is almost identical in all referred matrices: for ISBL layout (Table 18), furnaces, compressors and high-hazard reactors were at the top of the lists, while equipment handling nonflammable and nontoxic materials was at the other end. The former needed extra spacing for safety; the latter required only enough room for operation, repair and maintenance. The exception is the higher ranking of towers in the matrix of Industrial Risk Insurers (1991) — probably because of the large inventory of material, which leads to large economical losses in a case of rupture.

Cooling towers have large spacings around them: if the release of flammable materials into the cooling water and then into the atmosphere is possible, there must not be any ignition sources near the tower; cooling towers produce large volumes of wet air, which can cause fog, precipitation, freezing and corrosion downwind, and they require water basins and substantial foundations. Other utility systems have wide recommendations too: boilers have flame and hot surfaces which can act as ignition sources; large distances are often recommended for compressors to separate possible leaks from ignition sources — compressors also cause considerable vibration, which may cause leaks in piping, and need clear space around them to be maintained and operated properly.

Storage vessels are usually located on tank farms. The space around a tank and the distances to other equipment depend on the materials stored, their potential hazardousness and the possibility of unexpected changes in storage conditions. Fluid storages should be in a safe location away from process and public areas; it is also important to prevent fire spreading between tanks by keeping the heat radiation at an acceptable level (Mecklenburgh, 1985). Storage vessels may be atmospheric or pressurised, fixed- or floating-roof tanks, or low/high-temperature tanks; the spacing recommendations are given for each vessel type separately, according to the phase of the fluid, storage pressure and flash points (Table 19). Pumps may also handle fluids below or above their flash or autoignition points — recognised by general layout recommendations too.

11.4.4 Equipment in Other Indices 11.4.4 Thiết bị trong các chỉ số khác

A frequently used safety index in the process industry is the Dow Fire & Explosion Hazard Index (1987). The Dow F&E Index gives penalties for fired equipment and certain specified rotating equipment — part of the Special Process Hazards, within which the penalties of a process unit are summed. Fired equipment receives penalties if the material in the process unit near the air intake of the fired heater could be released above its boiling point, is a combustible dust, or could be released above its boiling point; the penalty depends on the distance from the possible leak source and varies between 0.1 and 1.0 — e.g. a distance of 15 m gives penalties of 0.27 (above the flash point) and 0.60 (above the boiling point). Any situation involving a material processed below its flash point receives no penalty.

Large rotating equipment — 1) compressors in excess of 450 kW, 2) pumps in excess of 56 kW, 3) agitators and circulating pumps in which failure could produce a process exotherm, and 4) other large, high-speed rotating equipment with a significant loss history, e.g. centrifuges — all have a constant penalty of 0.5. The scale of penalties is much narrower than the scale of recommended layout spacings or the cost of large losses, because the Dow F&E Index takes into account only large rotating equipment — known to be more likely to contribute to accidents than smaller equipment, which is safer and receives no penalties.

11.4.5 Equipment Failures and Their Evaluation 11.4.5 Hỏng hóc thiết bị và đánh giá

There is a certain amount of statistical information available on the failures of process system components. Arulanantham and Lees (1981) studied pressure-vessel failures in process plants such as olefins plants: failure is defined as a condition in which a crack, leak or other defect has developed to the extent that repair or replacement is required — a definition which includes some potentially dangerous as well as all catastrophic failures. In olefins plants, fired heaters have failure rates of about 0.4 failures/year, process pressure vessels 0.0025 failures/year and heat exchangers 0.0015 failures/year — fired heaters are much unsafer than process pressure vessels.

In the Canvey study (King, 1990; Health and Safety Executive, 1978) of the potential hazards to the public from the industrial activities in the Canvey Island/Thurrock area of Essex, pressure vessels had a ten times bigger assumed frequency of catastrophic failures than LPG pumps. Crawley and Grant (1997) developed a risk assessment tool for new offshore installations: they examined typical leak frequencies of equipment items and the ignition probability of these leaks in four pressure bands, making it possible to define leak size and frequency for any piece of equipment and the ignited leak frequency. In offshore installations, gas separation vessels were found to have a ten times higher ignited event frequency than oil pumps.

11.4.6 Equipment Safety Subindex for ISBL 11.4.6 Chỉ số an toàn thiết bị cho ISBL

From the data discussed above: the order of process items in the layout spacing recommendations is almost identical — furnaces and fired heaters at the top (Table 18); the next group is formed by compressors and high-hazard reactors; air coolers, ordinary reactors and high-hazard pumps appear next; after that come towers, process drums, heat exchangers and pumps; the last and safest group is formed of equipment handling nonflammable and nontoxic materials. The only discrepancy is the higher ranking of towers and drums in the Industrial Risk Insurers (1991) data — probably because of the large inventory of flammables; since inventory is considered separately by the inventory subindex, the vessels may best be included in the same group as heat exchangers and pumps.

Accident statistics on the equipment involved in large losses give somewhat contradictory information (Table 20): according to Mahoney (1992), the most common primary accident cause is the reactor, next are process drums, whereas heaters are among the safest; this contrasts with Instone's (1989) data, where heaters and boilers were the most common process items in accidents. Since furnaces are sources of ignition for flammable leaks from other equipment, they are not necessarily listed as primary causes even though they are probably involved as secondary causes in many losses. Therefore the inclusion of both reactors and furnaces in the list of the most unsafe equipment is well justified.

We have ranked furnaces unsafer (score 4) than reactors (score 3 or 2), since fired heaters are the most common ignition sources (Instone, 1989) for any leaks; Instone also lists furnaces as the most commonly involved process items in losses; the Dow F&E Index (1987) can give higher penalties for furnaces than for reactors (cooling systems and agitators) or large compressors; and Arulanantham and Lees (1981) give very high mechanical failure rates to olefin-plant fired heaters. Compressors are ranked very unsafe because they are referred to as very vulnerable process items (Marshall, 1987): they contain moving parts, are subject to vibration and can release flammable gas in a case of failure; they are also very high in the layout list. For process drums, towers, heat exchangers and pumps (below autoignition), Mahoney (1997) and Instone (1989) give similar low loss statistics and Arulanantham and Lees (1981) roughly the same mechanical failure rates — from this and the layout data, they can be grouped together as one single low score level (score 1).

To summarize: the process items were arranged into five groups — the safest group is the equipment handling nontoxic and nonflammable material; the second group contains common process items such as process drums, pumps and heat exchangers; the third group hazardous equipment such as reactors and pumps above autoignition; the fourth group more hazardous items such as high-hazard reactors; the most unsafe group is the equipment containing ignition sources such as fired heaters. 11.4.7 Equipment Safety Subindex for OSBL 11.4.7 Chỉ số an toàn thiết bị cho OSBL

For offsite equipment the scores 0–3 have been used instead of the 0–4 of ISBL (Heikkilä and Hurme, 1998b), since the offsites represent only one third of all losses (Instone, 1989) and are therefore not as essential as ISBL. Also, much of the OSBL risk is due to the large inventory of flammable or toxic chemicals — discussed by the inventory, flammability and toxicity subindices — and equipment is probably safer in OSBL than in ISBL because of the larger layout spacings.

The order of process items in the layout spacing recommendations (Table 19) is quite similar: flares are at the top of the list; the next unsafe are cooling towers, boilers and compressors; pressure storage tanks appear next; after that come low-pressure and atmospheric storage tanks and pumps of flammable liquids; the last and safest group is formed of equipment handling nonflammable and nontoxic materials. Accident statistics (Table 21) give somewhat contradictory information: according to Mahoney (1997), the most common primary accident cause within offsite systems is the tank; fired heaters and boilers represent only 2% of losses, while in layout they are recommended about the same spacings as tanks. Since heaters and boilers are very common sources of ignition (Table 22), their inclusion in the list of the most unsafe equipment is well justified. Flares are not high in the lists of Mahoney (1997) and Instone (1989); however, Planas-Cuchi et al. (1997) list them as equally important as furnaces and boilers together as ignition sources (Table 22). From this it has been concluded that flares should be included in the same equipment hazard group with boilers and furnaces — all equipment which acts as a source of ignition through flame (furnaces, boilers, flares) is considered the most dangerous and given score 3, due to the high loss statistics (Instone, 1989, Table 21) and their function as ignition sources (Table 22).

The next index group (score 2) contains high- and low-pressure (p < 1 barg) tanks, refrigerated storage tanks, compressors and cooling towers — all of which represent a source of flammable gas and are located high in the layout-spacing and large-loss lists. Atmospheric storage tanks of flammable liquids and pumps of flammable or toxic liquids belong to group 1 — sources of flammable liquid which do not produce a vapour cloud directly. Equipment handling nonflammable and nontoxic material belongs to the safest group (score 0). See Table 24.

11.5 Safe Process Structure Subindex 11.5 Chỉ số cấu trúc quá trình an toàn

A process structure defines which operations are involved in the process and how they are connected together. Therefore the Safe Process Structure Subindex describes the safety of the process from a systems-engineering point of view: how well certain unit operations or other process items work together, how they should be connected and controlled together; also how auxiliary systems such as cooling, heating or relief systems should be configured and connected to the main process. The importance of this subindex is increasing as the processes become more integrated through heat and mass-transfer networks (Heikkilä et al., 1998). The Process Structure Subindex does not describe the safety of the process items as such or their interaction through the non-process route (i.e. through layout) — this is described by the Equipment Safety Subindex (Section 11.4).

11.5.1 Evaluation of Safe Process Structure 11.5.1 Đánh giá cấu trúc quá trình an toàn

Many different alternative process configurations can be created for a process in the conceptual design phase; in choosing the most feasible alternative, safety should be one of the major evaluation criteria. Therefore information on the safety features of alternative process structures is needed in preliminary process design. Most of the other subindices of the ISI are quite straightforward to estimate, since they are based e.g. on the physical and chemical properties of the compounds present. The process structure subindex looks at the process from a systems-engineering point of view and is much more difficult to estimate: in fact there is no explicit way of estimating the safety of the process structure, and one has to rely on experience-based data documented as standards, design recommendations and accident reports.

11.5.2 Sources of Experience Based Safety Information 11.5.2 Nguồn thông tin an toàn dựa trên kinh nghiệm

Process solutions have shown their strong and weak, safe and unsafe points in operation practice. The knowledge of practised solutions consists of the details collected during operation and maintenance: practised solutions reveal, for instance, which unit operations are preferable for certain purposes and how the units can be connected safely together. Some of the information can also be found in design standards created on the basis of the operating experience of existing process plants (Lees, 1996).

Another source of design information is the accident reports made after an accident. They give valuable information on the possible weaknesses that can occur in unit operations when used for certain purposes. In the past many unit operations have shown their adverse characteristics; this information is mainly collected into accident reports and included in safety standards. Accident reports tell us, for example: which process equipment configurations have unfavourable properties; which types of chemicals do not suit certain unit operations; which unit operations/configurations are risky; when the connection of process units should be avoided. The difficulty in utilizing accident reports lies in the lack of accident-report standards — the reports vary a lot in how they document the details of the accident, the path to the final event, the causes and the consequences. Still, the reports can tell much experience-based information which can — and should — be utilized in designing new plants. In fact, a major goal in improving the design of safe process plants should be to enhance the reuse of design experience: the same mistakes are done again and again (Kletz, 1991).

A more refined form of the accident report is an accident database, where all the reports are presented in a standardized format; extensive databanks have already been collected from accident reports (Anon, 1996). Such a standardized format allows easier retrieval of accident information, also by computerized means.

11.5.3 Structure of the Database 11.5.3 Cấu trúc cơ sở dữ liệu

The basis for the estimation of safe process structure lies in the integration of two types of information sources: 1) recommendations and standards on how the process should be designed, and 2) an accident database describing the negative cases from which one can learn. Therefore a casebase of good and bad design cases is needed; both sources should be readily available to the design engineer through the database. A design problem can be compared with the cases in this combined databank, for instance by case-based reasoning (CBR).

In this approach, accident cases and design recommendations can be analysed level by level. In the database the knowledge of known processes is divided into categories: process — an independent processing unit (e.g. a hydrogenation unit); subprocess — an independent part of a process, such as a reactor or separation section; system — an independent part of a subprocess, such as a distillation column with all its auxiliary systems; subsystem — a functional part of a system, such as a reactor heat-recovery system or a column overhead system including its control systems; equipment — a unit operation or unit process such as a heat exchanger, a reactor or a distillation column; detail — an item in a pipe or a piece of equipment (a tray in a column, a control valve in a pipe). See Figure 6.

A search for cases in the databank can be made on these levels according to the nature of the design problem: if a process is being designed from the beginning, the first search is made for a whole process; the search is then made for those subprocesses, systems, subsystems and equipment which are relevant for the design. On the basis of the retrieved information the designer can evaluate the right index value for the process structure of the section under review. The input data for a database search contains information on the process level and on the raw materials and products, reaction types and their details such as catalysts and the phase of the reaction. As output there is information about unfavourable process configurations, recommended configurations and accident cases.

A plant is divided into inside and offsite battery-limit areas; the configurations of ISBL and OSBL areas differ considerably — generally the size of equipment, the amount of chemicals and the spacings are larger in the OSBL area — and this aspect is included in the database as well. The database does not always contain information directly related to the process under review; therefore it is important to be able to use analogies: in general, much of the design of new processes relies on analogies — most hydrogenation processes have similar features, most tanks of liquefied gases have similarities, etc. For that reason the database also includes information on the type of material in the incident (e.g. liquefied gas), the type of reaction (e.g. oxidation), the thermal nature of the reactor (e.g. exothermic), the phase of the reaction and the type of catalyst.

To learn from the accident cases it is essential to indicate the type of incident which happened (e.g. explosion), the direct cause of the incident (e.g. static electricity), the reason why this could take place (e.g. filling through the gas phase) and finally — most important — the lesson of how this can be avoided (e.g. fill the tank through the bottom).

11.5.4 Inherent Safety Index of Safe Process Structure 11.5.4 Chỉ số an toàn nội tại của cấu trúc quá trình an toàn

Process structures are divided into six groups of scores from 0 to 5 according to the knowledge of their safety behaviour in operation. The first group is the safest (score 0): recommended and standardized process and equipment solutions. The second group is based on sound engineering practice — the use of well-known and reliable process alternatives. The third group contains processes which look neutral, or on which no safety data is available. The fourth group includes configurations which are probably questionable on the basis of safety, even though accidents have not yet occurred. The fifth and sixth groups contain process cases on which documented minor or major accident cases exist, respectively. The final score of the subindex is chosen on the basis of the worst case of the different levels of the reasoning. The results can be used with the other subindices for estimating the total inherent safety of process alternatives, for the selection of the process concept or the details of the process configuration. Details of the Safe Process Structure Subindex are given by Heikkilä et al. (1998).
