What is a process?

A process is any coordinated sequence of operations that turns inputs into a more valuable output. Place that idea in the economy — where primary industries pull ore and raw materials out of the ground, secondary industries forge them into products, and tertiary industries provide the services around them — and you see exactly where process control, instrumentation, IT/OT and AI earn their keep.

01What is a process?

A process turns inputs into something more valuable

A process is a coordinated set of activities, machines, materials and people that converts inputs into outputs of greater value. The inputs are raw materials, energy, information and labour; the outputs are products, by-products and services. A refinery turning crude oil into fuel, a furnace turning ore into steel, a dairy pasteurising milk, and a plant bottling water are all processes.

Inputs

Raw materials, energy, information and labour that enter the process.

Transformation

Physical, chemical or biological operations that change the material.

Outputs

Products, by-products and services of greater value than the inputs.

Two families are worth separating. Discrete manufacturing makes countable units you can point at — cars, phones, bearings — assembled from parts. Process manufacturing makes things you measure by mass, volume, flow or energy — chemicals, fuels, metals, foods, pharmaceuticals, cement, paper. This field guide is about the second family: the process industries, where the product is a stream rather than a stack of parts.

Within process manufacturing, continuous processes run without stop — a refinery or a power plant — while batch processes make a defined quantity then clean and reset — a pharmaceutical campaign or a food recipe. Both live or die on measurement and control.

02Three sectors of an economy

Where does a "process" live in the economy?

Economists sort production into sectors. The classic three-sector model puts extraction first, manufacturing second, and services third. The conversion steps — where raw material becomes product — sit almost entirely in the second sector, and that is where process control is born.

Beyond three

Some models add a quaternary sector (research, development, data and knowledge work) and a quinary sector (non-profit and government). Automation, AI and most of this site's IT/OT story sit in the quaternary layer that increasingly cuts across all three.

03From ore to product

Follow one gram of iron

Take steel. Iron ore is dug from the ground (primary). It is crushed and concentrated, then smelted in a blast furnace and refined into molten iron and steel (secondary). The steel is cast and rolled into sheet and coil, stamped into body panels, and assembled into a vehicle (still secondary, now discrete). The finished car is then sold, financed, insured, fuelled and serviced (tertiary). Every conversion step between the mine and the showroom is a process — and each one has to be measured and held under control.

The same shape repeats everywhere. Bauxite becomes alumina, then aluminium, then foil and cans. Crude oil becomes fuels and plastics. Milk becomes yoghurt; grain becomes flour and bread. In every case the value is created in the secondary conversion, and the tertiary sector is what delivers it to the people who use it.

04Why the secondary sector needs control

Without instruments you are blind; without control you are passive

A modern process runs at pressures, temperatures and chemistries that are hostile to people and to the equipment itself. It moves tonnes per minute and burns energy by the megawatt. The margin between a good day and a hazardous, wasteful one is held by two disciplines working together: instrumentation (knowing what the process is doing) and process control (making it do what you want).

  • Consistency & quality at scale A process that drifts makes off-spec product by the trainload. Control holds every batch to the same setpoint.
  • Safety Flammable, toxic and pressurised material demands that limits are respected automatically, not by luck.
  • Efficiency & yield Every percent of raw material or energy saved at scale is real money and real carbon.
  • Compliance Environmental, safety and product standards are enforced with auditable records that only an instrumented process can produce.
  • Economics Margins in process industries are thin; control protects them against upset, waste and unplanned shutdown.
05Instrumentation: the senses

You cannot control what you cannot measure

Instrumentation is the process's senses. A sensor detects a physical quantity — temperature, pressure, flow, level, composition, position — and a transmitter turns it into a signal the system can read, traditionally 4–20 mA, increasingly digital fieldbus. That signal feeds controllers, recorders and historians. Without it, the operator is guessing.

06Process control: the reflexes

Measure, compare, act, repeat

Control closes the loop. The system measures the process value (PV), compares it to the target setpoint (SP), computes the error, and drives an actuator — a valve, pump or drive — to correct it. A PID controller does this continuously; feedback corrects what already happened, feedforward anticipates what is about to. DCS, PLC and SCADA are the execution layer that runs these loops across a whole plant.

07IT and OT: two worlds, one plant

The line between the office and the furnace is dissolving

Operational Technology (OT) is the hardware and software that senses and changes the physical world — sensors, actuators, PLCs, DCS, SCADA and safety systems, living close to the process. Information Technology (IT) is what computes, stores, networks and runs the business — servers, databases, ERP, planning and analytics. For decades they hardly spoke. Now data flows up from OT to IT for visibility, and optimised setpoints flow back down — the two worlds converge.

That convergence is mapped by the ISA-95 / Purdue model: enterprise systems on top (IT), manufacturing execution in the middle, and supervisory, control and field layers below (OT). The benefit is visibility and optimisation across the whole plant. The cost is a broader attack surface — so segmentation, least privilege and patching matter as much as the control loops themselves.

08Business intelligence

Turning the flood of process data into decisions

An instrumented plant produces a deluge of time-series data — every tag, every second. A historian stores it; business intelligence turns it into meaning. Dashboards and KPIs — overall equipment effectiveness, yield, energy intensity, throughput, quality and emissions — let managers see a plant the way an operator sees a loop. Trend analysis finds slow drift before it becomes a failure; benchmarking spreads the best unit's practice to the rest.

BI is the bridge between OT and the business. It pulls production, inventory and cost from the plant floor up into ERP, and moves planning back down. The maturity path runs from reactive (answering alarms) to predictive (reading trends) to prescriptive (recommending the move) — and AI is what accelerates the last two steps.

09AI in the loop

Intelligence is moving from the dashboard to the actuator

Artificial intelligence earns its place where patterns are too subtle or too numerous for a fixed rule. On a process plant that means predictive maintenance from vibration and temperature history, soft sensors that estimate variables too expensive to measure directly, anomaly and fault detection, setpoint and recipe optimisation, production scheduling, quality prediction, and increasingly autonomous advanced control built on model predictive control and reinforcement learning.

Keep safety independent

AI advises and optimises; it must not become the last line of defence. A Safety Instrumented System (SIS) stays separate, independent and certified to its SIL — the model can suggest, the SIS still protects. Trust also depends on data quality, validation before deployment, and explainability when a recommendation sends an actuator.
