The loop that is supposed to do nothing

A control loop earns its keep every second. A safety loop earns its keep once, on the worst day of the plant's life, and is judged entirely on that one moment. Everything about how it is specified follows from that asymmetry.

01The loop that sleeps

Two systems on the same pipe, optimised for opposite things

The basic process control system exists to keep the plant running. The safety instrumented system exists to stop it. They share a process connection and nothing else — and the moment they share more than that, the safety function inherits the control system's failure modes.

The word that does the damage

“Redundant” is not a synonym for “independent”. Two transmitters on one impulse line are redundant and not independent. One transmitter on two I/O cards is neither. Independence is a property of the whole path — sensor, cable, card, power, software, and the person who configured it.

02What SIL bounds

A number attached to a function, not to a device

SIL is the risk reduction a safety function must deliver, expressed as the probability that it fails to act when demanded. Low demand means the function is asked to work less than once a year — which is the case for almost every process trip.

Read the table as a budget, not a badge. A SIL 2 function may fail to act once in a hundred demands; whether that is acceptable depends on what happens next, which is a question for the risk assessment, not for the instrument engineer alone.

The most expensive misunderstanding in the discipline

A SIL 3 transmitter does not make a SIL 3 loop. The SIL belongs to the function, which is the sensor, the logic solver, the final element and the proof test regime taken together. The final element is usually the weakest of the four — a shutdown valve that has never been stroked is a SIL 3 device with an unknown PFD.

61508 is the umbrella; the standard you cite depends on the machine

IEC 61508 is the parent document for electrical, electronic and programmable-electronic safety systems. Part 1 sets the requirements for the safety lifecycle from concept through decommissioning; parts 2 and 3 prescribe measures against systematic hardware and software faults. Everything below it narrows the scope. For process plant that is IEC 61511 — the standard this page is mostly written against. For machinery it is IEC 62061 and ISO 13849, and in practice ISO 13849 is the one most machine builders actually work to.

Product standards sit below those, and the rule is that the most specific applicable standard takes precedence. IEC 61131-6 covers programmable controllers used in safety functions; ISO 13850 covers emergency stop. So a controller in a SIL 2 machine is not “61508-certified” in some general sense — it is assessed against 61131-6 for the safety functions it actually implements. That distinction matters at the procurement stage, where a certificate is often quoted as if it settled the question.

Security is now a legal requirement

One thing has changed since this page was first written. IEC 61508 touches on security but makes no concrete specification for it, and a networked plant brings attack paths that end in the same physical place as a failed safety function. NIS2 and the Cyber Resilience Act have turned a security architecture from an accessory into an obligation. The two disciplines remain different — safety is about unintended failure, security about intended interference — but functional safety in a networked plant now depends on a level of security that 61508 never demanded, and a SIL claim that ignores it is a claim about a plant that no longer exists.

03Where integrity comes from

Redundancy buys orders of magnitude, until β sets the floor

A single device fails dangerously at a rate λDU per hour and is only tested every TI hours, so on average it spends half the interval failed and undetected. Redundancy attacks that term — but two devices that share a diaphragm, a power supply or an engineer can fail together, and that fraction β cannot be voted away.

One device, tested once per interval:
PFDavg(1oo1) = λDU × TI / 2
Shared causes are not voted away:
PFDfloor = β × λDU × TI / 2
Redundancy only shrinks the (1 − β) part.

Worked example

Take λDU = 5 × 10-7 per hour and a one-year proof test interval of 8 760 hours. A single device gives PFD = 5 × 10-7 × 8 760 / 2 = 2.2 × 10-3, which is SIL 2.

Duplicate it with β = 0.1 and the floor is 0.1 × 2.2 × 10-3 = 2.2 × 10-4 — SIL 3, and no further redundancy helps. Raise β to 0.2 and the floor doubles to 4.4 × 10-4. The second device bought one level; the shared power supply took it back.

04The proof test

The interval is the term you actually control

PFD is linear in the proof test interval. Nobody can change the device's failure rate once it is on the pipe, but anybody can decide how often it gets tested — and that decision moves the number just as far.

IntervalPFD relative to one yearWhat it really costs
6 months0.5×Two shutdowns a year, twice the paperwork.
1 year1×The default in most specifications, for no better reason than the turnaround.
2 years2×Twice the risk, half the labour. Defensible only with high proof test coverage.
5 years5×Five times the risk for identical hardware. The devices did not get worse; the test interval did.

Coverage decides what the interval is worth

A proof test does not test the function; it tests the part of the function the test reaches. That fraction is the proof test coverage, and whatever it misses behaves as if it had never been tested at all.

For a shutdown valve, a test that injects a signal at the logic solver and watches the input card change state proves the wiring and nothing else. The seat, the stem, the spring return and the actuator — where most dangerous failures actually live — are untouched. A partial stroke test is better and still incomplete: it exercises the movement but not the closure.

05Independence

A wiring diagram, not an intention

IEC 61511 requires the safety function to be independent of the control function. The requirement is easy to state, cheap to draw, and routinely defeated by a shared terminal block that nobody documented.

Left: one transmitter, two consumers — the safety function inherits every failure of the control loop. Right: separate devices, separate paths, independence you can point at.

Five things that are shared without anyone deciding to share them

Each of these appears on a real project as a cost saving. Each of them reduces β resistance to zero and quietly converts a redundant architecture into a single point of failure.

The transmitter

One device feeds both the BPCS and the SIS. One diaphragm, one electronics stack, one failure mode — and the SIS learns nothing the BPCS did not already know.

The impulse line

Two transmitters, one tapping point. A plugged line blinds both, and both read a frozen value that looks entirely plausible.

The power supply

A single 24 V feed, or a single UPS, takes both systems down together. Independence has to reach the source.

The I/O card

Two channels on the same card share a multiplexer, a reference and a power rail. Two channels is not two paths.

The engineer

The same person configures the BPCS and the SIS, using the same laptop, from the same backup. The most common cause of common cause.

06How they fail

Six ways a safety system stops being one

Almost none of these are device failures. The hardware is usually fine. What fails is the regime around it — the register, the test, the setpoint, the assumption that the calculation still describes the plant.

The bypass that stayed in

A trip is inhibited for a two-day maintenance job. The job finishes, the bypass does not. Nobody owns the register, so the SIS is off for a year and every document still says SIL 2.

The plugged impulse line

The transmitter is healthy, the signal is in range, the diagnostics report nothing. It is reading the pressure from last Tuesday. A frozen value is indistinguishable from a steady process.

The valve that never moved

The proof test injects a signal at the logic solver and watches the input card respond. The valve is never stroked. The seat, the stem and the actuator — where most dangerous failures live — are untested.

The trip nobody wanted

A poorly chosen setpoint produces three spurious trips in commissioning. Operations asks for it to be disabled. The SIS was technically correct and practically deleted.

The setpoint above the relief valve

The SIF trips at a pressure the relief valve already handles, so it never acts. It passes every proof test because the test injects a signal, not a process condition.

The calculation that was never repeated

The loop was re-instrumented, the transmitter changed, the proof test interval doubled for a turnaround. The PFD calculation still reflects the original design and still says SIL 2.

07Not a trigger value

Why a monitoring threshold is not a safety function

The comparison page argues that geotechnical monitoring leaves the loop open on purpose. The same argument explains why a trigger value cannot be handed a SIL: there is no actuator to make the function act, so there is nothing whose failure probability could be calculated.

Where the two do meet

A geotechnical measurement can still be part of a safety function — a piezometer that trips a dewatering pump, a slope extensometer that closes a pipeline valve. In that case the sensor is one link in a function that does have an actuator, and the function can carry a SIL. What cannot carry a SIL is the measurement alone, or the threshold on a dashboard.

If you take one thing from this page

Ask for the proof test procedure before you accept the SIL claim. The certificate describes a device on a bench; the procedure describes what will actually happen on your plant, at three in the morning, by the technician who is on shift.
