Two disciplines, one word

Both put instruments on a structure and read numbers. One of them then does something about it, automatically and continuously. The other writes a report. That difference is not cultural — it follows from whether the system has a controllable input.

02Why the loop stays open

Four conditions for closing a loop

Feedback control is only possible when four conditions hold at once. A geotechnical structure typically satisfies the first two and fails the third — and that failure is structural, not a matter of better instruments.

The fourth condition is the one engineers forget. Feedback needs a response that is fast relative to how often you can act. If the ground takes three weeks to respond to a change in excavation rate, a controller correcting every second is not controlling anything — it is guessing, and it will be wrong for three weeks.

The useful exception

When the geotechnical variable is slow but the actuator is fast — a dewatering pump, a pressure relief gate, a pipeline block valve — the loop can genuinely close. Those are the hybrid cases in the next section.

Four tests

Can this be controlled?

1. Observable
The variable can be measured fast enough to matter.
2. Controllable
An input exists that changes the variable.
3. Authority
The actuator can move the variable against the expected disturbance.
4. Stability
The response is monotonic and fast relative to the correction interval.
03Where the two meet

Six places where a monitoring signal becomes a control signal

Real projects rarely respect the boundary. In each of these, a geotechnical measurement drives a process action — sometimes automatically, sometimes through a procedure.

Dewatering control

Piezometers in the excavation read pore pressure; the pump speed follows it. This is a genuine closed loop with a geotechnical sensor and a process actuator — the clearest hybrid in practice.

Tailings storage facility

The decant system is process control; the phreatic surface and beach width are geotechnical monitoring. A rising phreatic line should change the operating setpoint — usually it changes a monthly report instead.

Dam and levee

Seepage weirs and piezometers detect internal erosion; relief valves and gates control the water level. Instrumentation on both sides of the same structure, usually specified by different teams in different formats.

Pipeline geohazard

Slope movement or strain monitoring on a buried pipeline can trigger a block valve or a pressure reduction automatically. Slow loop, real actuator, real consequence.

Tunnel boring

Face pressure and slurry density are controlled in seconds — pure process control. Surface settlement is measured in days — pure geotechnical monitoring. The two must be read together, and usually are not.

Structural health monitoring

Strain, tilt and acceleration on a bridge or wind turbine. Continuous data, automated alarms — monitoring in form, but with the data rate of control. The missing piece is still the actuator.

04Vocabulary traps

Six words that mean different things

Most coordination failures between the two disciplines are linguistic. The same word appears in both specifications with different definitions, and nobody notices until commissioning.

WordIn process I&C it meansIn geotechnical monitoring it means
InstrumentA transmitter with a standardised output you can wire into a control system.A sensor plus a readout, often vibrating wire, read on a campaign basis.
AccuracyPercent of span, under reference conditions, traceable.Percent of full scale, dominated by installation and grout, rarely traceable.

AlarmAn ISA-18.2 rationalised alarm with a defined response time.A threshold notification in a three-tier trigger-action plan.

RangeThe 4–20 mA span, set by the configured LRV and URV.The physical measurement range of the sensor, fixed at manufacture.
05What this means in practice

Six rules that follow from the matrix

These are the mistakes that appear when the two disciplines are mixed without noticing the difference.

Do not put a process transmitter in a borehole

A DP transmitter needs a clean impulse line and occasional access. Grouted into a borehole it will drift, then fail, and you will not be able to remove it.

Do not put a vibrating-wire sensor in a fast loop

VW sensors are stable, not fast. Their reading interval is seconds to minutes; a control loop that needs 100 ms cannot use one.

Specify stability, not just accuracy

In a buried sensor, drift over five years matters more than a tenth of a percent on day one. Ask for the long-term stability figure and the temperature coefficient.

Assume some sensors will be lost

Install spares in the same borehole, or a second method that measures the same quantity differently. Redundancy in monitoring means independence, not duplication.

Cable is the weakest link

Lightning, rodents, excavation and UV destroy more geotechnical data than sensor failure. Budget for surge protection and route discipline, not just instruments.

Write the trigger-action plan first

A threshold without an action is decoration. Decide who does what, at which value, within which time — before the first sensor is installed.

The expensive mistake

Specifying control-grade hardware for a monitoring job, or monitoring-grade hardware for a control job, produces a system that works on paper and fails in service. The first is wasteful; the second is dangerous.
