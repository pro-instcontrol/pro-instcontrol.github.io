The loop decides

A controller is a small piece of arithmetic with a large responsibility: it compares what is happening with what should be happening and moves something. Everything difficult about control comes from the gap between those two — and from how long the process takes to notice.

01Anatomy
Single-input single-output feedback loop. e = SP − PV; u drives the final element.

Error is the only input

The controller never sees the process — only the difference between the setpoint and the measurement. Every tuning problem is a problem of interpreting that difference.

The valve is part of the loop

A sticky valve, an oversized valve or a positioner with a 3 % dead band will destroy the performance of a perfectly tuned controller. Tune the valve first.

Feedback is always late

The correction arrives after the process has already moved. That delay, not the algorithm, sets the achievable performance — which is why the next two sections are about time.

02Three terms

Proportional, integral, derivative — and what each one costs

Ninety percent of industrial loops run PI. Derivative is useful on slow, smooth processes and actively harmful on noisy ones.

TermActionTurn it upToo much
P — proportional gainOutput proportional to the current error.Faster response, smaller offset, tighter control.Overshoot, oscillation, eventual instability.
I — integral timeAccumulates error over time until it disappears. Note: lower Ti means more integral action.Offset driven to zero at steady state.Windup during saturation, slow oscillation, hunting.
D — derivative timeReacts to the rate of change, anticipating where the error is going.Damping, less overshoot, faster settling.Amplifies sensor noise, wears the valve, hides in the scan time.
Integral windup

When the output saturates — valve fully open — the integral term keeps accumulating error it cannot act on. When the process finally responds, the controller is still carrying that history and overshoots badly. Use anti-windup: stop integrating, or back-calculate from the actual valve position.

04Dead time

The one number that limits everything

Dead time (transport delay) is the interval between moving the valve and seeing any change at all. It is not lag — lag is the process easing towards a new value; dead time is nothing happening. A controller cannot distinguish “no response yet” from “no response”, so it keeps pushing.

The controllability ratio L/T — dead time over the dominant time constant — predicts how well PID can work before you touch a single tuning parameter. Where does dead time come from? Transport in a pipe, analyser sample lines, a slow-responding sensor in a thermowell, a scan time that is too coarse, or damping applied to hide noise.

The engineering response is rarely “tune harder”. It is to remove the delay: move the analyser closer, use a faster sensor, cut the damping, shorten the sample line. Every minute spent on the physical delay is worth an hour of tuning.

Rule

If the dead time is longer than the time constant, PID is the wrong tool — use feedforward, dead-time compensation, or change the process.

The other clamp: noise, and setpoint gap

Dead time caps how fast a loop can be. Noise caps how hard. Both are properties of the process and neither responds to tuning, which is why they are worth separating from the six methods above. A worked example from Ed Bullerdiek's PID spotlight series in Control Engineering: a fast self-limiting loop whose process variable wanders in a band of roughly ±13% around its average, with noise that is neither white nor cleanly cyclic. A 6-second filter — the most the series recommends for a loop that still has to be aggressive — brought the visible band down to about ±5%. That is a little over 60% of the noise removed, not the 70% a white-noise calculation would promise, precisely because the noise is not white.

Then the part that surprises people: a PID controller cannot reduce process noise. A correctly tuned one will not make it worse — that is the whole test — but it cannot make it better either. The output band follows the PV band multiplied by the gain: at K = 0.5 against a ±5% band, the valve strokes ±2.5%, continuously, forever. That is real wear on a real stem, and it buys nothing, because the movement is not correcting anything.

Setpoint gap is the standard way out. Set a gap at the noise limits — ±5% here — and drop the gap gain to zero inside it, so the controller stops moving the output while the PV wanders in a band it has no authority over anyway. The output then moves only for a real setpoint change or a real disturbance. The cost is explicit and should be stated in the design: every performance measure gets worse, because you have deliberately stopped correcting. Which gap algorithm to use — notch, V-notch, floating or error-squared — depends on whether your controller works in positional or velocity form; pairing them wrongly is the usual reason gap control “does not work”.

An accepted loss

This is the one place on the page where accepting worse control is the correct engineering decision, because the alternative is stem wear and a controller chasing a signal it cannot influence. The simulator below has no noise source, so it cannot show the gap directly — but it shows the same trade from the other side: raise the dead time and watch how much gain the loop will still take before it oscillates. Noise removes gain the way dead time removes speed, and neither gives it back.

05Beyond the single loop

When one loop is not enough

Most real plants are coupled: the flow loop changes the pressure, which changes the temperature, which changes the composition. These structures exist to break that coupling deliberately.

Cascade

A slow outer loop (temperature) writes the setpoint of a fast inner loop (flow). The inner loop absorbs valve and pressure disturbances before the outer loop sees them.

Feedforward

Measure the disturbance and act before the error appears. Requires a measurable disturbance and a rough model — the classic example is boiler feedwater on steam flow.

Ratio

One stream follows another at a fixed ratio — reagent to feed, dilution water to product. Often the ratio itself is trimmed by a slow composition analyser.

Split range

One controller drives two valves in sequence: the small one for fine control, the large one for capacity. Common on pressure letdown and compressor anti-surge.

Override / selective

Several controllers share one final element; a selector passes the safest signal. Used for compressor surge control and fired-heater constraints.

MPC

A model predicts the future over a horizon and optimises the moves, respecting constraints. Worth it when there are many variables, long dead times and real economic value in pushing to a limit.

What to watch for

Set derivative to zero first — most loops do not need it. Then raise gain until the response is just short of overshooting, and add integral action only until the offset is gone. If the loop becomes unstable when you raise the dead time, no tuning will fix it: the process is telling you the delay must be removed.

07Platforms & safety

Four platforms, one boundary

The choice is about scale, redundancy and engineering workflow — not about which box is more modern. IEC 61131-3 defines the programming languages that all four implement.

Keep the SIS separate

IEC 61511 requires the safety instrumented system to be independent of the basic process control system: separate logic solver, separate wiring, separate final elements where practicable. Sharing a valve between control and shutdown is one of the most common and most serious design errors — the controller can drive the valve to the unsafe position just before the trip needs it.

Portability: what IEC 61131-3 never standardised

The table above says all four platforms implement IEC 61131-3, and that standard did something real: it fixed the five languages — ladder diagram, structured text, function block diagram, sequential function chart, instruction list — so a programmer could move between vendors without relearning the notation. What it did not fix is the file. Every engineering environment kept its own project format, so a project stayed tied to one vendor's tool. Daniel Hollman makes the consequence concrete in Control Engineering: machine builders redeveloped applications whenever a customer specified a different control system, recreating tag structures, program logic and hardware configuration by hand.

IEC 61131-10, published in 2019, closes that gap with an XML exchange format built on PLCopen XML, which has existed since 2005. The difference between the two is worth keeping straight, because they are often used as synonyms. PLCopen XML was designed to exchange program content: program organisation units, variables, data types and graphical representations. IEC 61131-10 goes further and represents tasks, configurations, resources and project organisation as well. One is a way to move code; the other is a way to move a project.

Not a conversion button

Software transfers well: logic, reusable code, data structures. Hardware does not. Vendor libraries, hardware configuration, safety functions and specialised motion implementations all need engineering on the receiving side, and how much interoperability you actually get depends on what both environments support. Read 61131-10 as a way to stop redeveloping the parts that never needed redeveloping — an interoperable framework, not an automatic migration.

The tag layer is the technical debt nobody budgets

The same lesson shows up one layer up. Asked what legacy HMI/SCADA systems most often overlook, the instructors of Control Engineering's integration webcast named the tag architecture — scanning, naming, descriptions, scaling, alarming, data types, deadbands, historical deadbands, point grouping, which alarms belong together. It is taken for granted precisely because it is invisible, and it is usually the most obsolete material in the system: artefacts carried along for years because nobody had a reason to open them. The second surprise comes halfway through a migration, when somebody finds the specialised scripts and calculations buried in the HMI. They are rarely at the forefront, which is exactly why they are missed in the estimate.

The rule that survives

On naming, the instructors were blunt: the best convention is the one you will actually follow and back with a procedure or a standard. Every scheme has trade-offs; having no scheme is the only option with no upside. On the screens themselves, ISA-101 is the baseline — it is the difference between a display that is situationally aware and a wall where everything is equally important, which means nothing is.
