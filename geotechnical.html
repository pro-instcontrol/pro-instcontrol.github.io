Measuring the ground without moving it

Geotechnical instrumentation answers a different question from process instrumentation. Not “what is the value now?” but “is this structure behaving as we predicted?” — a question with a baseline, a rate, and a consequence.

01Why monitor

The observational method, in four requirements

EN 1997-1 §2.7 allows a design to be adjusted during construction, but only if four things exist before work starts: acceptable limits of behaviour, a prediction of what will happen, a monitoring programme that can detect the difference, and a response prepared in advance for when it does.

Notice what is missing from that list: instruments are the third item. The first two are engineering judgement, and the fourth is a management decision. A monitoring system without a threshold and a response is data collection, not risk management.

The rule that matters

Rate of change catches failure earlier than absolute value. A wall moving 2 mm/month is a different object from a wall that moved 2 mm and stopped — even though both read 2 mm.

This is why every serious monitoring programme reports velocity as well as displacement, and why the trigger values are usually written as both a magnitude and a rate.

Four requirements

What the standard asks

1. Limits
Acceptable limits of behaviour, agreed before construction.
2. Prediction
A range of behaviour, from the design model.
3. Monitoring
A programme able to detect deviation from the prediction.
4. Response
A pre-planned action if the limits are approached.

04Why vibrating wire

Frequency beats current when the cable is long

A vibrating-wire sensor outputs a frequency, not a voltage. Cable resistance, connector corrosion and junction resistance barely affect a frequency measurement — which is why the technology survives in boreholes where a 4–20 mA transmitter would drift within a year.

The cost is speed. The wire must be plucked and allowed to settle before the frequency can be counted, so a reading takes a second or more. That is fine for a slope and useless for a control loop.

The reading is Hz²

Most vibrating-wire sensors are calibrated as pressure or displacement against frequency squared, with a polynomial that includes a temperature term from the built-in thermistor. Skipping the temperature correction is the most common source of a slow, believable drift.

05Field reality

Six ways a monitoring system fails

Almost none of them are the sensor. In practice the failure is mechanical, electrical or procedural — and it usually happens quietly.

Lightning

A long cable in an open site is an antenna. One strike takes out the logger and every sensor on the segment. Surge protection and a single-point earth are not optional.

Grout and borehole quality

A piezometer surrounded by a poor grout seal measures the grout, not the ground. The error is systematic, stable, and invisible in the data.

Cable damage

Excavators, rodents, UV and settlement. Route cables in conduit, leave slack loops at settlement joints, and record the route while it is still visible.

Reference instability

Every relative measurement needs a fixed reference. If the reference head settles, the whole profile shifts and the movement looks real.

Silent data loss

A logger that stops reporting is obvious. A logger reporting the last value repeatedly is not. Add a heartbeat, a timestamp check and a rate-of-change sanity test.

Nobody reads it

The most common failure of all. If the dashboard has no named owner and no review cadence, the data is a cost, not a control.
