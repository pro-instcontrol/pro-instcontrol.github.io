The datasheet describes a device on a bench

Every accuracy figure you were quoted was measured with a clean pressure source, a short connection and a stable ambient temperature. On the plant you inherit a tapping point somebody chose at 4 p.m. on a Friday, forty metres of impulse line, and a shield grounded at both ends. This page is about the gap between the two.

01Where the number is decided

Half the error is installed, not manufactured

A pressure transmitter does not measure pressure; it measures the pressure at the end of its impulse line. A temperature sensor does not measure the fluid; it measures its own sheath, which is trying to reach the fluid. The installation is the measurement.

The rule is the same in both cases: slope the line so that whatever should not be there returns to the pipe. Gas service keeps condensate out; liquid service keeps gas out.

Tapping point rules by service

ServiceTap positionImpulse lineWhat you are avoiding
Gas and airTop of the pipe, never the bottom.Downward to the transmitter, so condensate runs back.A liquid slug sitting on the diaphragm.
LiquidSide of the pipe, not the bottom where solids settle.Upward to the transmitter, so gas vents back.A gas pocket that never fills.

Short is a specification, not a preference

Every metre of impulse line adds lag and adds a place for the process to settle. A DP flow loop with a thirty-metre line answers in tens of seconds and cannot be tuned for a fast disturbance, however good the transmitter. Specify the run length, then design the layout to meet it — not the other way round.

02Hazardous area

Four questions, in this order

A hazardous area classification is not a single label. It is a zone, a gas group, a temperature class and a protection concept, and a device is only suitable when all four agree. Reading a nameplate is the easy part; knowing which four things you are reading is the part that gets missed.

TermWhat it tells you
Zone 0An explosive atmosphere is present continuously, or for more than 1 000 hours a year.
Zone 1Likely to occur in normal operation — roughly 10 to 1 000 hours a year.
Zone 2Unlikely, and only for short periods — under 10 hours a year.
Group IIAPropane. The least easily ignited of the surface gas groups.
Group IIBEthylene. The middle group, and where most refinery hydrogen-free service sits.
Group IICHydrogen and acetylene. The most easily ignited — and the group that shrinks the allowable cable length.
T1 to T6Maximum surface temperature: 450, 300, 200, 135, 100 and 85 °C.
Ex iaIntrinsically safe, remains safe with two faults. The only concept that permits Zone 0.
Ex ibIntrinsically safe with one fault. Permits Zone 1.
Ex dFlameproof enclosure — it lets the explosion happen inside and does not let it out.
Ex eIncreased safety — no sparks or hot surfaces in normal operation.
Ex nNon-sparking, for Zone 2 only.
The mistake that survives inspection

A device rated for a lower gas group is not made safe by installing it carefully. IIC equipment works in IIA service; IIA equipment in IIC service is a hazard that no amount of correct wiring fixes. The same applies to temperature class: a T4 device in a T6 requirement is a hot surface with a certificate that does not cover it.

03Intrinsic safety

The entity concept: match the numbers, then the cable

Intrinsic safety works by limiting the energy that can reach the hazardous area, so that no spark and no hot surface can ignite the atmosphere. It is the only protection concept that allows work on a live circuit, and the only one that permits Zone 0.

The barrier output must not exceed the device input:
Uo ≤ Ui    Io ≤ Ii    Po ≤ Pi
And the cable has to be counted, not assumed:
Co ≥ Ci + Ccable     Lo ≥ Li + Lcable
The cable is part of the circuit. Half of all IS mistakes are made here.

Simple apparatus

An RTD, a thermocouple, a switch or a junction box stores almost no energy, so it needs no certificate of its own — it is defined as simple apparatus. It still has a capacitance and an inductance, they are just small enough that nobody writes them down. On a long cable run with a tight inductance limit, that assumption is worth checking.

The same applies to the transmitter: Ci and Li are on its certificate, usually in nanofarads and microhenries, and they are subtracted from what the cable is allowed to use.

Worked example

A 4–20 mA loop in IIC service

Transmitter
Ci = 5 nF, Li = 0
Barrier, IIC
Co = 100 nF, Lo = 1 mH
Cable
120 pF/m, 1.0 µH/m
By capacitance
(100 − 5) nF / 0.12 nF/m = 792 m
By inductance
1 000 µH / 1.0 µH/m = 1 000 m
04Grounding and shielding

A shield is an antenna until you decide which end is ground

Shielding and grounding are the same problem seen twice. The shield must intercept the interference and carry it away; the signal reference must be a single point, or the difference between two points becomes a signal of its own.

Ground the shield at one end

Normally the control-room end. Grounding both ends creates a loop, and a loop in a varying magnetic field carries a current — the shield then injects the noise it was installed to remove.

One reference point per loop

A 4–20 mA loop should be earthed in exactly one place. Two earths at different potentials put a current through the signal return that no filter will remove.

Isolate rather than bond

Where two earths cannot be made equal — separate buildings, an earthed tank, a remote structure — a galvanic isolator breaks the loop instead of trying to win the argument.

Never use the shield as a return

The shield is not a conductor. Using it as a signal return puts the entire interference current of the cable in series with your measurement.

Separate power and signal

Run signal cable in its own tray. Where it must cross power cable, cross at ninety degrees; parallel runs couple capacitance, and long parallel runs couple a lot of it.

Bond before you improve the earth

Most noise problems are potential differences between two earthed points, not a bad rod. Bonding the structures together removes the difference; driving a deeper rod usually just moves it.

05Commissioning

Six checks that catch what the datasheet cannot

Commissioning is not a formality between installation and handover. It is the only point where the installed system is tested as a system, and the last point where a mistake is cheap.

CheckWhat it provesHow to do it properly
Loop checkThe right sensor reaches the right point in the system.Inject at the sensor, read at the operator screen, and confirm the tag — not just the value.
Five-point calibrationLinearity and hysteresis, up and down.0, 25, 50, 75 and 100 per cent, ascending then descending. A one-direction check hides hysteresis entirely.
As-found and as-leftWhether the device drifted, and by how much.Record before you adjust. Without an as-found record there is no trend, and no argument with the vendor.

06How installations fail

Six failures that pass every inspection

None of these leaves a mark on a certificate. Each of them produces a number that looks like a measurement, which is worse than producing no number at all.

The tapping point in the wrong place

A pressure tap two diameters downstream of a bend sees a distorted profile; a temperature sensor in a dead leg sees the pipe wall, not the fluid. The device is accurate and the number is wrong.

The impulse line that plugged

Process fluid freezes, polymerises or settles in a line that is too long, too narrow, or has a dead leg. The transmitter keeps reading the last pressure it saw, with no diagnostic to say so.

The shield grounded at both ends

A shield earthed at both ends is a loop antenna with a current in it. The noise it injects is often worse than the noise it was fitted to reject.

The wet leg that boiled

A wet leg on a hot vessel loses its reference column and the level reads high, always high, and plausibly. Nobody notices until the vessel overfills.

The IS calculation that ignored the cable

The barrier and the transmitter match; the cable was never entered into the calculation. In IIC service a few hundred metres of ordinary cable can exceed the allowable inductance.

The loop check that tested the wrong tag

Two transmitters were swapped at the marshalling cabinet during a modification. Every check passes because every check confirms the signal arrives — not that it arrives from the right place.

The cheapest hour in the project

Walk the installation before the insulation is closed and the trays are covered. An afternoon with a drawing, a tape measure and the person who will maintain the loop finds more than a week of troubleshooting after start-up — and it is the only review where the fix is a bracket rather than a shutdown.
