01Why these industries

Safety and environmental compliance sit first for a reason: a smooth, optimised rate is worth nothing if the plant is on fire or the neighbourhood is contaminated. Every application below is an exercise in balancing all five.

02Boiler control

Four interlocking loops

An ISA boiler-control seminar reduces the whole problem to four basic control functions: furnace draft, drum-level feedwater, fuel-air (combustion) and steam temperature. Fuels may be gas, oil, coal or a combination — the loops are the same.

LoopWhat it doesTypical instruments
Drum level (feedwater)Three-element control (level + steam flow + feed flow) replaces single-element on variable load; corrects swell and shrink.LT on drum, FT on steam and feed lines
Combustion (fuel / air)Master pressure controller sets firing rate; cross-limiting keeps air ahead on increase, fuel ahead on decrease; O2 trim trims excess air.FT on fuel & air, ZrO2 O2 analyser, FD/ID fan dampers
Steam temperatureDesuperheater sprays water into superheated steam; single-element spray control holds superheat.TT on superheater outlet, spray control valve
Furnace draftMeasured on 2oo3 transmitters per ISA-77.41; ID-fan damper holds a slight negative pressure.3 pressure transmitters, ID fan damper

From one element to three

A single-element controller (drum level only) is acceptable on small, steady boilers. On variable load it is fooled by swell and shrink — a sudden load increase makes the level momentarily rise (swell) as bubbles expand, then fall. Adding steam flow gives a two-element loop that anticipates the swell; adding feedwater flow gives the three-element loop that also rejects disturbances on the feed side. Drum pressure is used to compensate level for the density change with pressure.

Three-element feedwater: level, steam flow and feed flow into one controller.

Reliability by design

ISA-77.41 calls for redundant furnace-pressure transmitters — two with deviation alarm and manual transfer, or three with a transmitter-select (2oo3) scheme. Symbols follow ANSI/ISA-5.1.

03Power-plant control & instrumentation

One integrated, interdependent machine

Lindsley's power-plant C&I text describes a station as a highly interdependent system: thermodynamics, chemistry, metallurgy and high-voltage electrical engineering held together by control technology. A small mistake can be severe, expensive or tragic — so the C&I is designed, not bolted on.

Unit / turbine control

Load, speed and extraction control of the turbo-generator producing MW onto the grid.

Boiler & HRSG control

Drum/HRSG steam production, feedwater and combustion — the same loops as the boiler section.

Distributed control system (DCS)

Thousands of I/O, advanced control and historian in one redundant architecture.

Turbine protection / ESD

Overspeed, vibration and trip systems — independent of the DCS, separate vote.

SCADA & plant network

Station-to-grid and auxiliary monitoring; KKS tagging identifies every tag.

Fuel & environmental

Coal/pulveriser, gas or waste-to-energy; emissions, efficiency and heat-rate optimisation.

Plant types

The same C&I serves the drum boiler, the heat-recovery steam generator (HRSG) of a combined-cycle gas turbine (CCGT), combined heat and power (CHP) and waste-to-energy — the general principles are identical; only the balance of plant differs.

04Oil refining & hydrocarbon processing

Many units, one safety culture

A refinery is a chain of unit operations — each with its own control problem and its own hazards. Natural-gas processing adds separation, dehydration (glycol), compression and orifice metering. What unifies them is the hazard: flammable, toxic or high-pressure fluid that must never escape.

Distillation

Atmospheric and vacuum columns; temperature and pressure profiles separate crude into cuts.

Fluid catalytic cracking (FCC)

Reactor–regenerator pair; catalyst circulation and strict temperature control keep yield and safety.

Hydrotreating / hydrogen

High-pressure reactors remove sulphur; hydrogen partial-pressure control is safety-critical.

Fired heaters

Mass-flow and excess-O2 control protect tubes and efficiency; flame safeguard trips on loss of flame.

Compressors & rotating equipment

Anti-surge control protects the machine; vibration and axial-position probes feed protection.

Pipelines & terminals

Custody-transfer metering (orifice, Coriolis), leak detection, tank gauging and overfill protection.

Independent protection

In hydrocarbon service the basic process control system (BPCS) is backed by a separate safety instrumented system (SIS) per IEC 61511, and storage tanks by independent high-high level protection per API 2350. Hazardous areas are classified to IEC 60079 with Ex protection concepts and IP codes.

05Food & beverage

Clean, safe, repeatable — in batches

Food and beverage shares the same sensors and loops as every other sector, but adds two demands: hygienic, cleanable hardware and recipe-driven batch control. The BCIT Wonderware InTouch demo screens are built around a food-industry line.

Heat treatment

Pasteurisation, UHT and aseptic hold; temperature is a micro-critical variable with full traceability.

Clean-in-place (CIP) / SIP

Automated cleaning/sterilising of the process without dismantling; conductivity or turbidity confirms rinses.

Batch & recipe control

ISA-88 / IEC 61512 recipe model: procedural control, units, phases and equipment states.

Hygienic instrumentation

3-A / EHEDG design, tri-clamp, polished welds, diaphragm seals; magmeters dominate clean flows.

Cold chain & refrigeration

Temperature and pressure control of ammonia/CO2 plants; loss of cooling is a product-safety event.

Fill, pack & line monitoring

Flow, weight and vision check; the operator screens in the BCIT Wonderware set are food-industry examples.

ISA-88 / IEC 61512

Batch plants model production as a recipe (procedural control) executed across units and phases, with equipment states such as idle, running, held and complete. This is the structure behind every automated brew, dairy and soft-drink line.

06The other seven

One dominant variable each

The four sectors above are where the textbooks spend their time. Most instrument engineers actually work in the rest, and in each of those one variable decides the design: it sets the technology, the calibration interval and the failure mode you will spend your weekends on.

SectorThe variable that dominatesWhat makes it hard
Water & wastewaterFlow, level, pH, residual chlorineThe sensor sits in the process, not beside it: fouling, biofouling and exhausted reagent drift the reading long before the instrument fails outright. Turndown from storm flow to night flow can reach 20:1.
Mining & metalsSlurry density, flow, particle sizeAbrasion eats liners and electrodes. A magnetic flowmeter needs a full pipe and a conductive fluid, and density usually comes from a nuclear gauge that carries its own licensing burden.
Pulp & paperConsistency, kappa number, basis weightConsistency is measured through the flowing stock, not sampled from it. Basis weight comes from a frame that traverses the sheet, so the measurement is a profile across the web, not a point.
Pharmaceutical & life sciencesConductivity, pressure, temperature, particle countThe instrument must be cleanable and provable: CIP/SIP cycles, surface finish, and a calibration record that survives an audit. Data integrity — ALCOA+, 21 CFR Part 11 — is part of the specification, not an afterthought.
Cement & mineralsKiln shell temperature, gas analysis, feed rateThe kiln is a rotating reactor tens of metres long with a residence time in tens of minutes, so most loops are feedforward: by the time feedback arrives the product has already left the hot zone.
Hydrogen & the energy transitionMass flow, purity, pressureHydrogen embrittles steel and escapes through the smallest gap, so mass flow (Coriolis) beats volumetric, and purity needs an analyser that is itself a process instrument. An electrolyser adds a fast pressure loop that couples the plant to the gas grid.
Biomass & waste-to-energyMoisture, calorific value, combustion O2Fuel properties change hourly, so the controller chases a moving target. Moisture is the largest disturbance and the hardest to measure online — which is why so much of the loop is still run on operator judgement.

It lives in trade press, in forums and in vendor application notes — Process Industry Forum is one of the larger English-language collections, with application write-ups, tutorials, a panel of named experts and a partner directory. 07The process decides

Four responses, four controllers

Before the question of which controller comes the question of which process. A level loop and a composition loop can carry identical PIDs and need opposite tuning, because one integrates and the other is deadtime-dominated. Control Global's R. Russell Rhinehart puts the order bluntly — fix the process before you tune the controller — and the trade press keeps returning to the same taxonomy.

ResponseWhat a step doesWhat the controller should doWhere it shows up here
Self-limiting, lag-dominantSettles at a new value; the lag is long compared with the dead time.PID. Gain above the baseline, derivative may help. This is the default case, and the one every tuning guide is written for.Steam temperature, most pressure and flow loops.
Self-limiting, deadtime-dominantAlso settles, but the dead time dominates the response.PI with derivative off and gain below the baseline, tuned critically damped. Overshoot is expensive: the next correction waits a full dead time.On-line composition analysers, moisture, sheet properties, kiln exit gas.
IntegratingRamps. It never settles on its own — the valve position sets the slope.P-only, or PI with a narrow band and minimal integral. A level loop is a buffer, not a setpoint tracker; the inventory is what absorbs the disturbance.Drum level, gas-header pressure, accumulators and surge drums.
Near-integrating / high orderRamps slowly, then bends towards a value it takes a long time to reach.PI, detuned. If you cannot tell which of the two it is, treat it as integrating and accept the slower response.Large mixing vessels, long pipelines, some furnace passes.
Why overshoot is expensive when the dead time dominates

On a deadtime-dominant loop the controller gets one correction, then must wait out the dead time to see what happened. Overshoot is therefore not a cosmetic defect — it is a second error that has to be fixed with the same handicap. Control Engineering's PID Spotlight series works the arithmetic on a 1:10 lag-to-deadtime process: good tuning is about K = 0.33 with Ti = 1 min/repeat and derivative at zero, while K = 0.60 produces the characteristic sharp reversal at the first peak. The series' rules are blunt — for a deadtime-dominant process the derivative should always be zero, and the gain belongs below the baseline, not above it.

The analyser is the slowest instrument in the plant

Composition loops are usually cascaded for the same reason: the primary composition controller sets the setpoint of a fast secondary loop — tray temperature, in a column — instead of stroking the valve itself. And in-line analysis keeps moving closer to the reactor. Yokogawa's ACOMP, presented at YNOW2026, pulls a sample from a polymer reactor, conditions it, and measures molecular weight, viscosity and conversion continuously: process-to-detector delay of 30 seconds to five minutes, then one point per second, against up to five hours for a laboratory result. Controlling monomer concentration along a deliberately chosen trajectory held 2 % error, and discharging on measurement rather than on a lab certificate added a batch per day on some reactors. The analyser did not get more accurate. It got faster — which, for a control loop, is the same thing.

Where these rules come from

08The common thread

Same chain, different consequences

Strip away the sector and every application above is the measurement-and-control loop from the Fundamentals page: a sensor turns a physical quantity into a signal, a transmitter scales it to 4–20 mA or a fieldbus, a controller (PID) compares it to setpoint, and a final element (valve or drive) acts on the process — with the safety layer standing apart.
